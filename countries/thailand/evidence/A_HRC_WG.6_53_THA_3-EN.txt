Summary of stakeholders’ submissions on Thailand*
Report of the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights
I. Background
1. The present report was prepared pursuant to Human Rights Council resolutions 5/1 and 16/21, taking into consideration the periodicity of the universal periodic review and the outcome of the previous review.1 It is a summary of 84 stakeholders’ submissions2 for the universal periodic review, presented in a summarized manner owing to word-limit constraints. A separate section is provided for the contribution by the national human rights institution that is accredited in full compliance with the Paris Principles.
II. Information provided by the national human rights institution accredited in full compliance with the Paris Principles
2. The National Human Rights Commission of Thailand (NHRCT) continued receiving complaints concerning torture, cruel and inhuman treatment, and enforced disappearance, which reflected persistent challenges in enforcing the 2022 Prevention and Suppression of Torture and Enforced Disappearance Act (the Anti-Torture Act), including limited understanding among officials, weak compliance monitoring, and insufficient victim protection. Certain provisions might remain inconsistent with the Convention against Torture.3 3. The NHRCT recommended that Thailand adopt a time-bound roadmap for accession to the OP-CAT, while making necessary preparations for relevant agencies and for establishing the national preventive mechanism (NPM). It further recommended promoting understanding of the NPM’s role in preventing torture and improving the detention standards to reduce the risk of torture.4 4. The NHRCT stated that no concrete progress had been made toward the ratification of ILO Conventions Nos. 87 and 98 on freedom of association and collective bargaining.5 5. It recommended that Thailand urgently enact comprehensive anti-discrimination legislation covering all grounds of discrimination, with robust protection and effective complaint and remedy mechanisms.6

6. It recommended that Thailand: adopt a time-bound action plan with clear targets and measurable indicators to advance the gradual abolition of the death penalty; and continuously promote knowledge and understanding of the death penalty within society, so as to lead to a moratorium on the death penalty in practice and to prepare for accession to the OP2-ICCPR.7
7. It stated that it continued to receive complaints concerning the use of law to restrict freedom of expression and opinion, strategic lawsuits against public participation (SLAPP), and harassment of human rights defenders. It recommended that Thailand: expedite the enactment of Anti-Strategic Lawsuits against Public Participation Bill, and strengthen mechanisms to protect human rights defenders from intimidation, including ensuring systematic and effective remedies; and promote awareness and develop practical guidelines for justice sector agencies on the interpretation and application of anti-SLAPP measures under the Criminal Procedure Code.8
8. It also recommended that Thailand: ensure that security measures and public communication respected freedom of expression without harassment or stigmatization; and develop effective measures to prevent and address hate speech while protecting the rights, freedoms, and safety of all affected individuals and communities on an equal and nondiscriminatory basis.9
9. It stated that the 2008 Anti-Trafficking in Persons Act, and its amendments, might not fully address evolving forms of the crime. The processes of identification, screening, and protection of victims were not applied consistently. Cases had also been found, in which persons were returned to their countries of origin without going through the National Referral Mechanism (NRM).10
10. It stated that migrant workers, informal workers and workers in new forms of work such as platform workers remained inadequately protected by labour law and faced unequal access to social security. It also identified labour rights violations in outsourced public-sector employment through public procurement, which was often used instead of direct employment.11
11. It recommended that Thailand: strengthen the disaster management system by ensuring a unified command structure, developing early warning systems and risk communication that were accessible to all groups, establishing measures to protect vulnerable groups, and reviewing the remedy system to comprehensively cover all damages; and strengthen international cooperation to address transboundary issues and promote the development of a regional action plan with ASEAN member States on water management, water development, transboundary pollution, and large-scale project management that might affect Thailand.12
12. It recommended that Thailand: act on its pledge to advance legislation on mandatory human rights due diligence (HRDD), taking into account the principle of proportionality based on the size and risk profile of businesses, and aligning it with existing legal and institutional frameworks to avoid duplication; and require HRDD across supply chains covering state-related investment projects and public procurement, indirect employment, and non-standard forms of employment.13
13. It stated that older persons were particularly vulnerable to discrimination and structural barriers across multiple dimensions, including employment, income security, access to health and social services, and pervasive age-based stereotypes. It recommended that Thailand review the definition of “older persons” and relevant policy frameworks considering changing life expectancy and health conditions, in order to avoid stereotyping and policymaking based solely on age, and to promote living with dignity, independence, and the participation of older persons in society.14

III. Information provided by other stakeholders
A. Scope of international obligations15 and cooperation with human rights mechanisms
14. HRF and JS29 recommended that Thailand ratify the OP2-ICCPR and the ICRMW.16 15. APT recommended that Thailand ratify the OP-CAT.17 16. JS37 and JS45 recommended that Thailand: consider ratifying the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court18 and the Convention on the Non-Applicability of Statutory Limitations to War Crimes and Crimes Against Humanity (1968). 19 CGNK also recommended that Thailand ratify the Convention on the Prevention and the Punishment of the Crime of Genocide.20 17. AI recommended that Thailand accede to the 1951 Convention relating to the Status of Refugees and its 1967 Protocol.21 18. SC recommended that Thailand ratify ILO Convention Nos. 87 and 98.22
B. National human rights framework
1. Constitutional and legislative framework 19. JS38 recommended that Thailand recognize the right to a clean, healthy, and sustainable environment within the Constitution and ensure its enforceability.23 20. FLD stated that Thailand lacked any specific laws, effective policies or practices addressing human rights defenders. The absence of a dedicated law on the protection of human rights defenders exposed them to heightened risks and reprisals.24 21. EMP TH recommended that Thailand repeal the Prevention and Suppression of Prostitution Act B.E. 2539 (1996).25 SWING also recommended that Thailand extend labour protections under the Labour Protection Act, 1998, to include sex workers and night workers in entertainment venues.26
2. Institutional infrastructure and policy measures 22. JS38 recommended that Thailand guarantee the structural independence of the NHRCT by reforming its appointment, composition, and oversight processes to ensure pluralism, transparency, and freedom from political, military, and security sector influence in accordance with the Paris Principles.27 23. JS52 recommended that Thailand advance legal protection and development to persons in vulnerable and marginalized situations to increase the role of communities in preventing and monitoring corruption, including promoting good governance.28 JS10 made a similar recommendation.29
C. Promotion and protection of human rights
1. Implementation of international human rights obligations, taking into account applicable international humanitarian law
Equality and non-discrimination 24. JS29 and JS44 recommended that Thailand enact the Anti-Discrimination Act that explicitly prohibited discrimination on the basis of gender identity, sexual orientation, age, ethnicity, and disability.30 JS40 also recommended that Thailand take proactive measures to reduce stigma and discrimination against people who used drugs across all sectors.31

Right to life, liberty and security of person, and freedom from torture
25. UPR-BCU recommended that Thailand ensure that it complied with the ‘most serious crimes’ principle, restricting the death penalty to crimes of intentional killing only.32
26. Fortify Rights noted allegations of torture, including a case in January 2024 involving the detention and subsequent death of a Myanmar national following alleged abuse by Thai soldiers. It also expressed concern regarding the lack of accountability for such acts and the enforcement of anti-torture legislation.33 JS35 recommended that Thailand ensure prompt, impartial, independent, and effective investigations into all allegations of torture, illtreatment and deaths in custody, including those involving military conscripts.34
27. Fortify Rights also noted that no military personnel had been charged in connection with the alleged torture and death of a Myanmar national. A civilian witness to the incident was initially convicted and later acquitted on appeal.35 HRW also stated that since 2001, more than 20 human rights defenders and civil society activists had been killed. Even in serious criminal cases, those responsible had not been punished.36
28. JS15 recommended that Thailand: address and resolve the issue of overcrowding in prisons by finding sustainable and effective measures to reduce the prison population; and increase the use of alternatives to prison sentences and detention, by developing noncustodial measures within the legal system, in line with the UN Standard Minimum Rules for Non-custodial Measures (the Tokyo Rules).37
International humanitarian law
29. HRW stated that border clashes between Thailand and Cambodia in 2025 killed and injured civilians and damaged civilian objects, medical facilities, and cultural sites. It alleged that Thailand used internationally prohibited cluster munitions.38 Asia Centre called upon Thailand to conduct an independent investigation into the alleged destruction of religious and cultural heritage sites during the conflict, and, where responsibility was established, to provide reparations, including full financial compensation and the reconstruction of damaged sites. It also urged Thailand to formally designate areas containing ancient religious and cultural heritage sites as falling under special protection pursuant to the Hague Convention for the Protection of Cultural Property in the Event of Armed Conflict and its Protocols.39
Administration of justice, including impunity, and the rule of law
30. APT stated that gaps persisted in the effective implementation of fundamental safeguards during the first hours of deprivation of liberty in Thailand. Prompt access to legal counsel remained the exception rather than the norm. While Thailand had a legal aid fund under the Ministry of Justice, limitations remained where access was not prompt, and coverage was uneven.40
31. Fortify Rights expressed concern regarding impunity and the lack of effective accountability mechanisms for violations committed by security forces.41 It recommended that Thailand repeal or reform any provisions that afforded impunity to members of the security forces for acts of torture, extrajudicial killing, and other serious human rights violations. 42 JS45 also recommended that Thailand: ensure prompt, independent, and impartial investigations into serious human rights violations, including the Tak Bai incident, and bring those responsible, including senior officials, to justice; and provide comprehensive reparations to victims and their families, including not only financial compensation but also symbolic measures such as public acknowledgment, memorialization, and guarantees of nonrecurrence.43
Fundamental freedoms and the right to participate in public and political life
32. ADF International recommended that Thailand guarantee full respect for the right of everyone to freedom of religion or belief and expression without discrimination, including non-Buddhist minorities and those fleeing religious persecution.44 ECLJ recommended that Thailand: respect religious freedom for all its citizens, not just those belonging to the majority religion; amend its laws by removing its blasphemy laws that could be used to target adherents of minority religions; and guarantee societal respect for other religions and allow
4

A/HRC/WG.6/53/THA/3
Christians and other religious minorities to practice their faith in public. 45 JS31 also recommended that Thailand: effectively protect places of worship and worshippers; implement security measures for all religious sites on an equal basis, without discrimination, including preventing violence during religious activities and ensuring the safety of clergy and individuals attending religious practices.46
33. JS6 further recommended that Thailand promptly adopt legislation recognizing the right to conscientious objection to military service in line with international human rights law and standards, including the exemption of conscientious objectors from the compulsory military service and reserve duties.47
34. HRW stated that Thailand supported multiple recommendations regarding freedom of expression at the previous cycle review. However, expression of critical and dissenting opinions remained restricted. Since the military coup in 2014, at least 1,997 people had been prosecuted in Thailand for exercising their rights to free expression and peaceful assembly.48 HRF expressed similar concerns.49
35. FLD expressed concern about the use of laws such as lèse-majesté charges in the Criminal Code Section 112, in connection to their political expression and protest, and that the issuance of cumulative sentences by the courts had significantly closed civic space.50 HRW expressed similar concerns.51 PEN-I also stated that Section 112 failed to meet the requirements of legality, necessity, and proportionality under international human rights law. The imposition of severe criminal penalties for peaceful expression undermined democratic accountability and restricted public debate on matters of legitimate public interest.52
36. FLS, JS5, and JS16 recommended that Thailand stop using criminal laws, including Section 112, sedition provisions, emergency regulations, and assembly-related laws, to punish individuals exercising their rights to freedom of expression and peaceful assembly.53 AI recommended that Thailand decriminalize defamation and end imprisonment or fines as penalties for acts of defamation.54
37. HRW also noted that Thai authorities had not protected activists and whistleblowers from vexatious or retaliatory lawsuits by state agencies and private companies. Thailand had yet to repeal criminal defamation laws and enact effective measures against the use of strategic litigation to silence public criticism.55 JS45 recommended that Thailand establish a distinct standalone Anti-SLAPP legislation, and amend the Criminal Procedure Code, including Sections 161/1 and 165/2, to establish stricter safeguards against the initiation of cases lacking merit, particularly those intended to harass or silence human rights defenders.56 JS43 made similar recommendations.57 FLS further recommended that Thailand establish rapid, gender-sensitive protection measures for women human rights defenders facing threats, online abuse, intimidation, and reprisals, including effective police protection, risk assessment, and family protection where needed. Thai authorities should conduct prompt, impartial, and effective investigations into online death threats, doxing, incitement to violence, smear campaigns, and other attacks against women human rights defenders, and hold all perpetrators accountable.58
38. AFI further stated that since the last review, artists and cultural workers had continued to face arrest, prosecution, censorship, and intimidation for peaceful forms of expression, including visual art, music, satire, and curatorial work, indicating that significant gaps persisted between Thailand’s commitments and the realities experienced in practice.59
39. WW recommended that Thailand: amend the Organic Act on the Election of Members of the House of Representatives to allow voters to use various types of marking patterns to ensure that every vote was counted; and amend the Referendum Act to allow for advance voting, consistent with the procedures for general elections.60
Right to privacy
40. AI stated that the devices of at least 35 individuals, including human rights defenders, were infected with NSO Group’s Pegasus spyware between 2020 and 2021, and that technical and circumstantial evidence led to the conclusion that Thai authorities were most likely behind the attack.61 JS26 recommended that Thailand: immediately cease the use of Pegasus spyware and all other commercial surveillance tools against activists, journalists, human

rights defenders, political opponents, and civil society organizations; conduct an independent and transparent investigation into all confirmed and alleged uses of such tools; provide effective remedies to those whose devices were infected; and publish the results of any investigation.62
Right to marriage and family life
41. ADF International noted that Thailand had become a hub for the international commercial surrogacy market, which was accompanied by numerous public scandals involving human trafficking, exploitation of surrogate mothers, as well as child abandonment.63
Prohibition of all forms of slavery, including trafficking in persons
42. WEA stated that while Thailand had made notable legislative and institutional progress, significant gaps remained in implementation, victim identification, and survivor protection. Strengthened coordination, survivor-centred approaches, gender-sensitive responses and adaptation to evolving trafficking patterns were critical to improving outcomes.64 ECLJ encouraged Thailand to continue the work it had already undertaken to combat human trafficking, which had shown an increase in the number of human trafficking prosecutions annually. It recommended that Thailand continue to commit resources aimed at eradicating human trafficking.65
43. JS32 also recommended that Thailand develop the capacity of officials to screen and protect victims of forced labour and human trafficking in the fisheries sector.66 JS1 further recommended that Thailand: urgently develop standards for screening and protecting victims covering all forms of structural forced labour, while enhancing the capacity of officials to interpret and enforce the law; and make efforts to guarantee access to justice without affecting the legal status of workers and effectively prohibit the retention of documents, in order to concretely prevent forced labour throughout the supply chain.67
Right to work and to just and favourable conditions of work
44. JS33 recommended that Thailand repeal the legal prohibition on farmers forming trade unions. 68 SC also recommended that Thailand: undertake reforms to increase the investigative capacity of the courts and/or dispute settlement body on behalf of workers or unions; take steps to prevent labour rights and trade union rights cases being held up in appeal without implementation; and impose more stringent sanctions on recalcitrant, non-compliant employers who refused to implement rulings of labour courts or related bodies in decisions favouring workers.69
45. SC also recommended that Thailand: under a reformed Labour Relations Act, ensure that all informal sector workers, including platform workers were covered under the legislation and enjoyed full and unfettered freedom of association with the right to organize and collectively bargain; and ensure platform workers faced no discrimination in employment classification, access to social security and workplace accident compensation.70 JS2 also made recommendations in that regard.71
Right to social security
46. JS49 recommended that Thailand: reform the Social Security Act and related regulations to ensure equal access for migrant workers to unemployment benefits, healthcare, pensions, and child allowances; and ensure inclusion of domestic workers, including migrant domestic workers, in the mandatory social security system without delay.72
Right to an adequate standard of living
47. JS55 recommended that Thailand: guarantee the right to adequate housing, including security of tenure, protection against forced eviction, and access to appropriate compensation or resettlement in accordance with international human rights standards; ensure that all evictions were carried out only as a last resort in full compliance with international human rights standards, and suspend evictions in cases where legal disputes remained unresolved or due process guarantees had not been met.73

Right to health
48. JS50 stated that the Thai mental health service system remained hampered by structural limitations. Disparities in accessibility remained high in rural provinces, while the quality and coverage of care were often undermined by a shortage of specialized psychiatric personnel and a centralized budget that favoured urban tertiary hospitals over communitybased psychosocial support.74 JS54 recommended that Thailand take all necessary legislative, administrative, personnel training, and other measures to ensure the enjoyment of the right to the highest attainable standard of health, without discrimination on the basis of sexual orientation or gender identity.75
49. JS51 recommended that Thailand ensure universal access to sexual and reproductive health and rights and take proactive measures to expand abortion services and reduce unnecessary restrictions imposed by individual healthcare facilities to ensure universal access.76 JS21 made similar recommendations.77
Right to education
50. JS41 recommended that Thailand: recognize the right to education and ensure that education was available and accessible for all children, particularly children in migration context; fully implement the Education for All policy by removing barriers for refugee and undocumented children, including recognition of Learning Centre certificates; grant all children the right to enrol in Thai public schools regardless of documentation status; and support mother-tongue-based multilingual education to improve inclusion and learning outcomes for refugee children.78
Cultural rights
51. JS34 recommended that Thailand: strengthen multilingual education and cultural preservation: develop comprehensive and inclusive curricula that integrated Indigenous languages, cultures, histories, and knowledge systems to ensure relevance and quality in Indigenous language education in Thailand.79 JS19 also recommended that Thailand fully implement key guarantees of the Act on Protection and Promotion of the Way of Life of Ethnic Groups, with particular focus on preserving tribal and ethnic languages and ensuring access to community-led education within the formal national curriculum.80
Environment and business and human rights
52. JS47 recommended that Thailand ensure that all national policies related to climate change, forestry, land, natural resources were fully aligned with international standards, were grounded in the principles of non-discrimination and participation and adopted a gender-lens with specific safeguards for Indigenous, ethnic and rural women.81
53. JS39 recommended that Thailand enact legislation on pollutant release and transfer registers to ensure transparent, publicly accessible reporting of emissions and accountability mechanisms. 82 JS23 also recommended that the Government ensure transparency and corporate accountability by requiring disclosure of carbon forest project locations, participating business entities, and greenhouse gas emissions data, and adopt regulations to remove exemptions based on “trade secrets”, ensuring such information was publicly accessible.83
54. FLD highlighted that Thailand adopted its second National Action Plan on Business and Human Rights (2023–2027) in September 2023, identifying human rights defenders as a priority area. However, the Plan lacked legally binding force, robust monitoring mechanisms, and meaningful qualitative indicators, which had allowed entrenched patterns of corporate impunity to persist. 84 AI noted that Thailand still lacked mandatory human rights due diligence legislation, leaving businesses under no legal obligation to identify, prevent or mitigate their human rights impact.85 FLD, SC and JS14 further recommended that Thailand enact a robust domestic human rights and environmental due diligence law that would extend jurisdiction over transnational corporations profiting from their supply chain in Thailand to sanctions for supply chain abuses.86 JS28 and JS46 made similar recommendations.87

55. FLD stated that strategic lawsuits against public participation (SLAPP) cases by companies and individuals to intimidate human rights defenders who exposed labour rights abuses, environmental damage, or corruption were widespread.88 FLD and SC recommended that Thailand repeal the existing criminal defamation laws under Sections 326-333 of the Criminal Code and review anti-SLAPP clauses for their effective implementation.89 JS11, JS38 made similar recommendations.90
2. Rights of specific persons or groups
Women
56. JS27 recommended that Thailand: amend the Domestic Violence Victim Protection Act and related laws to ensure survivor-centred protection, prompt enforcement, legal aid, interpretation, shelters, and psychosocial support; expand legal and policy protections against gender-based violence to address technology-facilitated violence and violence against marginalized groups, including sex workers, transgender women, and women human rights defenders; and establish a coordinated national femicide and gender-based violence data system with disaggregated data.91 JS18 also made recommendations in that regard.92
57. APT recommended that Thailand: improve detention conditions for women and ensure access to adequate gender-sensitive healthcare services, including for pregnant and breastfeeding women; and reduce the incarceration of women, particularly for non-violent offences, especially drug-related, through the increased use of non-custodial measures.93
Children
58. HKC stated that the persistence of mass-scale online child sexual exploitation and abuse, the fragmented prevention ecosystem, severe underreporting, and the delayed criminalization of online offenses until 2025 indicated that insufficient progress had been made against the scale of the problem of child sexual exploitation.94
59. JS44 recommended that Thailand adopt legislation to protect children, including LGBTIQ+ children, from sexual exploitation, harassment, bullying, and cyberbullying with established clear definitions, mandatory reporting obligations, and proportionate penalties.95 JS7 further recommended that Thailand: fast‑track the adoption of the draft Criminal Code amendments on online grooming, sextortion, cyberstalking, and cyberbullying, and ensure child‑friendly, trauma‑informed enforcement and justice procedures; and strengthen private‑sector accountability, requiring technology and gaming companies to conduct child‑impact assessments, enforce child‑safety standards, and ensure rapid removal of harmful content.96
Older persons
60. While noting that the Older Persons Act B.E.2546, 2003 established rights and welfare provisions for older persons in Thailand, JS8 recommended that Thailand: improve access to healthcare, transportation, and social support for elderly refugees; and develop targeted assistance programmes for older persons with chronic illnesses and disabilities.97
Persons with disabilities
61. AcAs recommended that Thailand: amend the Mental Health Act to establish clear legal safeguards, including defined time limits, independent oversight mechanisms, and access to judicial review for involuntary treatment decisions; and remove discriminatory provisions that restricted civic participation and implement protections to ensure that persons with psychosocial disabilities could fully exercise their political rights, including voting, candidacy, and public engagement.98
62. AcAs stated that with over 4.19 million Thais (approximately 6 per cent of the population) living with some form of disability, many continued to be barred from essential medical care, due to barriers such as financial constraints, lack of assistive technology, and physical inaccessibility.99 JS9 recommended that Thailand provide high-quality assistive devices tailored to individual cases and increase access to services for people with disabilities.100

63. AcAs recommended that Thailand: strengthen legal protections and enforcement mechanisms to prevent and respond to gender-based violence against women with disabilities, including improving accessibility of reporting systems, expanding survivor support services, enhancing law enforcement training, and addressing barriers within judicial processes that prevented victims from seeking justice; and strengthen enforcement of existing prohibitions against forced sterilization and establish clear legal accountability.101
Indigenous Peoples and minorities
64. AI highlighted that the 2025 Protection and Promotion of the Way of Life of Ethnic Groups Act marked an effort to improve the rights of ethnic minorities but failed to provide formal recognition of the status of “Indigenous peoples.”102 AI, JS12, and JS13 recommended that Thailand amend relevant laws to recognize Indigenous Peoples’s status and rights, including the right to free, prior and informed consent, in line with international human rights law.103 JS48 made similar recommendations.104 JS42 also recommended that Thailand legally recognize and protect customary land tenure and traditional livelihood practices, including rotational farming and community-based natural resource management. 105 JS48 also recommended that Thailand: ensure universal birth registration and access to nationality for Indigenous women and girls; and ensure access to basic services, including healthcare, clean water, sanitation, electricity, and culturally appropriate services in Indigenous languages, particularly in remote and coastal communities.106
Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender and intersex persons
65. RSAT stated that while the Marriage Equality Act marked a historic step forward, critical gaps in Thai legislation continued to deny LGBTIQ+ individuals the protection. Many individuals faced discrimination, violence, and prejudice across multiple spheres of life from bullying and gender-based violence in schools, to discrimination in the workplace, to demeaning treatment during military conscription processes.107 GirlxGirl expressed similar concerns.108 GirlxGirl and JS20 recommended that Thailand enact and enforce legislation prohibiting hate speech based on sex characteristics, sexual orientation, and gender identity.109 RSAT made similar recommendations.110 JS3 further recommended that Thailand enact specific legislation to ban conversion therapy in all forms and criminalize forced attempts to change an individual’s sexual orientation or gender identity.111
66. JS54 recommended that Thailand develop binding national guidelines through meaningful consultation with affected communities to ensure that platforms’ content moderation policies did not disproportionately target LBTIQ+ users, youth, sex workers, or human rights defenders, and to prohibit all forms of discrimination and guarantee equal and effective protection for all persons, with enforcement, accountability, and the right to privacy and safety in digital spaces.112
Migrants, refugees and asylum-seekers
67. HRW noted that migrant workers were barred by the Labor Relations Act from organizing and establishing labour unions or serving as union leaders.113
68. Fortify Rights stated that refugees were treated as irregular migrants under the Immigration Act of 1979 and were therefore subject to arrest, detention and deportation.114 JS41 recommended that Thailand enact comprehensive national asylum legislation that formally recognized refugee status and guaranteed protected persons access to work authorization, healthcare, education, and social protection.115 JS4 also recommended that Thailand end the practice of prolonged and arbitrary immigration detention of refugees and asylum seekers, and ensure judicial review of all detention decisions were in line with international human rights standards.116 JS53 further recommended that Thailand ensure that conditions in immigration detention centres complied with international standards, by addressing overcrowding, improving sanitation, ventilation, access to bedding, and guaranteeing adequate food, clean water, and hygiene products free of charge.117 JS17 and JS41 made similar recommendations.118
69. HRW stated that Thai authorities in recent years had violated prohibitions against refoulement by collaborating with foreign governments to arrest and return refugees and

asylum seekers. The Government sent 40 Uyghur men to a neighbouring country in February 2025, where there exists risks of torture, arbitrary detention, and long-term imprisonment.119 HRF recommended that Thailand end transnational repression and cease harassment of exiled activists and journalists in Thailand.120 Fortify Rights expressed similar concerns.121 JS25 and JS53 recommended that Thailand: establish a clear legal framework recognizing refugees and asylum seekers in line with international standards in order to prevent their criminalization and detention under immigration laws and ensure full respect for the principle of non-refoulement.122
3. Specific regions or territories
70. JS24 recommended that Thailand: end racial profiling and discriminatory security practices targeting Malay Muslim communities in the Southern Border Provinces, including the mass collection of DNA, discriminatory surveillance, and identity checks; and review and reform all security laws and practices applied under the state of emergency and martial law to ensure full compliance with international human rights standards and eliminate their discriminatory impact on ethnic and ethno-religious groups. 123 JS22 made similar recommendations.124
