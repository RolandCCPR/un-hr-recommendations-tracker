I. Human rights and the Sustainable Development Goals
1. The Thai Government recognizes that the promotion and protection of human rights are fundamental to sustainable development and the achievement of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Guided by the principle of Leaving No One Behind, Thailand has adopted this principle as a key policy framework to accelerate the achievement of the SDGs by 2030 and in preparation for the post-2030 development agenda.
2. The Thai Government has therefore integrated human rights into the implementation of the SDGs, encompassing economic, social and cultural rights, as well as civil and political rights. This is reflected in policies to eradicate poverty (SDG 1), promote quality education (SDG 4), ensure the right to health (SDG 3), advance gender equality (SDG 5), reduce inequalities (SDG 10), and strengthen effective, transparent and accountable institutions that uphold the rule of law (SDG 16).
3. According to the Sustainable Development Report 2026, Thailand remains among the region’s top performers in SDG implementation, having achieved SDG 1 (No Poverty) and SDG 4 (Quality Education). Nevertheless, challenges remain in strengthening the rule of law, access to justice, transparency and effective institutions (SDG 16), as well as addressing climate change, conserving natural resources and biodiversity (SDGs 13 and 15), and promoting sustainable cities and communities (SDG 11). The Thai Government remains committed to advancing human rights and sustainable development in a mutually reinforcing manner.
II. Thailand’s priorities on human rights
Recommendations 51.20, 51.56–51.64, 51.104, 51.106 and 51.107
4. The Thai Government remains committed to promoting, protecting and increasing access to human rights for all. Particular attention is given to persons in vulnerable situations, the right to health, business and human rights, and emerging challenges, including pandemics, climate change, the right to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment, and the impact of digital technologies on human rights.
5. The Thai Government is convinced that the foundation for progress in human rights starts at the national level through a shared understanding of rights and responsibilities among the public and relevant authorities. This is undertaken by promoting human rights education and awareness with regard to domestic law and international human rights obligations.
6. At the international level, the Thai Government remains committed to strengthening cooperation in the Human Rights Council (HRC), particularly through technical cooperation and capacity-building to support States in promoting and protecting human rights. Since its first term as an HRC member, Thailand has led the drafting and negotiation of the HRC resolution on Enhancement of technical cooperation and capacity-building in the field of human rights. Thailand continues to do so in its current term (2025–2027).
7. The Thai Government also remains committed to promoting and protecting human rights as well as to enhancing its capability in responding to various forms of global challenges, including conflict situations, energy crises, economic volatility, and climate change.

12. The Thai Government supports the Secretary-General’s UN80 Initiative to enhance the efficiency and agility of the United Nations, viewing that such reform should strengthen the organization's capacity to fulfil its mandate, particularly in the promotion and protection of human rights.

The Thai Government welcomed the official visits of two special procedures mandate holders of the HRC: (1) the Working Group on discrimination against women and girls (2– 13 December 2024); and (2) the Special Rapporteur on the right of everyone to the enjoyment of the highest attainable standard of physical and mental health (18–28 February 2025). On the occasion of the 60th anniversary of the adoption of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR) in 2026, the Thai Government reaffirms its commitment to promoting and protecting human rights in accordance with its obligations under both Covenants.

As a member of the ASEAN Intergovernmental Commission on Human Rights (AICHR), the Thai Government remains committed to advancing: (1) the ASEAN Enabling Masterplan 2025: Mainstreaming the Rights of Persons with Disabilities; (2) the right to a safe, clean, healthy and sustainable environment; and (3) Human Rights Cities, by promoting urban environments that respect and uphold human rights.

In the context of the Thailand-Cambodia situation, the Thai Government authorized the ICRC to visit and interview 18 Cambodian soldiers under Thai custody. 21. The Thai Government is undergoing a technical assessment process for accession to the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). It is promoting the enhancement of relevant standards through various measures, including on responsible business conduct, the development of an e-learning course based on the OECD framework “The Essentials of OECD Due Diligence for Responsible Business Conduct”, and the commissioning of a feasibility study on the establishment of an appropriate model for Thailand’s National Contact Point (NCP).

VII. Protection and promotion of human rights
27. The Thai Government recognizes that the promotion and protection of human rights require a holistic approach, taking into account the interlinkages among all categories of human rights, as well as the importance of sustained engagement with various mechanisms under the HRC. This includes implementing recommendations under the UPR process and taking into consideration diverse views from various sectors. Concurrently, the Thai Government has incorporated the transition towards an ageing society and the future prospect of becoming a super-aged society into the formulation of relevant policies.
A. Economic, social and cultural rights
Recommendations 51.52–51.53, 51.55, 51.99–51.102 and 51.104–51.110
28. The Thai Government has adopted the National Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) to assess and monitor poverty situations and to inform the formulation of targeted policies and assistance measures for vulnerable population groups and areas. The MPI considers multiple dimensions of deprivation affecting people’s quality of life beyond income, including health, education, living conditions, and access to basic services and welfare. 29. The 13th National Economic and Social Development Plan (2023-2027) sets Milestone 9: “Thailand has less intergenerational poverty and adequate, appropriate social protection.” It aims to enhance and develop flexible and diverse social protection schemes to ensure that all groups receive appropriate welfare and that such schemes respond to the varying needs of people at different stages of life and social circumstances, while ensuring fiscal sustainability.
Right to health
Recommendations 51.102–51.103, 51.113–51.118, 51.121, 51.123, 51.184, 51.187 and voluntary pledge 54 (d)
30. The Thai Government has enhanced the Universal Health Coverage (UHC) system by developing the “30 Baht Treats All Diseases” scheme into the “30 Baht Treats Everywhere” scheme to improve the efficiency, coverage and quality of health services. The initiative places emphasis on expanding access to health services closer to communities through the strengthening of primary healthcare systems.
31. The Thai Government has integrated medical innovations to enhance access to health services, including telemedicine, telepharmacy, and digital health data management to improve the efficiency of healthcare service delivery. It has also developed medicine delivery systems through Village Health Volunteers (VHVs) and “Health Rider” services to facilitate access for service users.
32. The Thai Government has expanded health coverage to include undocumented migrant workers and persons with status-related challenges. In collaboration with the World Health Organization (WHO), Thailand has developed the Health Insurance for Non-Thai People System (HINT), also known as the “Tor 99” healthcare entitlement, to promote equitable access to health services and provide health protection for persons with nationality and status-related challenges without discrimination.

Right to education
Recommendations 51.17, 51.99, 51.102–51.104, 51.108, 51.111, 51.125–51.127, 51.154, 51.158, 51.166–51.167, 51.170–51.174, 51.178, 51.190 and voluntary pledge 54 (e)
35. The Thai Government has implemented the “Anywhere Anytime” policy to promote a flexible learning ecosystem through the full implementation of the National Credit Bank system. The system enables people of all ages to accumulate credits earned through online learning, vocational training, or work experience, which can subsequently be transferred or recognized for further education or the award of formal educational qualifications, thereby promoting lifelong learning.
36. The Thai Government has implemented the “Bring Children Back to School, Bring Learning to Children” (OBEC Zero Dropout) initiative in 2025 by the Office of the Basic Education Commission (OBEC), in response to the Ministry of Education’s Thailand Zero Dropout policy. The initiative aims to identify, support and monitor children and youth aged 3-18 who are out of the education system, including more than 18,000 children who were identified as missing from the system in its first year, to enable them to return to quality education. It also promotes the transition towards flexible education to ensure that all children and youth have continued access to educational opportunities and self-development pathways that align with their potential and life circumstances.
37. The removal of fixed completion time requirements aims to support lifelong learning by allowing learners to complete their studies beyond the previous 4-year or 8-year time limits, and accumulate skills while working.
38. In 2025, the Thai Government supported education provision for students and schools located in special areas, such as remote highland areas and islands, where access to education is constrained by challenges, including limited transportation, household poverty, and shortages of teachers and educational resources. These efforts aim to promote equal and equitable educational opportunities. A total of 1,604 schools in special areas received support to improve the effectiveness of education management in accordance with their local contexts. In addition, subsidies were allocated during school holidays and before and after academic terms for 503 schools providing boarding facilities.
39. The 4th Education Plan for Persons with Disabilities B.E. 2023-2027 sets direction for development under the vision “All persons with disabilities receive education, develop their full potential, and live with dignity and security in a changing world.” The Plan emphasizes the participation of all sectors in promoting equitable and inclusive access to quality education for persons with disabilities.
40. The Thai Government has implemented an initiative on strengthening schools and community collaboration to promote a multi-cultural society for peace in the Southern Border Provinces (SBPs). The initiative integrates cooperation among educational institutions, more than 65 communities, and partner organizations to foster acceptance of diversity in ethnicity, religion, identity and culture. It also aims to develop the vocational capacities and skills of learners, youth and local communities in the SBPs, preparing them for employment and enabling them to generate income for themselves and their families. The initiative covers education provision in Pondok Islamic learning institutions and vocational training based on the Phra Dabos School model in the five SBPs. It has benefitted 9,573 persons through vocational skills development and enhancement (as of fiscal year 2025).
41. The Thai Government has supported students without civil registration records or Thai nationality in obtaining educational documentation through the Ministry of Education

Right to work
Recommendation 51.128 and 51.134
42. On 12 June 2024, the Thai Government ratified the International Labour Organization (ILO)’s Tripartite Consultation (International Labour Standards) Convention, 1976 (No. 144), which is one of the four ILO governance conventions. The Convention promotes the tripartite consultation of national mechanisms and processes on issues related to international labour standards.
43. On 10 June 2025, the Thai Government ratified ILO’s Occupational Safety and Health Convention 1981 (No. 155), and the Protocol of 2002 to the Occupational Safety and Health Convention, 1981. The Convention promotes workers’ fundamental right to a safe and healthy working environment, as well as effective records and notifications of occupational accidents and diseases in a more effective manner. The Convention and its Protocol came into force on 10 June 2026.
44. The Labour Protection Act (No. 8) B.E. 2566 (2023) allows employees to work from home or residence, or to work through the use of information technology at any place. It also grants employees the right to disconnect through any means with employers, chiefs, controllers or inspectors, unless the employee’ prior written consent is obtained.
45. The Labour Protection Act (No. 9) B.E. 2568 (2025) stipulates key protection measures including: (1) service contractor employees working in government agencies are afforded with holidays and rest period not less than those provided for according to the Labour Protection Act; (2) pregnant employees are entitled to maternity leave up to 120 days per pregnancy (previously was 98 days). The Ministry of Labour’s Ministerial Regulation No. 15 B.E. 2567 (2024) also provides additional protection for all domestic workers. This includes maternity leave, specified working hours per day (8 hours per day) and ensuring their minimum wage.
46. From 2021 to 2025, the Thai Government cooperated with the Committee of Experts on the Application of Conventions and Recommendations (CEACR) under the ILO during the process of reporting on the implementation of 10 ILO Conventions and in considering related recommendations.
Trafficking in persons
Recommendations 51.77–51.80, 51.82–51.85, 51.106 and 51.107
47. Since 2022, the Thai Government has implemented the National Referral Mechanism (NRM) for the screening, identification, and protection of trafficking victims, including providing a Reflection Period (RP) for victims' recovery. The Thai Government has also advocated for other countries to put in place the NRM and the Transnational Referral Mechanism (TRM). In May 2026, the Thai government made a voluntary pledge at the 2nd International Migration Review Forum (IMRF) in New York “to strengthen trafficking in persons responses in the context of international migration, by promoting Transnational Referral Mechanism (TRM), using a victim-centered, whole-of-government and whole-ofsociety approach".
48. The Thai Government has made continuous efforts to prevent and combat new forms of trafficking in persons, particularly trafficking in persons in the context of online scams. Since February 2025, Thailand has assisted in repatriating over 13,500 individuals of 47 nationalities rescued from scam centres in its neighbouring countries, including 5,331 confirmed victims of trafficking in persons for forced criminality (as of 14 July 2026).

49. "The Consular Guidelines to Support Vulnerable Migrants, Especially Potential Victims of Human Trafficking in Thailand" was developed and disseminated by the Ministry of Social Development and Human Security in collaboration with the International Organization for Migration (IOM) on 19 May 2025 to enhance effectiveness of the coordination with relevant partners to provide consular support and protection to victims, including those rescued from scam centres.

The Royal Thai Police established the "Anti-Cyber Scam Centre (ACSC)" as a onestop service hub, to integrate collaboration with government agencies, international organizations, private sector, and civil society. B. Rights of specific groups
Children
Recommendations 51.1–51.2, 51.4, 51.23, 51.85, 51.104, 51.154, 51.157–51.162, 51.181, 51.184 and 51.185
52. On 30 August 2024, the Thai Government deposited with the United Nations an instrument of withdrawal of its reservation to Article 22 of the Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC) on the protection of children seeking refugee status or recognized as refugees under applicable domestic or international law and procedures. On 28 March 2025, the Thai Government established a working group to monitor and follow-up on the implementation of measures following the withdrawal of the reservation.
53. The Civil and Commercial Code Amendment Act (No. 25) B.E. 2568, which prohibits corporal and psychological punishment of children in all settings, entered into force on 25 March 2025. The Act aims to end all forms of violent punishment against children, whether physical or psychological, in all contexts, including homes, schools and childcare settings. It represents an important measure to protect children’s rights in line with the CRC. Thailand is the second country in ASEAN to enact legislation prohibiting all forms of violent punishment against children.
54. The Criminal Code Amendment Act (No. 29) B.E. 2565 increased the minimum age of criminal responsibility from 10 to 12 years old. This is consistent with the guidance provided in General Comment No. 24 (2019) on Children’s Rights in Juvenile Justice of the Committee on the Rights of the Child, and in accordance with Thailand’s accepted recommendations and voluntary pledges under the second cycle of the UPR.
55. On 29 February 2024, the Thai Government developed a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) on the Protection of the Welfare of Children Below the Minimum Age of Criminal Responsibility as a framework for cooperation among relevant agencies in determining appropriate procedures for children below the minimum age of criminal responsibility. This is in accordance with the Criminal Code provisions on the minimum age of criminal responsibility and the Child Protection Act B.E. 2546 (2003). The Thai Government, in cooperation with UNICEF Thailand, has also developed Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) for the protection of children at risk of committing offences, children alleged to have committed acts defined as offences by law but who are below the minimum

On 15 September 2023, the Thai Government withdrew its Interpretative Declarations to the Convention against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment (CAT), concerning Article 1 on the definition of torture, Article 4 on the criminalisation of torture, attempts to commit torture, and complicity in torture, and Article 5 on the establishment of jurisdiction over such offences.
89. The Thai Government enacted the Prevention and Suppression of Torture and Enforced Disappearance Act, B.E. 2565 (2022), which entered into force on 26 February 2023. The Act establishes a comprehensive legal framework to prevent, criminalize, investigate, and punish acts of torture and enforced disappearance, while strengthening safeguards for the protection of human rights in accordance with Thailand's international obligations. The implementation of the Act encompasses three dimensions: Prevention, Suppression, and Remedy.

(Anti-SLAPP Bill), to establish a legal mechanism for preventing and addressing the problem of strategic lawsuits against public participation, and to strengthen the protection of human rights defenders, the media, civil society, and the general public in exercising their rights and freedoms in good faith for the public interest.
92. The Thai Government has strengthened the justice process in protecting the rights and liberties of the people through the issuance of the Recommendation of the President of the Supreme Court on the Criteria and Procedures for Case Consideration under Section 161/1 of the Criminal Procedure Code B.E. 2569 (2026). This empowers courts to examine and dismiss cases where it appears that the plaintiff has filed a case in bad faith or with the intent to distort facts for the purpose of harassment or to take unfair advantage of the defendant, to prevent the use of court proceedings as a tool for intimidation or the suppression of the exercise of the right to freedom of opinion and expression and participation in public affairs.
93. The Public Prosecutors’ Regulations on Criminal Proceedings also address cases involving a single offence that may be prosecuted in multiple jurisdictions to ensure consistency in the administration of justice and protect individual rights and freedoms. Where complaints are filed in several jurisdictions within the same region, the case file must be promptly referred to the relevant Regional Public Prosecutor’s Office. Where different regions are involved, it must be referred to the Department of the Attorney General’s Litigation Affairs.
