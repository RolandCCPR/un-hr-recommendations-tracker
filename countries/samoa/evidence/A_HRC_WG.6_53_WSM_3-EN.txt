Summary of stakeholders’ submissions on Samoa*
Report of the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights
I. Background
1. The present report was prepared pursuant to Human Rights Council resolutions 5/1 and 16/21, taking into consideration the periodicity of the universal periodic review and the outcome of the previous review.1 It is a summary of 12 stakeholders’ submissions2 for the universal periodic review, presented in a summarized manner owing to word-limit constraints. A separate section is provided for the contribution by the national human rights institution that is accredited in full compliance with the Paris Principles.
II. Information provided by the national human rights institution accredited in full compliance with the Paris Principles
2. The Office of the Ombudsman/National Human Rights Institution Samoa (OMB/NHRI) recommended that Samoa ratify and incorporate into domestic law ICERD, ICESCR, ICRMW, ICCPR-OP 1, ICCPR-OP 2, the Protocol to Prevent, Suppress and Punish Trafficking in Persons, Especially Women and Children and OP-CAT.3 3. The work of the OMB/NHRI continued to be challenged by budgetary and human resource limitations. Furthermore, the position of Ombudsman remained vacant since May 2023. OMB/NHRI recommended that Samoa prioritize the appointment of an Ombudsman.4 4. In 2025, OMB/NHRI examined the impact of plastic pollution on the right to a nontoxic environment. Preliminary findings highlighted threats to the right to a healthy environment including disproportionate levels of microplastics in water and fish species. OMB/NHRI recommended Samoa to recognise the right to a healthy environment in the Constitution and integrate it into legal frameworks.5 5. Pensions were available to the elderly and persons with disabilities, both of which had increased in late 2025. However, Samoa had not established a social protection floor. OMB/NHRI recommended that Samoa take steps to extend government benefits to other vulnerable groups, as part of a medium-term plan to establish a basic social protection floor. It should also strengthen oversight systems for the disbursements of pensions and grants.6

6. In relation to the right to health, OMB/NHRI reported that a major dengue fever outbreak had been declared in April 2025 and was ongoing at the time of reporting. Although acknowledging that the Ministry of Health diligently reported data on reported and labconfirmed cases, OMB/NHRI highlighted that accurate monitoring and tracking of outbreaks was challenged by limited access to laboratory testing. OMB/NHRI recommended that Samoa prioritise investment in enhancing pathology testing facilities for non-communicable, vector-borne diseases, particularly dengue fever.7
7. The number of doctors and nurses working in hospitals dropped between 2024–2025. OMB/NHRI recommended that Samoa invest in adequately staffing public hospitals and clinics with competitive salaries to retain them.8
8. OMB/NHRI recommended Samoa to ensure free birth control and testing for sexually transmitted infections are available at all hospitals. Moreover, the Ministry of Health should work to create safe spaces where these services can be accessed confidentially.9
9. OMB/NHRI highlighted that children represented 43% of the Samoan population and that Samoa’s legislative framework required harmonisation to ensure there was a consistent legal definition of ‘child’.10
10. OMB/NHRI also reported that violence against children remained a significant issue and parents had a legal right to administer ‘reasonable punishment’ to their children. It recommended that Samoa take urgent steps to prohibit corporal punishment in all alternative care settings, early childhood care, day care for older children and in all education settings for all children under 18 years.11
11. OMB/NHRI noted that the age of criminal responsibility was 10 years and recommended that Samoa raise the age of criminal responsibility to at least 14 years of age.12
12. While commending national policies that acknowledge the risks of child trafficking, OMB/NHRI noted that the low minimum working age and lack of laws prohibiting use of children in illicit activities increased vulnerability to trafficking and exploitation.13
13. OMB/NHRI noted that marriage of minors under 18 years may occur with the consent of one parent or guardian. It recommended Samoa to amend the Marriage Ordinance 1961 to explicitly prohibit marriage of minors and forced marriage.14
14. Concerning the right to education, OMB/NHRI recommended Samoa to urgently implement a targeted national intervention strategy to raise literacy and numeracy rates.15
15. OMB/NHRI highlighted that Samoa’s Constitution did not include a comprehensive definition of gender equality or explicitly prohibited discrimination based on gender, sexual orientation, disability or HIV status. Abortion was unlawful unless it fell within certain statutory exceptions. OMB/NHRI recommended Samoa to consider Constitutional amendments to include a comprehensive definition of gender equality and explicit prohibition of discrimination based on gender, sexual orientation, disability or HIV status.16
16. While commending Samoa on implementation of dedicated policies and strategies to combat GBV and family violence, OMB/NHRI noted that there was limited publicly available information on their progress and implementation status.17
17. OMB/NHRI recommended Samoa to amend the Family Safety Act 2013 to include economic violence and coercive control in the definition of violence.18
18. OMB/NHRI indicated that some villages had by-laws and cultural practices that restricted women’s access to matai titles (an eligibility requirement election to Parliament), creating a structural barrier to women’s political participation.19
19. OMB/NHRI stated that much work remained to be done to integrate Samoa’s obligations under the CRPD into domestic constitutional and legal frameworks. It recommended Samoa to implement CRPD Committee’s Concluding Observations on Samoa’s initial report.
20. OMB/NHRI acknowledged that Samoa’s first National Anti-Corruption Policy and Strategy which was launched in June 2024 was an important step towards improved

III. Information provided by other stakeholders
A. Scope of international obligations23 and cooperation with human rights mechanisms
23. The Center for Global Nonkilling (CGNK) and JS1 recommended Samoa to ratify ICCPR-OP 2.24 JS1 recommended Samoa to request technical assistance from the Friends of the Second Optional Protocol to facilitate the process of finalizing ratification.25 24. CGNK noted that the crime of genocide was present in the Samoan legislation, thus nothing hindered the ratification of the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide.26 25. The European Centre for Law and Justice (ECLJ) urged Samoa to ratify the Protocol to Prevent, Suppress and Punish Trafficking in Persons Especially Women and Children, supplementing the United Nations Convention against Transnational Organized Crime, and immediately implement policies to uphold the principles enshrined within.27 26. Oceania Human Rights (OceaniaHR) stated that Samoa should coordinate an open visit of the UN Human Rights Council Special Procedures.28
B. National human rights framework
Institutional infrastructure and policy measures 27. JS2 urged Samoa to strengthen its National Mechanism for Implementation, Reporting, and Follow-up and to ensure meaningful participation of civil society, particularly young people, in the monitoring and evaluation processes.29
C. Promotion and protection of human rights
1. Implementation of international human rights obligations, taking into account applicable international humanitarian law
Equality and non-discrimination 28. Nuanua O Le Alofa (NOLA) indicated that Samoa lacked a comprehensive and enforceable legal framework for disability rights. It noted that disability remained largely absent from constitutional protections, while existing legislation was fragmented, outdated, and inconsistent with standards enshrined in the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. NOLA recommended Samoa to address the legal vacuum which perpetuated systemic exclusion and weakened accountability, through amendments of the Constitution to explicitly prohibit disability-based discrimination and guarantee equality before the law as well as through the enactment of a comprehensive Disability Rights Act fully aligned with international standards.30

Right to life, liberty and security of person, and freedom from torture
29. CGNK regretted that Samoa still retains the death penalty in its constitution.31
Administration of justice, including impunity, and the rule of law
30. The International Center for Advocates Against Discrimination (ICAAD) acknowledged the passage of the Crimes Act 2013, which modernised Samoa's sexual offences framework and criminalised marital rape, and the Family Safety Act 2013, which introduced protection orders for victims of domestic violence. However, it noted that the implementation of these legislative reforms had been uneven and undermined by the continued reliance on customary practices to mitigate sentences in GBV cases.32
31. ICAAD identified instances where courts had ordered defendants and victims to attend counselling together in GBV cases, placing the victim at risk of continued injury and abuse and causing further trauma.33
32. ICAAD recommended Samoa to amend the Village Fono Act 1990 and the Alternative Dispute Resolution Act 2007 to limit the application of customary practices as mitigating factors in sentencing for GBV offences, and to prohibit the ordering of joint counselling between defendants and victims in GBV cases. It also recommended Samoa to ensure that village fono were equipped and encouraged to support the formal justice system, including by assisting in the enforcement of protection orders and by refraining from discouraging victims from reporting incidents to the police.34
33. ICAAD recommended Samoa to require the citation and consideration of medical reports were available in GBV sentencing decisions, and to invest in training for medical and legal professionals to improve the integration of medical evidence into the justice system. It also recommended Samoa to continue efforts to collect and publish sex-disaggregated data on GBV prevalence, reporting, prosecution, and sentencing outcomes.35
34. ICAAD highlighted that victim anonymity remained a concern noting that in Samoa's smaller communities, protecting victim anonymity was particularly important for improving the sense of safety for victims and trust in the judicial system. It recommended that Samoa strengthen the protection of victim anonymity in GBV proceedings, particularly for child victims, and ensure that courts consistently apply existing provisions governing the suppression of identifying information.36
35. Judgment UN Skills Development (JUNSD) recommended Samoa to strengthen the judicial infrastructure and expand the reach of the formal justice system. JUNSD also recommended that Samoa enhance transparency and accountability in public institutions as well as integrate community arbitration and mediation mechanisms into the national system.37
36. NOLA stated that access to justice for persons with disabilities was inconsistent, under-resourced, and often inaccessible also due to the absence of a formal system guaranteeing reasonable accommodation within legal processes. It expressed concerns over persisting cases of arbitrary detention and confinement of persons with disabilities, including Deaf persons and those with intellectual or psychosocial disabilities.38 NOLA recommended Samoa to prohibit deprivation of liberty on the basis of disability and to guarantee access to interpretation services, legal aid, and accessible legal information for persons with disabilities.39
Fundamental freedoms and the right to participate in public and political life
37. While acknowledging some progress, NOLA highlighted the persistence of barriers to political participation, noting that electoral processes were not fully accessible for persons with disabilities. It recommended Samoa to ensure full accessibility of electoral processes, including voting, information, and facilities as well as to promote and support the representation of persons with disabilities in leadership and public life.40
38. NOLA recommended Samoa to strengthen the participation of Organizations of Persons with Disabilities in monitoring and delivery of social protection programmes.41

Right to marriage and family life
39. Christian Council International (CCI) indicated that the legal minimum age of marriage in Samoa was 18 years for both males and females, following legislative reform. However, CCI noted survey data indicating that approximately 7% of girls were married before the age of 18, and a smaller proportion before age 15, raising concerns that such practices increased vulnerability to abuse, early pregnancy and discontinuation of education. CCI recommended Samoa to continue to strengthen efforts to eliminate all form of violence against all women and children.42
Prohibition of all forms of slavery, including trafficking in persons
40. ECLJ indicated that in Samoa, human trafficking was mainly used for forced labour in the agricultural, construction, food service, and domestic work sectors, often involving trafficking individuals from Samoa to other nearby countries. It noted that there was little data on how widespread the problem of human trafficking was, and the country lacked systematic reporting on the issue. ECLJ urged Samoa to take the problem of human trafficking seriously and implement procedures to identify victims and cases of human trafficking. It also urged Samoa to create a dedicated police unit to investigate all cases of human trafficking.43
Right to work and to just and favourable conditions of work
41. JS2 acknowledged that Samoa took regulatory and policy steps to address labour market challenges through the Samoa National Employment Policy 2021–2026. However, informal employment remained widespread, particularly among youth who lacked formal qualifications or access to secure jobs, and often entailed low earnings, limited social protection, and precarious working conditions. JS2 also noted that limited access to entrepreneurial support including start up financing, business development services, and mentorship, further constrained young people’s ability to create and sustain their own enterprises.44
42. JS2 recommended Samoa to develop a comprehensive National Youth Employment Strategy, promote youth entrepreneurship programs and enhance apprenticeships and workbased learning opportunities.45
Right to social security
43. NOLA indicated that social protection systems were inadequate, inconsistent, and not grounded in a rights-based framework, highlighting that persons with disabilities continued to rely heavily on families, increasing the risk of neglect and harmful practices. NOLA recommended Samoa to establish a legislated, rights-based social protection system for persons with disabilities and expand coverage to address disability-related costs.46
Right to an adequate standard of living
44. NOLA was concerned that support for independent living for persons with disabilities was minimal. It recommended Samoa to develop community-based support services that promote independent living.47
45. JS2 observed that poverty remained a significant underlying factor affecting children’s rights and overall well-being in Samoa, influencing access to education, health care, protection, and opportunities for development. It recommended Samoa to scale up targeted poverty reduction initiatives, including financial support, livelihood programmes, and services for vulnerable families, particularly in rural areas.48
Right to health
46. NOLA reported several issues impacting health systems in Samoa including the fact that facilities and information were often inaccessible; mental health legislation remained non-compliant with human rights standards and rehabilitation and psychosocial support services were insufficient It recommended Samoa to reform relevant legislation to align with
5

CRPD standards as well as to allocate dedicated budgets for accessibility and reasonable accommodation in healthcare.49
47. JS2 acknowledged that the Samoa Health Sector Plan 2021–2025 set out a comprehensive framework to strengthen service delivery, improve access to care, and enhance health system performance across the life cycle. However, JS2 noted that significant gaps persisted affecting the quality, accessibility, and responsiveness of the health system. JS2 recommended Samoa to establish a National School Mental Health Programme; improve healthcare infrastructure in rural and remote areas; expand investment in health workforce development; ensure availability of essential medicines at affordable prices; and promote preventive healthcare and health literacy campaigns.50
48. Samoa Family Health Association (SFHA) appreciated efforts by the Ministry of Education, Sports and Culture in delivering Family Life Education in schools, but expressed concerns that its implementation and contents may have not followed the UN International Technical Guidelines on Sexuality Education and may have increased young people's vulnerability to, for example, intimate partner violence, sexual violence, unplanned pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections.51
49. SFHA recommended Samoa to effectively implement and monitor holistic, rightsbased comprehensive programmes related to sexual and reproductive health, with adequate budget allocation, aligned with the National Sexual and Reproductive Health Policy, for all Samoans including young people.52
50. SFHA reiterated its recommendation that Samoa should implement the “Inter-Agency Essential Services Guide” to establish a national referral system for survivors of violence and provide funding support for non-governmental organizations providing direct support to survivors including psychosocial, economic, and legal support, and medical.53
51. JS3 raised concerns over existing stigma associated with mental health issues and the inadequate funding for mental health services for the general population, including female law enforcement officers suffering from Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder. It recommended Samoa to ensure sustainable funding for comprehensive mental health services.54
Right to education
52. JS2 recognized Samoa’s commitment to ensuring inclusive and equitable education, including the provision of free primary and secondary schooling and ongoing reforms under the Education Sector Plan 2025–2030. JS2 stated that despite these efforts, persistent challenges continued to limit the realization of quality education for all such as hidden costs including transportation, uniforms, learning materials, and other school-related expenses. JS2 noted that disparities between urban and rural areas were evident in the availability of qualified teachers, infrastructure, and learning resources.55
53. JS2 recommended Samoa to develop a targeted subsidy system especially for lowincome and rural families; enhance school infrastructure; scale up teacher recruitment and professional development programs; expand access to digital and technological learning resources; and promote community and civil society engagement in education planning and monitoring.56
54. SFHA and CCI recommended that Samoa develop, implement and expands an enabling environment and support mechanisms for pregnant adolescent girls and adolescent mothers to complete their educational pursuits.57
55. CCI highlighted that the Education Act 2009 provided the legal basis for compulsory education and outlined the responsibilities of the State in ensuring access to schooling.58
56. OceaniaHR recommended that Samoa create a national plan of action for human rights education, especially teaching the rights in the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child.59
Development and the environment
57. OceaniaHR and JS3 indicated that public understanding of climate change and environmental protection was limited and that strengthened enforcement of environmental laws, expanded civic education, and preservation of traditional knowledge were essential.60

58. OceaniaHR recommended that Samoa partner with civil society to provide environmental education rooted in traditional ancestral knowledge on taking care of common environment. It also recommended Samoa to support Voluntary Local Reviews and also Locally Determined Contributions for the Paris Agreement.61
59. NOLA recommended Samoa to institutionalise disability inclusion across all disaster management and climate frameworks. It also recommended that Samoa ensure early warning systems and communication channels are fully accessible.62
2. Rights of specific persons or groups
Women
60. ICAAD stated that violence against women and girls remained endemic in Samoa.63 OceaniaHR expressed similar concerns.64
61. JS3 highlighted that survivors of domestic and gender-based violence faced barriers in reporting abuse and accessing protection services, including shelters, counselling, and trauma-informed care. It recommended Samoa to strengthen enforcement of laws and policies to ensure survivor-centered protection and to increase funding for services such as counselling, shelters, and community programs.65
62. JUNSD recommended Samoa to raise community awareness to empower women and ensure their active participation in development.66 JS2 recommended that Samoa increase women’s participation in decision-making, public life, and entrepreneurship through targeted programs and capacity-building initiatives.67
63. JUNSD recommended Samoa to emulate and expand projects focused on women and children, thereby enhancing access to education and economic protection.68
Children
64. JS2 recommended Samoa to strengthen child protection systems and reporting mechanisms, to promote positive parenting practices as well as to implement comprehensive national anti-bullying policies that promote safe learning environments, including prevention programmes and accountability mechanisms.69
65. CCI highlighted that gaps remained in the legal protection of children from corporal punishment, noting that the Infants Ordinance 1961 permitted “reasonable punishment” by parents and caregivers, leaving children without full legal protection from violence in the home. CCI recommended Samoa to expand community-based prevention programmes that promote non-violent forms of child discipline and support for families.70
66. JS3 recommended that Samoa identify gaps where practices implemented by religious institutions (e.g., corporal punishment) may conflict with child protection standards.71
Persons with disabilities
67. NOLA acknowledged that Samoa had demonstrated commitment to disability inclusion through policy frameworks, sectoral initiatives, and engagement with organizations of persons with disabilities, but noted that progress remained inconsistent.72
68. NOLA reported that persons with disabilities—particularly women and girls— experienced disproportionately high levels of violence, abuse, and neglect, noting that protection systems remained inaccessible and reporting mechanisms were weak. Harmful practices, including confinement and social exclusion, continued. NOLA recommended that Samoa ensure all protection services were accessible, inclusive, and survivor-centred. NOLA also recommended that Samoa integrate disability inclusion across all GBV and protection legislation and policies.73
69. NOLA reported that the economic participation of persons with disabilities remained extremely low due to various factors including weak or absent legal protections against discrimination in employment and workplaces largely inaccessible. NOLA recommended Samoa to establish inclusive employment programmes, including quotas and incentives, and to invest in skills development, vocational training, and entrepreneurship initiatives.74

70. NOLA indicated that there was no enforceable legal guarantee of inclusive education in Samoa and reported barriers such as lack of reasonable accommodation and limited accessible learning materials, negatively impacting the learning outcomes for students with disabilities. It recommended that Samoa legally guarantee the right to inclusive education and finalise and adopt a national inclusive education policy with clear implementation mechanisms. 75 JS3 expressed similar concerns and recommended Samoa to increase domestic budget allocations for inclusive education and enforce national accessibility standards across all education infrastructure, transport, and learning materials.76
71. NOLA recommended Samoa to regulate and enforce accessibility standards in public transport systems.77
Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender and intersex persons
72. JS3 expressed concern that the Crimes Act 2013 criminalized consensual same-sex relations. It recommended that Samoa repeal discriminatory provisions of the Crimes Act, adopt anti-discrimination protections for SOGIESC persons and ensure safe reporting mechanisms in relation to violence and discrimination.78
