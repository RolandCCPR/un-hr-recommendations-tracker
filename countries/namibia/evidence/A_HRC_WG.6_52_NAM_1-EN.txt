I. Process for preparation of the report
1. The report was prepared by obtaining information in writing and holding consultative meetings from Offices, Ministries, and Agencies O/M/A's) which are members of the InterMinisterial Committee on Human Rights and Humanitarian Law (IMC).
2. Two meetings were held to verify the information submitted by O/M/A's. The meetings were attended by members of the IMC. The report underwent a process of approval by the Minister of Justice and Labour Relations and the Cabinet of the Republic of Namibia. Institutional consultations were held with civil society organisations and their views were taken into account.
3. Beyond the institutional consultations held, the Government is committed to broadening its engagement and to hold further public consultations to ensure that the perspectives of a wide array of civil society actors, community leaders, and the general public are reflected in the final report and in the ongoing implementation of human rights commitments.
II. Developments since the previous cycle
4. Namibia is a sovereign, secular, democratic and unitary State founded upon the principles of democracy, rule of law and justice for all. During the year 2023, Namibia conducted its population and housing census. The population of Namibia grew by 43.1% between 2011 and 2023, which increased from 2.11 million in 2011 to 3.02 million in 2023.

Namibia continues to implement the National Plan of Action on Women Peace and Security 2019-2024 (under review) and the National Gender Equality and Equity Policy for 2025-2035.

18. The Constitution of the Republic of Namibia remains unchanged since the submission of the previous report. A number of legislative amendments and enactments were made during the period under review. A list of new laws and amendments since the last national report is annexed as Annexure B.
19. The Namibian Constitution; Child Care and Protection Act, 2015; Criminal Procedure Act 1977, Combating of Domestic Violence Act; and Education Act, 2020; prohibits corporal punishment in all settings for adults and children. The Code of Conduct for the Teaching Service issued in terms of the Education Act 2004, states that a teacher "may not administer corporal punishment or any other degrading punishment upon a learner". The Criminal Procedure Act 1977 and other legislation which previously allowed whipping or caning as punishments for crimes committed by adults or juveniles were declared invalid by the Supreme Court of the Republic of Namibia. Consequently, corporal punishment is outlawed and prohibited in Namibia. The Child Care and Protection Act of 2015, effective from 2019, prohibits corporal punishment in settings such as schools, foster care, shelters, prisons, and police cells, and reaffirms its ban in judicial and traditional courts, while requiring parents to respect children's dignity. However, it does not explicitly outlaw corporal punishment in the home, leaving some ambiguity. To address this gap, the Government and stakeholders developed a Parenting Manual on Positive Parenting, which promotes non-violent childrearing practices. The manual provides parents with practical tools for positive discipline such as setting boundaries, praising good behaviour, using non-violent consequences, fostering emotional bonds while raising awareness about the harms of corporal punishment, encouraging nurturing and respectful approaches that uphold children's rights and wellbeing.
20. To ensure that people who live near to resource rich areas benefit from social services and development projects, Namibia has a mining policy and law. The Mineral (Prospecting and Mining) Act 1992 stipulates that no persons shall carry any reconnaissance, prospecting or mining operations in Namibia except in accordance with the licence granted. Licence conditions which require operators to provide social services and other benefits or projects, are usually stipulated to improve the lives of people who live near mines. Namibia has a Policy on Community based Natural Resource Management 2013. Community Based Natural Resources Management (CBNRM) Policy is built on the basis that espouses sound environmental management and promotes the sustainable use of natural resources, in a manner that empowers local communities who should share the responsibilities and benefits of such management.

B. Cross cutting issues
Recommendations (138.79, 81, 84-86, 89, 92, 99) (SDGs: 3, 4, 5, 10, 16)
34. The Constitution and its legislative framework prohibit discrimination on the grounds of sex, race, colour, ethnic origin, religion, creed or social or economic status. The Labour Act 2007 provides that - "A person must not discriminate in any employment decision directly or indirectly, or adopt any requirement or engage in any practice which has the effect of discrimination against any individual on one or more of the following grounds - (a) race, colour, or ethnic origin;(b) sex, marital status or family responsibilities; (c) religion, creed or political opinion; (d) social or economic status; (e) degree of physical or mental disability; (f) AIDS or HIV status; or (g) previous, current or future pregnancy".
35. Namibia is implementing the Third NGEEP 2025-2035. The Third NGEEP is anchored on Article 10 of the Namibian Constitution, which states that "(1) All persons shall be equal before the law. (2) No persons may be discriminated against on the grounds of sex, race, colour, ethnic origin, religion, creed or social or economic status". The Third NGEEP is aligned to the provisions of various regional and global commitments on gender equality.
36. Namibia has closed its gender gap by at least 81.1%. The Country is number 8 globally while in Sub-Saharan Africa, it is number 1. These improvements were in the areas of: Education; Economic Participation and Opportunity; Political Empowerment; and Health. Namibia has achieved full parity on both the Health and Survival and Educational Attainment sub-indexes.
37. Since 21 March 2025, Namibia has a female President, Vice President, Speaker of the National Assembly and the majority of Cabinet Ministers in Namibia are female. Currently, Namibia's Cabinet is composed of a majority of women (57%). The Government continues to lobby for the 50/50 representation in the political decision-making sector. The Government, through collaborations with International Institute for Democracy and Electoral Assistance (IDEA) continues to implement and hold capacity building workshops for women in Political and decision making roles, to enhance gender equality in these sectors.
38. To combat racial discrimination, the Government enacted and enforces the Racial Discrimination Prohibition Act, 1991 and is a state party to International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination.
39. There is a quota reserved for indigenous people/marginalised communities namely, the San, Ovatue and Ovatjimba at the University of Namibia and, Namibia University of Science and Technology. There is standing Cabinet Directive and policy to reserve certain vacancies in the Public Service to indigenous persons with disabilities and women.

41. 71. The Namibian Correctional Service (NCS) in collaboration with the justice system in Namibia introduced Community Service Orders (CSO) program in 2010 as a measure to reduce overcrowding in the correctional facilities. The NCS has rolled out CSO to 35 courts out of a total number of 71 courts nation-wide earmarked for CSO rollout. The NCS planned to roll-out CSO to 7 courts in Omaheke and Karas regions during the 2023/2024 financial year.
72. There is a reduction of overcrowding since the programme diverts offenders that have committed petty offences to work in the community instead of admitting them into correctional facilities. CSO are being considered as a post-effective practice since on average the government's expenditure is at N$1987.50 per inmate, per month. Offenders will retain their liberty, maintain their employment if they are employed, enabling them to continue positively contributing to the wellbeing of their family while serving their sentence.
73. Since April 2019 to 31 December 2023, a total of 2030 offenders were placed under CSO. The designed or total capacity of the prisons in Namibia is 5430 cells. The total number of prisoners stands at 4721 which is broken down as follows: 4578 sentenced inmates and 143 trial awaiting inmates. Consequently, there is available and unoccupied space of 709 inmates. Annexure C - Table #7 & Table#8.
74. Cleaning materials are provided to all Correctional facilities to ensure good sanitation and health; all facilities are clean and have potable water. All correctional facilities have working groups which include cleaning teams that clean the surroundings and the dormitories. All facilities have a clinic with qualified nurses and clinics at level I facilities (Hardap, Windhoek, Walvis Bay and Oluno) are supervised by medical doctors. Annexure C -Table #9.
75. Juveniles are kept separate from Adult inmates in accordance with section 64 of the Namibian Correctional Services Act, 2012 (Act No. 9 of 2012). There are guidelines on treatment of offenders as per the Mandela Rules. When the offenders are not treated according to the prescribed standards, the offenders can complain and has the right to take the matter to court. The Government has developed and implements a Correctional Service Health policy to address public health concerns in prisons, especially in relation to the prevention and treatment of HIV/AIDS to enhance the right to life. The implementation of the Namibian Correctional Service's Health Policy has brought about significant changes in managing communicable diseases such as Tuberculosis (TB), HIV and Hepatitis.
