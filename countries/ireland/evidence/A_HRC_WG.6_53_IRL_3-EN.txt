Human Rights Council
Working Group on the Universal Periodic Review Fifty-third session Geneva, 2–13 November 2026
Summary of stakeholders’ submissions on Ireland*
Report of the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights
I. Background
1. The present report was prepared pursuant to Human Rights Council resolutions 5/1 and 16/21, taking into consideration the periodicity of the universal periodic review and the outcome of the previous review.1 It is a summary of 50 stakeholders’ submissions2 for the universal periodic review, presented in a summarized manner owing to word-limit constraints. A separate section is provided for the contribution by the national human rights institution that is accredited in full compliance with the Paris Principles.
II. Information provided by the national human rights institution accredited in full compliance with the Paris Principles
2. IHREC, the national human rights institution, recommended ratification of OP-CAT, robust oversight in all relevant settings, and accelerated investment in community-based services.3 3. IHREC reported progress on hate crime with the enactment of the Criminal Justice (Hate Offences) Act 2024, but noted that hate speech reform had been dropped from the legislative agenda, leaving the ineffective Prohibition of Incitement to Hatred Act 1989 in place. Hate crimes and incidents were on the rise, fuelled by far-right actors and online incitement, with a chilling effect on political participation. IHREC recommended comprehensive reform of hate speech laws and adoption of a national strategy to increase political participation by under-represented groups.4 4. IHREC noted steps taken to address legacy issues around Mother and Baby Homes and Magdalene Laundries, including the Birth Information and Tracing Act 2022 and the Mother and Baby Institutions Payment Scheme, but found that deficiencies in their design fell short of effective redress and that no individual had been prosecuted for institutional abuse. It further noted that the European Court of Human Rights (ECtHR) judgment in O’Keeffe v. Ireland remained unimplemented after more than a decade. IHREC

recommended revision of the Payment Scheme and a comprehensive redress scheme for survivors of child sexual abuse in schools.5
5. IHREC reported that Ireland's housing crisis had significantly worsened since 2021, with homelessness figures doubling and housing becoming increasingly inaccessible, disproportionately affecting Travellers, Roma, asylum-seekers, and people with disabilities. IHREC recommended that Ireland ensure provision of affordable housing at the necessary scale, ratify Article 31 of the European Social Charter, and support a constitutional referendum on the right to housing.6
6. IHREC noted that Ireland remained an outlier among European and OECD countries in not having universal healthcare, with Sláintecare implementation slow and uneven, resulting in a two-tier system. Marginalised groups, including migrants, asylum seekers, people experiencing homelessness, and Travellers and Roma, faced disproportionately poor health outcomes. IHREC recommended full and timely implementation of Sláintecare.7
7. IHREC noted that while abortion services had been legalised and Safe Access Zones introduced in 2024, an independent review (the O'Shea Report) in 2023 found that significant barriers remained, including a mandatory three-day waiting period, criminalisation beyond a strict 12-week limit, and lack of clarity around fatal foetal abnormalities.8
8. IHREC noted that Ireland had not yet enacted comprehensive measures to prevent business activity from contributing to or profiting from unlawful territorial occupation, and that the Occupied Territories Bill had been narrowed in scope. IHREC recommended urgent enactment of the Occupied Territories Bill consistent with the ICJ9 Advisory Opinion of July 2024, and adoption of oversight measures to prevent use of Irish infrastructure in the direct or indirect facilitation of genocide.10
9. IHREC reported that Ireland was on track to achieve only 23 per cent emissions reductions by 2030, far short of its legally binding 51 per cent target, and that water quality and biodiversity protection remained significant concerns. IHREC recommended that Ireland hold a referendum to amend the Constitution to include provisions on the protection of biodiversity and the environment.11
10. On domestic, sexual and gender-based violence (DSGBV), IHREC reported rising demand and under-resourced services, and the absence of femicide as a stand-alone offence. IHREC reported that police responses to domestic abuse call-outs had increased by 45 per cent between 2020 and 2024, and that refuge accommodation was severely inadequate.12 IHREC recommended full implementation of the O'Shea Report, legislation specifically criminalising femicide, dedicated monitoring system for femicide, and adequately resourced DSGBV services.13
11. IHREC reported that Ireland had ratified the OP-CRPD in 2024, but that systemic failure across all aspects of life inhibited the full realisation of rights by people with disabilities, including a chronic lack of community-based supports and a de facto policy of institutionalisation. IHREC recommended robust oversight in all relevant settings, and accelerated investment in community-based services.14
12. IHREC expressed deep concern that Ireland had participated in a joint statement calling for changes in the interpretation of the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR)15 in relation to migration, which risked undermining the absolute nature of Article 3 protections. It recommended that Ireland defend the integrity of the ECHR and the independence of the ECtHR.16
III. Information provided by other stakeholders
A. Scope of international obligations17 and cooperation with human rights mechanisms

B. National human rights framework
1. Constitutional and legislative framework
14. Several stakeholders recommended the prompt enactment of legislation to include socio-economic status and gender identity as a protected ground of discrimination. They recommended that the State, public bodies come clearly within its scope, noting that Ireland had approved in principle a new ground of socio-economic disadvantage in the Equality (Miscellaneous Provisions) Bill 2024 but had not yet enacted it.22 JS7 noted that Ireland was lagging behind most European peers, with over 20 countries already including such protections.23
15. Atheist Ireland recommended amending the Equal Status Act to protect philosophical beliefs from discrimination in line with Article 18 of the ICCPR and removing all forms of discrimination on religious grounds from the Employment Equality Acts, noting that no material changes had been made since the previous review.24
2. Institutional infrastructure and policy measures
16. Several stakeholders recommended that Ireland designate an independent National Prevention Mechanism with a clear mandate, operational independence, and adequate human resources to carry out regular and unannounced visits to all places of deprivation of liberty.25
17. JS9 reported that Ireland had undertaken an independent review of its civil legal aid scheme, published in July 2025, but had not yet introduced substantive reforms.26 The Legal Aid Board had acknowledged that Ireland's civil legal aid system risked collapse during 2026 without urgent action.27 According to stakeholders, the minority review report made stark findings, including chronic under-resourcing, waiting times of up to 69 weeks for a first consultation, an outdated means test not updated since 2006, and the exclusion of legal aid for cases before quasi-judicial bodies. 28 JS9 recommended that Ireland provide a clear timeline for the reform of the civil legal aid system and a comprehensive response to the reports which emerged from the independent review report.29
C. Promotion and protection of human rights
1. Implementation of international human rights obligations, taking into account applicable international humanitarian law
Equality and non-discrimination
18. Several stakeholders reported that hate crimes and hate-related incidents were on the rise in Ireland, fuelled by far-right actors and online incitement. 30 Stakeholders noted that while the Criminal Justice (Hate Offences) Act 2024 was enacted, crucial hate speech provisions had been omitted, leaving racially minoritised communities without adequate legal recourse.31 JS4 recommended full implementation of the National Action Plan Against Racism (NAPAR), 32 prohibition of racial profiling in law,33 and legislative measures to collect disaggregated data on race, ethnicity, religion, gender and disability status across the criminal justice system.34 JS10 also noted that racialized policing data collection remained inadequate, as the revised Garda Síochána (Powers) Bill 2026 lacked demographic data collection provisions.35
19. JS4 reported that the Policing Authority commissioned research by the Irish Network Against Racism corroborated widespread evidence of racial profiling, discriminatory conduct and inadequate cultural sensitivity by An Garda Síochána, toward racially minoritised communities.36 JS4 recommended that Ireland strengthen oversight by ensuring Fiosrú (the Police Ombudsman) and the Policing and Community Safety Authority have dedicated hate crime units.37

20. AMRI reported that mixed race children who had been unjustly detained and subjected to racial discrimination in Irish institutions continued to be denied adequate reparations as adults. A 2026 Annual Report of the Special Rapporteur on Racial Equality and Racism in Ireland identified five key gaps in recognition, justice, compliance, support and historical accountability in relation to the treatment of children of African descent in institutions, recommending that redress be aligned with UN recommendations and that tailored remedies for racial discrimination be ensured.38
Right to life, liberty and security of person, and freedom from torture
21. IPRT reported that Ireland was experiencing a prison overcrowding crisis, operating at unsafe levels with 5,850 people in custody against a bed capacity of 4,736,39 and that there was a lack of adequate mental healthcare in prisons.40 CoE’s Committee for the Prevention of Torture found that three or four people were held in cramped, squalid cells designed for single or double occupancy, that physical safety in male prisons had deteriorated sharply with increased allegations of severe abuse by staff, and that the widespread use of closed observation and critical gaps in mental healthcare raised serious concerns, including conditions that may amount to inhuman and degrading treatment.41 JS12 expressed concern that the Draft General Scheme of the Inspection of Places of Detention Bill 2022 failed to cover several forms of de jure deprivation of liberty.42
22. IPRT reported that approximately 2,078 people in prison were waiting to be seen by a psychologist, representing 46 per cent of the prison population.43 IPRT recommended that prisons establish maximum occupancy limits, that the principle of prison as a last resort be legislated for, 44 and that people with serious mental health needs be diverted away from prison into timely, appropriate care.45
Administration of justice, including impunity, and the rule of law
23. JFM Research highlighted continuing impunity regarding the Magdalene Laundries, noting a climate of denial,46 and a failure to establish investigations into unidentified remains at High Park.47 JS14 reported that many affected people had been excluded from the progress made since the State apology in 2021,48 that the Mother and Baby Institutions Payment Scheme Act 2023 was inadequate,49 and that access to records remained restricted for many categories of affected people. 50 JS14 inquired if the Government will acknowledge and apologise for all forced family separation abuses,51 and amend the Payment Scheme.52
24. CoE reported that a case concerning the lack of effective remedies for the excessive length of judicial proceedings, McFarlane v. Ireland and the case of O'Keeffe v. Ireland on failure to protect children from sexual abuse in a national school setting both remained pending under the enhanced supervision procedure of the Committee of Ministers, having been outstanding for five years or more.53
25. DRCC reported that barriers within the criminal justice process contributed to high levels of attrition in sexual violence cases, with process described as lengthy and emotionally demanding.54 DRCC called for stronger protection55 and TGC for prohibition of enforced disclosure of victims’ counselling records in pretrial disclosure hearings and in sexual offence trials,56 noting that the proposed Criminal Law and Civil Law (Miscellaneous Provisions) Bill 2026 did not strike an appropriate balance between victims’ rights and the rights of the accused to a fair trial.57
The right to participate in public and political life
26. WfE reported that only 25 per cent of Teachtaí Dála (members of parliament) were women following the November 2024 general election, placing Ireland 98th in the world for women's representation.58 WfW recommended implementation of candidate gender quotas at local government level, a gender quota for cabinet formation, and the full implementation of recommendations of the Task Force on Safe Participation in Political Life.59
Right to privacy
27. JS14 reported that under the Birth Information and Tracing Act 2022, certain applicants — including some adopted and boarded-out people — must attend a mandatory
4

Information Session before they can access their own birth certificate, where one or both parents have registered a preference for no contact, and that in practice, State agencies have been redacting records and withholding them despite Ministerial assurances to the contrary.60 JS14 recommended that the Act be amended to ensure all applicants receive a schedule of their records, that no records be withheld or redacted, and that mothers and all others affected by Ireland's forced family separation system be included in its remit.61
Prohibition of all forms of slavery, including trafficking in persons
28. Several stakeholders noted that Ireland remained a Tier 2 country according to the 2025 Trafficking in Persons Report, meaning it was not fully meeting minimum standards.62 JS1 welcomed the Third National Action Plan on Human Trafficking published in October 2023, and recommended Ireland develop a costed, time-bound implementation framework.63
29. Several stakeholders recommended that Ireland urgently operationalise the revised National Referral Mechanism and expand the number of agencies authorised to identify victims. 64 JS6 recommended that Ireland implement a child-specific National Referral Mechanism,65 and JS8 recommended strengthening the national framework for identifying and supporting child victims of trafficking.66
Right to work and to just and favourable conditions of work
30. CoE noted that the minimum wage paid to workers aged 18 and 19 did not ensure a decent standard of living, that the wage paid to young workers between 16 and 18 was too low, and that there had been insufficient progress in promoting equal pay between women and men or ensuring a balanced representation of women in decision-making in private companies.67
31. The Irish Traveller Movement reported that Traveller unemployment remained at 63 per cent nationally,68 and JS5 noted that LGBTQI+ people, particularly trans and non-binary people, faced heightened employment and economic inequalities.69 JS4 recommended that Ireland collect and publish disaggregated data on labour market participation by ethnicity and religion, and conduct systemic equality audits to monitor pay gaps by ethnicity. 70 JS2 recommended that Ireland regulate and phase out zero hour contracts, particularly in sectors where Roma workers are over represented.71
32. NWCI reported that family leave payments remained significantly below average earnings and that Ireland was the only EU country where state support for family leave was not linked to earnings, leading to women reducing working hours or leaving employment with disproportionate impact on lone parents.72 MMM reported that 30 per cent of Irish mothers reduced working hours following childbirth, and that 63 per cent changed their working status, reflecting a significant motherhood penalty. 73 MMM recommended that Ireland acknowledge motherhood as an intersecting ground of gender-based discrimination and address it in employment and equality policies.74 AkiDwA reported that many migrant women remained concentrated in low-paid and precarious sectors, with approximately 20 per cent of non-EU migrants at risk of poverty compared to 11 per cent of Irish-born individuals.75
Right to social security
33. CoE reported that it had not been established that Traveller families, single-parent families and other vulnerable families received appropriate economic protection, therefore the situation was not in conformity with Article 16 of the revised European Social Charter.76 The Irish Traveller Movement reported that Travellers experienced ongoing systemic challenges in accessing justice and social protection, with waiting times at the Travellerspecific Legal Service reaching 36 weeks for a first consultation.77
34. JS9 reported that Roma and Traveller communities continued to face significant barriers in accessing social security payments due to the habitual residence condition under the Social Welfare Consolidation Act 2005.78 JS9 expressed stark concern at proposals to introduce a similar habitual residence condition for access to social housing and emergency accommodation, warning that this would remove the safety net for communities already most at risk of marginalisation and homelessness.79 Stakeholders recommended that Ireland review

the habitual residence condition and its discriminatory impact on minority ethnic and migrant communities, and not proceed with extending it to social housing supports.80
35. JS2 reported that Roma people faced significant obstacles in accessing Personal Public Service (PPS) numbers, bank accounts, and social welfare payments, and recommended that Ireland streamline access to these services.81 IPRT noted that people leaving prison without housing were locked out of social welfare supports. 82 NWCI recommended that the Minimum Essential Standard of Living be established as the benchmark for social protection payments.83
Right to an adequate standard of living
36. Several stakeholders highlighted the worsening housing crisis.84 OSCE-ODIHR noted that Traveller communities faced challenges in accessing adequate housing, including poorquality accommodation lacking basic utilities.85 The Irish Traveller Movement reported that only 96 new units of Traveller-specific accommodation had been built during the 2019-2024 National Traveller Accommodation Programme, with 20 per cent of Traveller households applying for social housing living in institutional or emergency accommodation. 86 JS3 reported that over 90 per cent of Roma experienced discrimination when accessing accommodation, with administrative barriers, including digital access, documentation and language, preventing Roma families from accessing social housing.87 The Irish Traveller Movement recommended establishment of the National Traveller Accommodation Authority, repeal of criminal trespass legislation, and acceleration of new accommodation units.88 JS3 recommended targeted measures to eliminate administrative barriers faced by Roma in accessing social housing.89
37. OCO reported that over 250,000 children were experiencing enforced deprivation and that 8.5 per cent of children were living in consistent poverty in 2024, up from 4.8 per cent in 2023, representing the highest level since the previous UPR cycle. OCO noted that Traveller and Roma children, children in the international protection system, and homeless children were not included in official poverty statistics. OCO recommended that measures to combat child poverty incorporate a rights-based approach and focus on both adequate income supports and access to services.90
Right to health
38. Several stakeholders raised concerns about barriers to accessing abortion care.91 IFPA reported that the mandatory three-day waiting period was contrary to WHO best practice,92 that narrowly defined foetal anomaly grounds continued to force women to travel abroad,93 and that the O'Shea Report had not been implemented.94 NWCI estimated that at least 240 women and pregnant people per year were still travelling abroad for care. 95 Pro Life Campaign expressed concern about reported documented cases of coerced and forced abortion.96 Several stakeholders recommended implementing the O'Shea recommendations, including ending the mandatory waiting period.97
39. Several stakeholders raised serious concern about Child and Adolescent Mental Health Services (CAMHS). 98 JS8 reported that over 4,200 children were on CAMHS waiting lists, and over 28,000 children were waiting to access primary care psychology services.99 MHR reported that only 5.8 per cent of the health budget was allocated to mental health in 2026, far below the 10 per cent Sláintecare target.100 Several stakeholders recommended urgent investment in mental health services, and amendment of the Mental Health Bill 2024 to prohibit the admission of children to adult psychiatric units.101
40. JS5 noted that gender-affirming healthcare was inaccessible for most trans people, with an estimated 18-year waiting time and over 2,500 people on waiting lists.102 JS5 and NWCI recommended that Ireland commit to the development and resourcing of genderaffirming healthcare services informed by international best practice and co-designed with the transgender community.103
41. ECLJ and ADF International expressed concern about the proposed legalisation on assisted suicide.104

Right to education
42. Stakeholders reported that families continued to face high costs and unequal access to early childhood education and care (ECEC), particularly Traveller, Roma, migrant and rural families, and recommended a publicly funded and delivered ECEC model.105 AsIAm reported that Autistic and disabled children continued to face fragmented access to educational supports despite expanded Special Education Teacher posts, and recommended that the Convention on Education centres the voices of Autistic and disabled students.106 JS15 noted the unavailability of certain science, technology, engineering and mathematics subjects at single-sex girls' schools, and negative experiences of transgender and gender-diverse students,107 recommending that Ireland make immediate investments in single-sex schools, ensuring they have the necessary resources and facilities to offer a broader, more equitable curriculum and equal opportunities to all students regardless of gender.108
43. OCO reported concerns about disproportionate use of reduced school days for Traveller and Roma children, and the lack of a framework to monitor schools with higher rates of suspension or exclusion, and recommended strengthening measures for equal access to education for children in disadvantaged groups.109
44. DRCC recommended comprehensive, age-appropriate consent education addressing attitudes, behaviours and social norms.110 Atheist Ireland recommended ensuring access to neutral and objective education, through non-denominational schools and non-discriminatory exemptions from religious instruction. 111 JS11 recommended that Ireland amend the Education (Admission to Schools) Act 2018 so as to respect parity between faith communities.112 ICHR Galaway recommended that Ireland ensure the availability of Irishmedium education throughout the State.113
Development, the environment, and business and human rights
45. Several stakeholders reported that Ireland remained significantly off track to meet its legally binding 51 per cent emissions reduction target by 2030.114 Stakeholders recommended that Ireland ensure sufficiently ambitious Annual Climate Action Plans and Carbon Budgets, and adoption of an intersectional climate justice strategy.115
46. Several stakeholders reported the absence of a comprehensive mandatory human rights due diligence framework for business.116 A second National Action Plan on Business and Human Rights was six years overdue.117 Stakeholders reported that a plant in County Limerick, responsible for the production of alumina, had been linked via its supply chain to the continuation of an illegal military invasion of another State.118 JS17 recommended that Ireland adopt binding legislation requiring companies to identify, prevent, mitigate and account for adverse human rights impacts in their domestic and international operations.119
47. CAID acknowledged Ireland's calls for a ceasefire in Gaza, increased support to UNRWA, and intervention in cases before the ICJ.120 However, stakeholders reported that the Government had narrowed the scope of the Occupied Territories Bill to exclude trade in services, notwithstanding that the ICJ Advisory Opinion made no distinction between trade in goods and services. 121 Several stakeholders recommended urgent enactment of the Occupied Territories Bill in line with the ICJ Advisory Opinion of July 2024.122
2. Rights of specific persons or groups
Women
48. Several stakeholders reported that DSGBV services remained chronically underresourced and inaccessible to migrant women.123 DRCC noted sustained increases in demand and persistent waiting lists for counselling services, with reliance on short-term funding limiting sustainability. 124 SiSi reported that systems intended to protect victims systematically failed to protect survivors of domestic violence, with abuse reframed as conflict, and the use of the discredited concept of parental alienation overriding children's expressed fears and safety.125 AkiDwA recommended that DSGBV frameworks be fully accessible to migrant women through culturally competent, trauma-informed services and safe reporting mechanisms independent of immigration enforcement.126

49. MOVE noted uneven geographic access to perpetrator intervention programmes as a key gap in Ireland's domestic violence prevention infrastructure. 127 JS6 reported strong linkages between human trafficking and DSGBV or migration that required integrated policy responses. 128 Several stakeholders recommended that Ireland adopt a comprehensive, adequately funded national response including perpetrator programmes, and survivor-centred justice reform.129
Children
50. Children participating in the JS8’s consultations called for an end to paedophilia and child sexual abuse, for stronger legal protections against exploitation and trafficking, and for greater attention to online safety.130 JS8 recommended that Ireland develop and implement a multi-stakeholder national action plan to prevent and address all forms of violence against children, including sexual abuse, trafficking, domestic violence and online exploitation, with adequate funding, clear targets and timelines.131
Older persons
51. Several stakeholders reported the absence of a clear statutory obligation on the State to protect adults at risk from abuse, neglect or financial exploitation and that institutionalisation was being imposed on many older people.132 NWCI reported that older persons, experienced long waiting lists, and that continued reliance on private provision raised concerns about affordability, access and quality.133 SAGE noted that the Law Reform Commission had recommended a new overarching statutory framework for adult safeguarding in 2024.134 SAGE recommended that adult safeguarding legislation be fasttracked and an independent National Adult Safeguarding Authority be established.135
Persons with disabilities
52. AsIAm reported that the Assessment of Need system was under significant pressure, with over 20,000 children overdue an assessment, and average waiting times exceeding 27 months.136 MHR noted that the Mental Health Bill 2024 retained legal basis for involuntary detention, treatment, seclusion and restraint.137 CoE highlighted ongoing deficiencies in consent-to-treatment frameworks.138 MHR recommended that Ireland enact and implement the Mental Health Bill 2024 without delay, ensuring that remaining gaps are fully aligned with CRPD, particularly in relation to autonomy, legal capacity, and non-discrimination,139 and to establish statutory rights to independent advocacy for all individuals accessing mental health services.140
Minorities
53. CoE reported that Travellers and Roma continued to face structural anti-Traveller racism and antigypsyism, including over-policing, and significant overrepresentation in the criminal justice system.141 According to JS3, in 2024, 75 per cent of Travellers reported having experienced discrimination in the previous year,142 61 per cent were unemployed,143 41 per cent of Roma lived in severe material deprivation, 144 and that while Travellers accounted for 0.6 per cent of the overall population, they accounted for 22 per cent of the female prison population.145 JS3 reported that the inability to monitor outcomes of inclusion measures remained a problem due to the lack of systematic ethnic data collection.146 JS3 recommended that Ireland ensure full and timely implementation of the National Traveller and Roma Inclusion Strategy II 2024-2028 with clear targets and adequate resourcing.147
Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender and intersex persons
54. JS5 reported that while some progress had been made on LGBTQI+ commitments, significant gaps persisted. 148 JS5 noted that LGBTQI+ organisations continued to deliver frontline services not otherwise provided by the State but faced fragile and largely short-term funding.149 The National LGBTQI+ Inclusion Strategy II was published in 2025, one year after its declared start date, with a civil society shadow assessment identifying areas with minimal to no implementation. 150 JS5 recommended sustainable funding for LGBTQI+ organisations and meaningful consultation with the community in the development of strategies and legislation.151

55. Several stakeholders noted that key legislative commitments remained outstanding, including a ban on conversion practices, reform of the Gender Recognition Act 2015 to include non-binary people and under-16s, and the addition of gender identity as a protected ground in equality legislation.152 JS5 recommended full delivery of the 2020 Programme for Government commitments and reform of the Gender Recognition Act.153 JS8 recommended an anti-bullying strategy for schools covering gender identity and sexual orientation.154 LGBT Disregard welcomed the announced disregard legislation for men convicted of consensual sexual activity, but called for its full scope to include men convicted under military legislation and for broader restorative justice measures.155
Migrants, refugees and asylum-seekers
56. IRC reported that accommodation in international protection settings remained inadequate, with complaints about overcrowding, rural isolation, lack of privacy, and negative impacts on children. The IRC noted that vulnerability assessments had been paused for much of 2024 and that only 51 of over 300 centres were subject to independent industrystandard inspection. 156 Between January 2025 and January 2026, 4,618 men seeking international protection were refused accommodation.157
57. JS13 reported that as of early 2026, a significant number of male international protection applicants, including identified victims of torture, had not been provided with basic accommodation upon arrival. 158 JS13 also raised concerns about the International Protection Bill 2026 implementing the EU Pact on Migration and Asylum, including inadequate vulnerability assessments, 159 risks of re-traumatisation by requirements to repeatedly recount traumatic experiences and long waiting period, 160 and provision prohibiting family reunification applications until two years after a sponsor had been granted international protection, with no waiver available for vulnerable applicants including victims of torture.161 JS13 recommended that Ireland include a possibility to waive the two-year period for vulnerable persons.162
58. OCO raised similar concern that the International Protection Bill 2026 imposed onerous new family reunification requirements on beneficiaries of international protection including unaccompanied minors, by permitting for the first time the detention of children solely due to immigration status and providing for the use of medical exams to determine age, which OCO considered neither evidence-based nor human rights compliant. OCO recommended that an independent assessment on the child rights impacts of the legislation be commissioned.163
59. Several stakeholders expressed concern about political discourse framing migration as a threat to social cohesion, which had coincided with escalating anti-immigrant incidents, arson attacks on accommodation centres, and fear among migrant communities. 164 JS2 recommended that Ireland ensure that public discourse by political leaders does not undermine human rights obligations and explicitly condemn racist violence and anti-migrant intimidation, including attacks on accommodation centres.165 The CoE Commissioner wrote to the Irish Prime Minister in 2023 calling for a comprehensive review of structural shortcomings in the international protection system.166
