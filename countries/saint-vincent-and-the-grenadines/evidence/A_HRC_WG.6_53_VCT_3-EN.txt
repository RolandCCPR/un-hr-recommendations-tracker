Summary of stakeholders’ submissions on Saint Vincent and the Grenadines*
Report of the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights
I. Background
1. The present report was prepared pursuant to Human Rights Council resolutions 5/1 and 16/21, taking into consideration the periodicity of the universal periodic review and the outcome of the previous review.1 It is a summary of 8 stakeholders’ submissions2 for the universal periodic review, presented in a summarized manner owing to word-limit constraints.
II. Information provided by stakeholders
A. Scope of international obligations and cooperation with human rights mechanisms
2. Joint Submission 2 (JS2) stated that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had ratified eight of the nine core international human rights instruments.3 Joint Submission 1 (JS1) noted, however, that the country had not yet ratified several important treaties, including the International Convention for the Protection of All Persons from Enforced Disappearances; the Second Optional Protocol to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights; the Optional Protocol to the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights; the Optional Protocol to the Convention on the Rights of the Child on a communications procedure; the Optional Protocol to the Convention against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment; and the Optional Protocol to the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women, and recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines ratify them.4 3. The European Centre for Law and Justice (ECLJ) noted that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines was a party to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and the Protocol to Prevent, Suppress and Punish Trafficking in Persons, Especially Women and Children, supplementing the United Nations convention against Transnational Organized Crime, both protecting against trafficking in persons. 5 The International Center for Advocates Against Discrimination (ICAAD) noted that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines

4. JS1 stated that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had ratified the Charter of the Organization of American States (OAS) and was actively engaged with various OAS mechanisms. Despite this, it noted that the country had ratified very few OAS human rights treaties and recommended that it ratify them, including the American Convention on Human Rights; the Inter-American Convention against All Forms of Discrimination and Intolerance; and the Additional Protocol to the American Convention on Human Rights in the Area of Economic, Social and Cultural Rights.7
B. National human rights framework
Institutional infrastructure and policy measures
5. Joint Submission 5 (JS5) stated that, in its third cycle review, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had accepted 12 recommendations to establish an independent national human rights institution in line with the Principles Relating to the Status of National Human Rights Institutions (Paris Principles), including completing a feasibility study related thereto. It noted, however, that the State did not have an independent national human rights institution and had not conducted the feasibility study. JS5 recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines engage with regional and international organizations, such as the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights, to access resources to conduct such a study. 8 JS1 recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines establish an independent national human rights institution with a broad mandate to protect human rights, with adequate human and financial resources, in accordance with the Paris Principles.9
6. JS1 stated that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines should prepare and publish a national action plan on human rights to ensure systemic and comprehensive action for the promotion and protection of human rights for all people in the country.10
7. JS1 commended Saint Vincent and the Grenadines for having established the National Human Rights Monitoring and Reporting Mechanism in the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Foreign Trade, Foreign Investment and Diaspora Affairs to coordinate the implementation, monitoring and reporting of accepted recommendations.11 JS5 noted that the mechanism was endowed with the ability to consult with other stakeholders, including civil society organisations, in the implementation of its mandate.12
C. Promotion and protection of human rights
1. Implementation of international human rights obligations, taking into account applicable international humanitarian law
Equality and non-discrimination
8. JS1 and JS2 noted that, while the Constitution of Saint Vincent and the Grenadines explicitly protected persons from discrimination, it only covered six grounds: sex, race, place of origin, political opinion, colour, and creed.13 JS1 further noted that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had not adopted any national legislation providing full and effective protection against discrimination and prohibiting direct, indirect and intersecting discrimination.14 JS2 added that, despite Constitutional provisions, the absence of a national human rights institution aligned with the Paris Principles made it difficult to practically implement Constitutional protections.15
9. JS1 and JS2 recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines adopt comprehensive anti-discrimination legislation.16 JS2 added that the design of such legislation had to be inclusive and participatory.17
10. Christian Council International (CCI) recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines continue efforts to protect all individuals from violence, harassment and arbitrary

discrimination.18 JS1 noted that there were no hate crimes laws in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines.19
Right to life, liberty and security of person, and freedom from torture
11. Joint Submission 4 (JS4) stated that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had observed a de facto moratorium on executions since 1995 and that one person remained on death row.20 It added that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had consistently voted against the United Nations General Assembly resolution calling for a global moratorium on the death penalty, most recently in 2024.21
12. JS4 recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines abolish the death penalty and replace it with a sentence that is fair, proportionate, and aligned with international human rights standards. It also recommended that, in the interim, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines impose an official moratorium on executions and collaborate with civil society organisations to conduct a comprehensive public awareness campaign to educate the public and elected officials on international human rights standards related to the death penalty and on alternatives to it.22
13. JS4 noted reports documenting inadequate prison conditions in the country, including chronic understaffing, severe overcrowding, persistent contraband flows, and the absence of dedicated space to separate children from adults. It recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines ensure that detention conditions comply with the Nelson Mandela Rules.23
Administration of justice, including impunity, and the rule of law
14. JS4 stated that persons in conflict with the law continue to face prolonged pretrial detention due to the absence of a legal aid system. It added that the State only provided legal representation for people charged with capital offences and that many people remained in custody for more than a year before their first court appearance. JS4 recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines increase the resources and personnel available to the criminal justice system to minimize the backlog and reduce the use and length of pretrial detention.24
15. JS1 noted that, in 2019, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had passed the Child Justice Act to establish a judicial process for children accused of committing crimes, but that the Act had not been proclaimed by the Governor-General, which was a necessary step for it to become enforceable. It recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines proclaim the Child Justice Act.25
16. JS1 recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines ensure the consistent application of the Eastern Caribbean Supreme Court Sentencing Guidelines, particularly the recognition of the victim’s sexual orientation or gender identity as an aggravating factor for the crime.26
Right to marriage and family life
17. Three submissions noted that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines did not recognize same-sex marriage or civil unions under national law.27 JS2 stated that, as a result, LGBTQI+ individuals were denied equal rights in areas such as family life, inheritance and social protection.28 JS5 recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines enact legislation to make civil unions and marriages available to same-sex couples.29
18. JS1 noted that, according to the Marriage Act, the minimum age of marriage with parental consent was 15 for girls and 16 for boys. It recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines amend the Marriage Act to raise the minimum age of marriage to 18 years for girls and boys.30
Prohibition of all forms of slavery, including trafficking in persons
19. ECLJ noted that, in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, human trafficking for both commercial sex and labour was a persistent problem. It added that victims of human trafficking included citizens of Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, as well as individuals from other Caribbean countries and Asia, and that the main method used by traffickers to recruit victims involved social media posts promising lucrative jobs.31

20. ECLJ welcomed that in 2023 Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had amended the Prevention of Trafficking in Persons Act to enact stricter penalties for individuals convicted of human trafficking. 32 However, it noted that the country lacked formal victim and protection programs and that, despite progress in preventing human trafficking through labour inspections and legal frameworks, there remained a decade-long lack of prosecutions and insufficient services for victims.33
21. ECLJ underscored Saint Vincent and the Grenadines’ failure to identify a single victim of human trafficking since the last universal periodic review, due to a lack of capacity to thoroughly investigate and prosecute such cases. It added that there was a considerable backlog of investigations and that the Anti-Trafficking in Persons Unit was underfunded and lacked the resources needed to monitor the country’s thirty-two islands. 34 ECLJ recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines allocate the resources needed to investigate cases of human trafficking, identify victims, and prosecute human traffickers to the fullest extent of the law.35
Right to work and to just and favourable conditions of work
22. JS1 noted that an Employment Relations (Prevention of Sexual Harassment) Bill had been drafted but not yet adopted and recommended to pass it and ensure that sexual harassment was explicitly defined as a crime.36
23. JS5 stated that there were no laws in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines that prevented discrimination in employment based on sexual orientation, gender identity and expression, and sex characteristics, and that employment discrimination was common for job seekers that identified as LGBTQI+.37
24. Joint Submission 3 (JS3) raised concerns regarding the situation of healthcare workers from a third country deployed to Saint Vincent and the Grenadines under a bilateral agreement to support the health-care system in the country. It stated that the agreement was not publicly accessible and raised concerns regarding the compliance of this kind of agreements, concluded by several countries worldwide, with international human rights and labour standards, including on freedom of movement, freedom of expression and access to justice. JS3 recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines establish mechanisms for the direct hiring of these workers by the State under domestic law, thereby ensuring the protection of workers’ rights.38
Right to health
25. CCI stated that adolescent pregnancy remained a concern. It recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines expand family-centred prevention initiatives aimed at reducing adolescent pregnancy.39
26. CCI noted that in 2023 the maternal mortality ratio of Saint Vincent and the Grenadines was estimated at approximately 56 deaths per 100,000 live births, remaining below the global average. It recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines continue strengthening maternal health services, particularly in the areas of prenatal and postnatal care for adolescent and vulnerable mothers.40
27. CCI stated that, under the Criminal Code (Act No. 23 of 1988) abortion remained generally prohibited except in specific circumstances, including when it was necessary to preserve the life of the pregnant woman, protect her physical or mental health, or in cases involving rape, incest, or substantial risk of severe foetal impairment.41
28. JS1 noted that conversion therapies were not illegal in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines and recommended that the country pass laws that explicitly ban conversion therapies based on sexual orientation and gender identity. 42 JS5 made a similar recommendation.43
29. JS5 stated that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines did not prohibit medically unnecessary interventions aimed at modifying sex characteristics of intersex minors without their free, prior and full informed consent. It recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines enact legislation that restricts medically unnecessary interventions aimed to

30. JS5 stated that laws criminalizing consensual same-sex conduct between adults hindered efforts to protect public health and effectively address HIV, as they may drive affected individuals underground and away from essential health services, including HIV prevention, testing, treatment, and care.45
Right to education
31. CCI noted that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines provided free and compulsory education for children aged 5 to 16 years, covering primary and secondary levels and that literacy rates were relatively high, with adult literacy estimated at approximately 96%, and youth literacy rates slightly higher. It also noted that net enrolment at the primary level was high, exceeding 90%, but declined at the secondary level, reflecting challenges in retention and completion.46
32. CCI stated that adolescent pregnancy remained a contributing factor to school dropout, continuing to disrupt education continuity, limiting opportunities for young mothers and reinforcing cycles of vulnerability and inequality. It recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines extend support mechanisms for pregnant students and adolescent mothers to ensure continuity of education and reintegration into formal schooling.47
33. CCI noted that comprehensive sexuality education was delivered through the Health and Family Life Education Programme, and that during the last universal periodic review Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had noted recommendations calling for the revision of this programme to align with the International Guidance on Comprehensive Sexuality Education and including comprehensive sexuality education as a mandatory component of the national curriculum.48
34. JS1 and JS5 stated that the syllabus for the Caribbean Secondary Education Certificate Social Studies curriculum was revised in 2023 to include content addressing issues related to sexual orientation and gender identity, including those affecting LGBTI persons. They recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines implement comprehensive sexuality education in line with international standards.49
35. JS5 noted that the lack of safe and inclusive environment contributed to bullying and other forms of violence which impacted the right to education of LGBTQI+ children and adults. It recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines provide professional development and training to educators on how to create a safe space for LGBTIQ+ students in both primary and secondary schools; and establish anti-bullying and discrimination policies including creating a mechanism to report and adjudicate incidents.50
2. Rights of specific persons or groups
Women
36. ICAAD stated that the pervasiveness of patriarchal attitudes towards women were deeply embedded within society and culture and that the effects of this was seen not only in interpersonal relationships, but at an institutional level within the legal and education systems where labour force participation rates greatly favoured men.51
37. ICAAD stated that the Gender Affairs programme under the Ministry of the Family and Gender Affairs, Persons with Disabilities, Local Government and Labour, which was the primary government department responsible for promoting policies, programmes and other interventions that enhanced gender equality, did not have adequate resourcing to implement its stated objectives and recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines increase its funding.52
38. ICAAD stated that, while Saint Vincent and the Grenadines performed relatively strongly on some gender equality indicators, it continued to face significant challenges in addressing gender-based violence against women, compared with other Eastern Caribbean States.53 JS5 stated that gender-based violence had diminished in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines through anti-violence campaigns and government initiatives. 54 CCI

acknowledged the legal and institutional framework in place but recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines continue to strengthen efforts to eliminate all forms of violence against all women and children.55
39. ICAAD stated that the Domestic Violence Act 2015 had expanded the protection previously available to victims. However, it noted that the Act did not explicitly criminalize domestic violence as it was meant to expand the range of remedies for victims and establish a framework for domestic violence response.56 JS1 noted that, under the 2015 Domestic Violence Act, domestic violence was treated as a civil matter and not a crime and recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines amend the Act to ensure that domestic violence is defined as a crime.57
40. JS1 stated that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had not implemented the recommendations from the previous universal periodic review related to explicitly making marital rape a crime and enacting a sexual harassment bill and amending the Domestic Violence Act to make domestic violence a crime.58 It noted that the State had been working for over 5 years on a Sexual Offences Bill to update laws in relation to sexual offences under the Criminal Code, but that the bill was still pending.59 ICAAD recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines close legal gaps to align with international standards, including by creating specific offences against sexual harassment and marital rape.60
41. ICAAD stated that, despite efforts to streamline and update data collection systems, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines did not produce timely and sufficiently accurate data on gender-based violence. This was making it difficult to accurately assess the level of genderbased violence in the country and, in turn, to create evidence-based policies and programs to address the problem. ICAAD recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines place more emphasis on accurate reporting and recording of gender-based violence statistics and that this be the focus of specific roles within the Ministry of the Family and Gender Affairs, Persons with Disabilities, Local Government and Labour. It also recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines establish a gender-based violence data and case management system to better enable reporting and data collection.61
42. ICAAD noted that in 2024, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines launched an “interagency” gender-based violence task force to help reduce impunity, increase access to justice and ensure adequate, shock responsive and integrated quality health and social protection services for gender-based violence survivors and victims. It added that, in 2024, Gender Affairs undertook antiviolence campaigns in schools, clinics and communities as part of the 16 days of activism against gender-based violence.62
43. ICAAD recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines enhance police and law enforcement capacity and accountability to ensure consistent and adequate reporting and enforcement of domestic violence crimes.63
44. ICAAD recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines improve access to government-established crisis and emergency housing for gender-based violence and domestic violence victims by removing any requirement for recipients to have a Protection Order issued by the courts.64
Children
45. CCI stated that violence against children was a significant concern in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines. It noted that corporal punishment remained widely practiced within the home and was legally permitted under the concept of ‘reasonable punishment’. It recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines expand community-based prevention programmes promoting non-violent forms of child discipline and supporting families.65
46. CCI noted that reports of sexual abuse and exploitation of children persisted, often occurring within family or community environments, which contributed to underreporting and long-term harm.66
47. JS1 noted that, in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, the age of sexual consent was 15 for girls, but there was no explicit age of sexual consent for boys. It recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines amend existing laws to ensure that the age of sexual consent is gender neutral.67

Persons with disabilities
48. JS1 noted that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had had a comprehensive Disability Bill for several years and recommended to adopt it.68
Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender and intersex persons
49. JS5 noted that in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines there were no laws that permitted legal gender recognition or non-binary identities, meaning that individuals were only recognized as their sex assigned at birth, no matter their gender expression.69
50. JS1 stated that neither the Constitution nor any enforceable domestic laws explicitly protected persons from discrimination on the grounds of sexual orientation, gender identity, or gender expression in areas such as education, health, employment, housing, or the provision of goods and services. 70 JS5 expressed similar concerns. 71 Both submissions recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines enact legislation that explicitly protects individuals from discrimination by State and non-State actors on the basis of sexual orientation, gender identity and expression, and sex characteristics.72
51. JS1 stated that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines did not have legislation addressing hate crimes based on sexual orientation, gender identity and expression, and sex characteristics, nor comprehensive measures to prevent such acts through education, awareness-raising, and structural protections.73 JS1 and JS5 recommended that the country enact legislation to sanction such acts.74
52. Three submissions noted that in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines consensual samesex conduct was criminalized under sections 146 and 148 of the Penal Code, which prohibited acts of buggery and gross indecency. They highlighted that the existence of these provisions impacted the lives of those who identified as lesbians, gay, bisexual, transgender, intersex, queer, and other gender or sexual minorities, contributing to their continued stigmatization and marginalization and to the risk of abuse, violence, discrimination, and lack of protection from law enforcement. They recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines repeal the buggery and gross indecency provisions in the Criminal Code that penalize consensual samesex activity.75
53. JS1 recommended that the Domestic Violence Act be amended to ensure that the definition of domestic violence was gender neutral, thus covering same sex couples.76
54. JS2 noted that the definition of rape in the Criminal Code only recognized women as victims and men as perpetrators and that the language was restrictive and heteronormative, denying certain victims access to justice and effective remedies. 77 Three submissions recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines amend the Criminal Code to revise the definition of rape to be gender-neutral to ensure equal protection for all victims regardless of gender.78 JS1 also recommended to broaden the definition of rape to cover all forms of nonconsensual sexual acts.79
55. JS5 recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines leverage the existing National Human Rights Monitoring and Reporting Mechanism to collect data and report on discrimination against LGBTIQ+ individuals.80
Migrants, refugees and asylum-seekers
56. JS1 noted that, despite having ratified the 1951 Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees and its 1967 Protocol, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had not enacted any asylum legislation. It recommended that the State enact refugee and asylum legislation ensuring that all persons applying for international protection have access to a fair and full asylum procedure.81
