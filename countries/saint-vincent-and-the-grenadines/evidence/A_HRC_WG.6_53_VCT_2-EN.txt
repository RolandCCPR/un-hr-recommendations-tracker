Saint Vincent and the Grenadines
Compilation of information prepared by the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights
I. Background
1. The present report was prepared pursuant to Human Rights Council resolutions 5/1 and 16/21, taking into consideration the outcome of the previous review.1 It is a compilation of information contained in relevant United Nations documents, presented in a summarized manner owing to word-limit constraints.
II. Scope of international obligations and cooperation with human rights mechanisms
2. In 2022, the Special Rapporteur on the issue of human rights obligations relating to the enjoyment of a safe, clean, healthy and sustainable environment commended Saint Vincent and the Grenadines for having ratified all the major global human rights treaties, including the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women, the Convention on the Rights of the Child and the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities.2
3. The Special Rapporteur on environment noted, however, that some optional protocols remained to be ratified, such as the Optional Protocol to the Convention on the Rights of the Child on a communications procedure and the Optional Protocol to the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights.3 The United Nations subregional team in Barbados stated that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had not ratified the Second Optional Protocol to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, aiming at the abolition of the death penalty, the Optional Protocol to the Convention against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment and the Optional Protocol to the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women.4
4. The United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines ratify the Optional Protocol to the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights.5
5. The Special Rapporteur on environment observed that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines was a Party to all the major environmental treaties and was also engaged in numerous regional environmental initiatives through the Organisation of Eastern Caribbean States, the Caribbean Community and the Caribbean Forum. 6 The Special Rapporteur

It has worked with Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, supporting relevant actors to enhance their engagement with international human rights mechanisms.11
III. National human rights framework
1. Constitutional and legislative framework
10. The Special Rapporteur on environment noted that the Constitution of Saint Vincent and the Grenadines guaranteed civil and political human rights, including freedom of conscience, freedom of expression, freedom of assembly and association, freedom of movement and protection against discrimination. The Special Rapporteur observed, however, that economic, social, cultural and environmental rights were not guaranteed in the Constitution.12
2. Institutional infrastructure and policy measures
11. The Special Rapporteur on environment reiterated the recommendation made previously by United Nations agencies to establish an independent national human rights institution in accordance with the principles relating to the status of national institutions for the promotion and protection of human rights (the Paris Principles), a recommendation made during the 2011, 2016 and 2021 universal periodic review cycles.13
IV. Promotion and protection of human rights
A. Implementation of international human rights obligations, taking into account applicable international humanitarian law
1. Equality and non-discrimination
12. The United Nations subregional team noted that, despite constitutional protections against discrimination on certain grounds, the legal framework in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines still lacked explicit protections against discrimination based on sexual orientation or gender identity, and that no comprehensive anti-discrimination legislation had been adopted.14

2. Right to life, liberty and security of person, and freedom from torture
13. The United Nations subregional team stated that the newer Belle Isle detention facility represented a significant improvement in detention infrastructure. However, it noted that the older Kingstown prison reportedly continued to face structural limitations and that reports had indicated that conditions remained inadequate, and included overcrowding, sanitation concerns and difficulties in separating categories of detainees.15
3. Administration of justice, including impunity, and the rule of law
14. The United Nations subregional team noted that, in 2025, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had ranked thirty-first out of 182 countries in the Transparency International Corruption Perceptions Index, indicating relatively moderate levels of perceived publicsector corruption as compared to the global average.16
15. The Special Rapporteur on environment noted that there did not appear to be any environmental cases being brought to the courts, suggesting a need for increased enforcement of environmental laws, capacity-building in the legal profession, and judicial education.17
4. Fundamental freedoms and the right to participate in public and political life
16. UNESCO noted that in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines defamation constituted a criminal offence punishable by up to two years’ imprisonment. It also observed that, in 2026, the country had adopted the Cybercrimes Act, which had extended the scope of defamation to include online publications and had introduced penalties of substantial fines and up to seven years’ imprisonment. UNESCO recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines decriminalize defamation and place it within civil defamation legislation that was in accordance with international standards.18
17. UNESCO stated that although Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had adopted the Freedom of Information Act in 2003 recognizing the right to access information in the possession of public authorities, the law had not yet entered into force. UNESCO added that there was no dedicated regulatory or oversight body to manage requests for access to information, to handle refusals or to enforce transparency obligations. UNESCO recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines introduce an access-to-information law that was in accordance with international standards, and in particular that it set up an independent oversight institution with the capacity to implement the access-to-information law.19
18. UNESCO noted that the National Telecommunication Regulatory Commission – responsible for technical regulation and the setting of technical telecommunications standards, and for monitoring and ensuring licensing – was composed of three to five members, all of whom were appointed by and could be removed by the minister responsible for telecommunications. UNESCO encouraged Saint Vincent and the Grenadines to assess the system of supervision of the broadcasting sector to ensure that this process was transparent and independent.20
5. Right to marriage and family life
19. UNESCO recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines amend its legislation to ensure that the minimum age of marriage was 18 years, without exceptions.21
6. Prohibition of all forms of slavery, including trafficking in persons
20. In its 2025 direct request under the Forced Labour Convention, 1930 (No. 29), the Committee of Experts on the Application of Conventions and Recommendations of the International Labour Organization (ILO) noted that the Prevention of Trafficking in Persons Act of 2011, which criminalized trafficking in persons for the purpose of sexual or labour exploitation, had been amended in 2023 in regard to the penalties for offences set out in the legislation. It added that the Act also outlined ways and modes of assistance for victims of trafficking in persons and provided for the establishment of a national task force against the trafficking of persons.22

21. The United Nations subregional team noted that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had adopted a National Action Plan to Combat Trafficking in Persons, for 2021–2025, which promoted a multisectoral approach to victim identification, protection and prevention.23
7. Right to work and to just and favourable conditions of work
22. The ILO Committee of Experts, in its 2025 observation under the Discrimination (Employment and Occupation) Convention, 1958 (No. 111), urged the Government to adopt without further delay an effective framework prohibiting direct and indirect discrimination on all grounds, covering all workers. It also noted the lack of information regarding the adoption of the draft Employment Relations (Prevention of Sexual Harassment) Act and urged Saint Vincent and the Grenadines to take the necessary measures to adopt it without delay.24
23. The United Nations subregional team noted that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had expanded technical and vocational education and training initiatives to strengthen access to employment and skills development, including youth training and apprenticeship programmes designed to improve employability and reduce youth unemployment.25
24. The ILO Committee of Experts, in its 2025 direct request under the Equal Remuneration Convention, 1951 (No. 100) and the Discrimination (Employment and Occupation) Convention, 1958 (No. 111), noted that, according to World Bank data, in 2023 women’s labour force participation remained significantly low, at 55.1 per cent, as compared to 75.3 per cent for men. It added that women earned on average 96.2 per cent of men’s income.26
25. The United Nations subregional team indicated that women remained disproportionately concentrated in certain sectors of employment, particularly services and tourism, which could increase vulnerability to economic shocks.27
26. The ILO Committee of Experts, in its 2025 observation under the Freedom of Association and Protection of the Right to Organise Convention, 1948 (No. 87), noted that the revised Labour Relations Bill was under review and expressed the expectation that it would be in full conformity with the Convention. It welcomed the fact that the draft legislation recognized the right to organize for all workers, while requesting amendments to ensure trade union autonomy and that judicial appeals against cancellation of registration had suspensive effect.28
27. The ILO Committee of Experts, in its 2023 observation under the Labour Inspection Convention, 1947 (No. 81) and the Labour Inspection (Agriculture) Convention, 1969 (No. 129), noted that labour inspection services operated with a limited number of inspectors and that several positions for occupational safety and health inspectors remained vacant. It encouraged the Government to ensure that the labour inspection services had at their disposal an adequate number of inspectors to enable them to carry out their duties effectively.29
8. Right to an adequate standard of living
28. The United Nations subregional team noted that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines maintained a relatively high level of human development compared to many small island States, although structural vulnerabilities remained due to economic shocks, natural disasters and limited economic diversification.30
29. The Special Rapporteur on environment noted that the impacts of the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic on the economic health of Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had been multiplied by the eruption of La Soufrière volcano on 9 April 2021, which had forced the evacuation of more than 20,000 people. The eruption had caused disruption to the water supply and shortages for some people, interruptions of the power supply, loss of livelihoods and food insecurity, partially through the loss of backyard gardens. The Special Rapporteur also noted that data indicated that women had suffered a disproportionate loss of income as a result of the eruption as compared to their male counterparts.31
30. In 2022, the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) reported that, prior to the eruption of La Soufrière volcano, approximately 30 per cent of the population was living below the poverty line. It stated that the eruption had had a significant impact on people’s

lives and livelihoods and had further exacerbated food insecurity and poverty, which had already increased during the COVID-19 pandemic. In response, the Ministry of National Mobilization had partnered with UNICEF to temporarily expand and strengthen the social protection system.32
31. The Special Rapporteur on environment commended the Zero Hunger Trust Fund, which provided nutritional and financial support to vulnerable children and older persons, and programmes such as the Love Box, whereby the Government purchased food from farmers needing markets, then donated the food to families displaced for months by the volcanic eruption.33
32. The Special Rapporteur noted that housing quality was poor in informal settlements, increasing vulnerability to climate-related natural disasters, and access to water and sanitation was limited, leading to pollution.34 He recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines ensure safe, sufficient water and adequate sanitation, avoid cutting off water services for poor households due to non-payment of bills and consider establishing a solidarity levy on all customers to pay for water used by poor households.35
9. Right to health
33. The Special Rapporteur on environment noted that the World Health Organization had estimated that environmental risk factors, such as air pollution and water contamination, contributed to 140 deaths per year in the country, or approximately 15 per cent of total deaths. 36 He also noted an increase in vector-borne diseases in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, including dengue fever.37
34. The Special Rapporteur stated that the decline in both commercial and subsistence agriculture was causing a shift towards more expensive, often highly processed imported foods, with negative health consequences, including obesity and diabetes.38 He also noted that, despite efforts to dispose of obsolete, dangerous pesticides, some pesticides classified by the World Health Organization and the Food and Agriculture Organization as highly hazardous reportedly continued to be permitted.39 The Special Rapporteur recommended banning any further use of highly hazardous pesticides (categories I and II) and collecting any remaining inventory for safe disposal.40
35. The United Nations subregional team noted that the legal framework regulating abortion remained restrictive and that under the Criminal Code abortion was generally prohibited and could carry penalties of up to 14 years’ imprisonment. It added that termination of pregnancy could be permitted in limited circumstances, including where the life and physical or mental health of the pregnant woman was at risk, in cases of rape or incest, or where there was a substantial risk of serious fetal impairment.41
10. Right to education
36. UNESCO stated that the Saint Vincent Constitution Order of 1979 did not enshrine the right to education, and that the Education Act of 2006 did not comprehensively guarantee it, as its implementation was subject to available resources. UNESCO recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines consider enshrining the right to education within the Constitution and its legislation.42
37. UNESCO noted that the Education Act of 2006 provided for 11 years of compulsory and free primary and secondary education and for free but not compulsory pre-primary education. UNESCO recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines guarantee in its legislation at least 12 years of free primary and secondary education and introduce legislation to make pre-primary education compulsory for at least one year.43
38. The United Nations subregional team noted that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines recognized education as a key pillar of national development and had prioritized access to education under the National Economic and Social Development Plan 2013–2025. 44 UNESCO noted that government expenditure on education as a percentage of total government spending was 12.86 per cent in 2023 and 12.63 per cent in 2024.45

39. The United Nations subregional team stated that the education system showed relatively low dropout rates, and that in the 2023/24 academic year, boys had accounted for more than 60 per cent of cases of dropout.46
40. The ILO Committee of Experts, in its 2023 observation under the Minimum Age Convention, 1973 (No. 138), noted that the minimum age for work or employment was 14 years and that the age of completion of compulsory education (set at 16 years under the Education Act of 2006) continued to be higher than the minimum age for work or employment. It requested Saint Vincent and the Grenadines to take the measures necessary to raise general minimum age for admission to employment or work in order to link it with the age of completion of compulsory schooling. 47 UNESCO made a similar recommendation.48
11. Cultural rights
41. UNESCO encouraged Saint Vincent and the Grenadines to fully implement the relevant provisions of the Convention for the Protection of the World Cultural and Natural Heritage, of 1972, the Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage, of 2003, and the Convention on the Protection and Promotion of the Diversity of Cultural Expressions, of 2005, that promote access to and participation in cultural heritage and creative expressions, by communities, practitioners, cultural actors and civil society organizations, as well as representatives of vulnerable groups (minorities, migrants, refugees, youth, Indigenous Peoples and persons with disabilities), and to ensure that equal opportunities were given to women and girls to address gender disparities.49
12. Development, the environment, and business and human rights
42. The United Nations subregional team stated that climate-related disasters in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had highlighted the close link between environmental protection and the enjoyment of human rights. It also noted that the 2021 eruption of La Soufrière volcano had caused widespread displacement and damage to housing, agriculture and infrastructure, affecting access to livelihoods and basic services.50
43. The Special Rapporteur on environment emphasized that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines was acutely vulnerable to the adverse impacts of climate change. He noted that 80 per cent of the population, infrastructure and economic activity were located at less than 8 metres above sea level, and that the climate crisis was not only multiplying risks but was also exacerbating inequalities.51 The Special Rapporteur noted the extensive damage inflicted on the country by flooding, droughts, landslides, rising sea levels, saltwater intrusion and ecosystem changes. He stated that those climate change-related impacts were wreaking havoc on farms, forests, fisheries, housing, infrastructure and communities, and in doing so were violating the rights to life, health, food, water, housing, cultural rights and a clean, healthy and sustainable environment.52
44. The Special Rapporteur acknowledged the many laws that related to environmental protection. However, he noted that the Constitution was silent on matters related to the environment and recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines introduce a constitutional and/or legislative amendment to recognize the right to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment.53 He also recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines carry out a review of environmental legislation to identify where new or amended legislation and/or regulations were needed.54
45. The Special Rapporteur observed that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines was implementing mitigation and adaptation actions domestically, pursuant to its national climate change policy, national climate strategy and implementation plan, and national adaptation plan. He noted that the national adaptation plan aimed to mainstream climate change adaptation into planning and budgeting across all economic sectors and both the public and the private sectors.55
46. The Special Rapporteur was impressed by the progress made by Saint Vincent and the Grenadines on many fronts, including water, solid waste management, and adapting to climate change. 56 He recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, inter alia, continue taking an effective, rights-based approach to domestic climate action, including

47. The Special Rapporteur noted that, pursuant to the Regional Agreement on Access to Information, Public Participation and Justice in Environmental Matters in Latin America and the Caribbean (Escazú Agreement), everyone in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had the right to information about environmental matters, the right to participate in environmental decision-making, the rights to freedom of expression and association on environmental matters, and the right of access to effective remedies for environmental harm. It added that, while those initiatives contributed to the protection of human rights in the context of business activities, the absence of a comprehensive policy framework or national action plan on business and human rights indicated scope for further progress in aligning national practices with the Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights.59
B. Rights of specific persons or groups
1. Women
49. The United Nations subregional team noted that the Constitution of Saint Vincent and the Grenadines guaranteed equality before the law and protection against discrimination on several grounds, including sex, but that domestic legislation did not contain a comprehensive statutory definition of discrimination against women, and protections were primarily grounded in constitutional provisions rather than in a stand-alone equality law.60
50. The subregional team stated that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had continued to strengthen its policy framework for gender equality through the work of the Gender Affairs Division within the Ministry of National Mobilisation, Social Development, Family Affairs, Gender Affairs and the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. It noted that, despite the initiatives taken, structural gender inequalities persisted, and that, according to international assessments, women remained underrepresented in national political decision-making structures. Data gaps also remained significant, with only about 25.5 per cent of the genderrelated indicators in the Sustainable Development Goals available, limiting the ability to fully monitor progress towards gender equality.61
51. The United Nations subregional team noted that violence against women remained a significant concern in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines. It stated that in 2024 the country had launched the National Task Force on Gender-Based Violence to improve data collection, enhance inter-agency cooperation and strengthen national prevention strategies. However, it added that international reports continued to highlight persistent challenges, including underreporting of sexual violence, limited resources for victim support services, and the need for improved data collection on gender-based violence.62
52. The Special Rapporteur on environment stated that rural women were particularly vulnerable to the effects of climate-related natural disasters and that it was important to ensure the participation of women, especially rural women, in the design and implementation of policies and programmes intended to prepare for, reduce the risks of and recover from climate-related and natural disasters.63

2. Children
53. The United Nations subregional team stated that, since 2020, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had progressively implemented the National Child Protection Policy, which provided a coordinated, multi-sectoral framework for prevention of abuse, neglect, exploitation and violence against children. It added that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines had been recognized as the first Eastern Caribbean country to adopt such a comprehensive policy.64
54. UNESCO noted that the Education Act of 2006 authorized the use of corporal punishment in schools. It recommended that Saint Vincent and the Grenadines legally proscribe corporal punishment in education settings.65
55. The ILO Committee of Experts noted that there had been no progress in prohibiting hazardous work for children under 18 years of age. It urged Saint Vincent and the Grenadines to take the measures necessary to ensure that its legislation prohibited the employment of children under 18 years of age in hazardous work, and determined the types of hazardous work prohibited to children under the age of 18 years.66
56. The Special Rapporteur on environment emphasized that the climate crisis was causing children to miss school, contributing to high levels of stress and anxiety, and putting them – especially girls – at risk of violence in disaster situations. He noted that the Caribbean Community’s Commission on Youth had urged Governments to overcome the “lack of awareness and education with regard to environmental issues, in particular the effects of climate change”.67
3. Persons with disabilities
57. The United Nations subregional team noted that the Constitution guaranteed equality before the law, and that the National Economic and Social Development Plan 2013–2025 had identified the protection of vulnerable groups, including persons with disabilities, as a key development priority.68
58. The ILO Committee of Experts, in its 2025 direct request under the Discrimination (Employment and Occupation) Convention, 1958 (No. 111), stated that a profile note published by the World Bank in 2023 providing an overview of the legal and social context affecting persons with disabilities in the country had showed that the legal framework did not provide equal opportunities for persons with disabilities to participate in the civil, political, economic and social spheres; promote their access to the general education system; and establish a quota system for the employment of persons with disabilities in both the public and the private sectors.69
4. Indigenous Peoples and minorities
59. The Special Rapporteur on environment noted that the Garifuna Indigenous people, many of whom lived in coastal communities in the red zone around La Soufrière, were among the groups most exposed to the effects of climate-related and environmental harm.70
5. Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender and intersex persons
60. The United Nations subregional team noted reports documenting discrimination and social stigma affecting LGBTQ+ persons in areas such as employment, housing and education, highlighting the need for legal reforms and stronger equality protections.71
