I. Background
1. The present report was prepared pursuant to Human Rights Council resolutions 5/1 and 16/21, taking into consideration the periodicity of the universal periodic review and the outcome of the previous review.1 It is a summary of 43 stakeholders’ submissions2 for the universal periodic review, presented in a summarized manner owing to word-limit constraints.3 A separate section is provided for the contribution by the national human rights institution that is accredited in full compliance with the Paris Principles.
II. Information provided by the national human rights institution accredited in full compliance with the Paris Principles
2. CHRAGG highlighted developments including implementation of the Action Plan on Anti-Trafficking in Persons (2021-2024), the National Strategy to Eliminate Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) 2020/21-2024/25), Judiciary Strategic Plan 2020/2021-2024/2025, and the National Action Plan for the Rights and Welfare for Persons with Albinism (2024/20252027/2029),4 and recommended that the United Republic of Tanzania:
(a) Enact legislation protecting human rights defenders;5 (b) Finalize the second national human rights action plan;6 (c) Repeal The Customary Law (Declaration) Order (GN. No. 436/1963) (No.4);7 (d) Strengthen the justice system, especially in rural areas;8 (e) Amend the legislation governing the National Electoral Commission to ensure transparency, and merit-based, non-partisan appointments of its officials;9 (f) Increase financing for water projects;10 (g) Increase the budget for the health sector, intensifying HIV and Malaria prevention campaigns, addressing stunting through multi-sectorial nutrition programmes

III. Information provided by other stakeholders
A. Scope of international obligations and cooperation with human rights mechanisms
3. Stakeholders noted that the United Republic of Tanzania had yet to ratify CAT; 20 Indigenous and Tribal Peoples Convention, 1989 (No. 169);21 ICPPED;22 and TPNW.23 4. EF recalled that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had not supported recommendations to issue a standing invitation to the Human Rights Council’s special procedure mechanism, which had limited independent field assessments of the human rights situation in the country.24
B. National human rights framework
1. Constitutional and legislative framework 5. JS7 noted that Zanzibar is governed by the Constitution of the United Republic of Tanzania, but has its own President, legislature, court system, and penal code. Shari’a law was applied by courts to matters of succession in communities that generally follow Shari’a law and Muslims may bring cases before Islamic courts. All cases tried in Zanzibar’s courts, apart from those involving Shari’a law and constitutional matters, may be appealed at the Court of Appeal of the United Republic of Tanzania on mainland Tanzania.25 6. ERF noted that in 2025, a prison bill had been introduced to transform prisons into modern correctional and rehabilitation institutions, improve detention conditions, expand the use of community service orders as an alternative to imprisonment for minor offenses, and implement a parole system.26
2. Institutional infrastructure and policy measures 7. JS26 stated that the National Human Rights Action Plan (2018-2022) had expired without implementation, and that the National Action Plan on Business and Human Rights (2025/26-2029/30) had yet to be launched, which underscored weak operationalization of policy commitments.27 8. JS14 noted that there was no specific national programme for comprehensive human rights education for public service officials and students and considered a relevant supported recommendation from the previous review to have not been implemented.28

C. Promotion and protection of human rights
1. Implementation of international human rights obligations, taking into account applicable international humanitarian law
Equality and non-discrimination
9. JS4 noted that although the Constitution guaranteed equality before the law and protection from discrimination, in practice significant gaps remained in the implementation of these formal legal protections, particularly in relation to persons of diverse sexual orientation, gender identity, gender expression and sex characteristics (SOGIESC).29
10. JS10 stated that the coexistence of parallel legal systems created ambiguity and perpetuated discrimination against women. Customary and religious laws influenced matters such as inheritance and property rights, which disadvantaged women.30
Right to life, liberty and security of person, and freedom from torture
11. JS28 recalled that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had not supported recommendations to abolish the death penalty. As no executions had been carried out since 1994, a de facto moratorium appeared to be in place, and death sentences had been commuted to life imprisonment through presidential pardons. However, the mandatory death penalty for the murder and treason as prescribed in the Penal Code required reform, as it removed judicial discretion and consideration of mitigating factors in sentencing proceedings, including mental health conditions.31
12. JS7 drew attention to reported cases of enforced disappearances, arbitrary detention, torture and extrajudicial killing of political actors, activists, journalists and protesters before, during, and after the October 2025 elections.32 According to JS21, post-election protests had erupted in response to the exclusion of opposition candidates and alleged electoral irregularities.33 JS5 noted reports of hundreds of deaths allegedly attributed to the police and unidentified security personnel, including victims who had posed no threat to public order or security.34
13. JS15 highlighted ongoing risks of torture and ill-treatment in police custody due to practices including unreliable and manipulatable detention registers, and detention beyond the legal time limits, which facilitated incommunicado detention, situations resembling enforced disappearances, and torture.35
14. JS1 highlighted reported attacks and killings of persons with albinism and the inadequate protection systems for them. It underscored that ACtHPR had found that persons with albinism had been subjected to targeted killings for ritual purposes.36 Also, the use of shelters for housing persons with albinism had created conditions that violated the best interest of the child and there was therefore a need for a human rights-based family-centred protection framework.37
15. According to JS4, there had been no measurable progress in addressing violence against LGBTI persons since the previous review. Victims had often not reported incidents of violence due to fear of arrest, stigma and retaliation, and when such incidents had been reported, law enforcement officers had often failed to conduct effective investigations.38
16. JS28 described the conditions in prisons as deplorable, and highlighted overcrowding, poor sanitation, and insufficiency of food and other essential resources.39
Human rights and counter-terrorism
17. JS7 recalled that during the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported a recommendation to adopt a strategy and action plan to prevent radicalization and extremism. Although efforts had been made in that regard, no such strategy and plan had been published, while incidences of radicalism and extremism had continued to be reported. A 2022 investigation had revealed efforts to radicalize young Zanzibari men, some of whom subsequently disappeared. The widening intelligence and security gaps had undermined

efforts to effectively address terrorism and prevent violent extremism, while the country was emerging as a source of recruitment for jihadi networks.40
Administration of justice, including impunity, and the rule of law
18. JS17 stated that articles 109(1), 109(8), 110A, 112, 118 and 120A of the Constitution conferred on the Presidency extensive powers in relation to judicial appointments, tenure and removal. These provisions weakened institutional safeguards, increased the risk of executive influence and were inconsistent with international standards on judicial independence and separation of powers. Limitations on judicial independence contributed to violations of fair trial rights, particularly in politically sensitive cases.41
19. JS28 stated that fair trial rights had been eroded by the limited access to legal aid. Despite the Legal Aid Act, 2017, only a few indigent accused had received assistance. Also, vulnerable groups, such as juveniles, persons with disabilities, and individuals from marginalized communities were not guaranteed legal representation.42 JS24 stated that legal aid services were not available in most rural regions, which left survivors of gender-based violence with no meaningful recourse.43
20. According to JS28, section 29 of the Evidence Act provided that a confession would not be excluded solely on the grounds of coercion unless the court was satisfied the coercion was likely to have caused a false admission of guilt. The admission of coerced confessions undermined the integrity of the justice system and risked entrenching a system that inadvertently incentivised human rights violations as a means of extracting evidence.44
21. ERF stated that a significantly high number of persons had been held in pro-longed pre-trial detention, awaiting the commencement of their trials.45 JS15 added that the failure by courts to scrutinize prolonged unlawful detention, among other systemic failures, contributed to structural impunity.46
22. JS17 stated that, following the October 2025 election, over 1,700 persons had reportedly been held under investigation for non-bailable offences. The use of non-bailable offences in politically sensitive cases effectively removed judicial discretion and undermined the presumption of innocence.47
23. J17 stated that the Advocates Act permitted government actors to participate in disciplinary proceedings against lawyers, creating actual and perceived conflicts of interest, particularly in cases where such lawyers were engaged in litigation against the State. This practice violated paragraph 28 of the Basic Principles on the Role of Lawyers. 48 JS17 also highlighted reprisals against lawyers involved with human rights causes or associated with opposition political parties.49
24. JS27 stated that since 2021, accountability for police abuses had remained severely undermined by a combination of political interference and structural conflicts of interest, which resulted in impunity.50
25. JS2 state that although some law enforcement officials, prosecutors, and judicial personnel had received training on gender-based violence and cybercrime, there was no evidence of nationwide training on technology-facilitated gender-based violence. Consequently, many frontline actors lacked the expertise to identify and investigate such cases.51
Fundamental freedoms and the right to participate in public and political life
26. JS9 noted a decline in the freedoms of association, assembly, and expression,52 while JS3 noted a decline in civic space and restrictions on digital rights and freedoms.53
27. EF noted that during the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported recommendations relating to the freedoms of expression, assembly and associations, and stated that the broad provisions in the legal framework, including the Media Services Act of 2016, the Cybercrimes Act of 2015, and the Statistics Act of 2015, had granted the Authorities sweeping powers to restrict such freedoms.54
28. JS5 stated that, notwithstanding the guarantees contained in article 18 of the Constitution protecting the freedoms of expression and opinion, limitation clauses had
4

A/HRC/WG.6/53/TZA/3
systematically been broadly interpreted to restrict such freedoms on grounds including national security and public morality. 55 CPJ stated that the Authorities had relied on restrictive legislation and administrative measures to control the media, which had led to journalists facing intimidation, arrests and detention for their critical reporting.56
29. JS3 highlighted the enactment of legislation and regulations that restricted civic and digital freedoms, including the Media Services Act, as amended in 2023, and the Electronic and Postal Communications (Online Content) Regulations of 2020, as amended in 2022 and 2025, particularly through licensing requirements and content restrictions.57 ERF stated that in May 2025, the Communications Regulatory Authority had shut down more than 80 000 websites, social media accounts, blogs, and online platforms.58
30. RVT underscored deterioration in the protection of human rights defenders, since the previous review. JS3 stated that human rights defenders, civil society organizations, journalists, online activists and bloggers, had been subjected to harassment, arrests, and prosecutions.59
31. JS21 stated that civil society organizations had faced burdensome compliance requirements and operated within a fragmented legal framework with some organizations falling under the Non-Governmental Organizations Act of 2002, as amended, while others the Societies Act. Compliance obligations were notably extensive.60
32. JS5 stated that the Non-Governmental Organizations Act of 2002, as amended in 2005 and 2019, granted the Authorities broad discretion to deny registration or impose restrictions on organizations. 61 JS4 stated that between 2023 and 2025, the Authorities had deregistered 14 organizations that had provided health and support services to LGBTI persons.62
33. ADF stated that freedom of religion or belief was unduly restricted under the Penal Code, particularly sections 125 and 129; 63 the Societies Act prescribed mandatory registration of religious organizations, with five-year renewal periods; 64 converts from Islam to Christianity in Zanzibar had experienced family rejection, and threats from community members opposed to their change of faith.65
34. JS7 stated that non-Muslims in Zanzibar were expected to comply with certain Islamic practices, including the wearing of the hijab in school; and that the Christian community had faced discrimination.66
35. JS7 recalled that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported recommendations on holding free and fair elections and highlighted widespread repression leading up to, during, and after the October 2025 elections. In its preliminary report, SADC67 Election Observation Mission found that the elections had fallen short of its principles and guidelines,68 as voters had not been able to fully express their democratic will. AUEOM69 had raised similar concerns.70
36. JS21 stated that, in the period preceding the 2025 elections, the Authorities had systematically restricted opposition rallies and had curtailed the movement of members of opposition parties.71 It also highlighted the banning and disqualification of candidates and political partes.72 JS5 made similar observations and underscored that the Political Parties Act, amended in 2019, granted the Authorities broad powers, including the power to deregister political parties.73
37. JS21 alleged that, in Zanzibar, ballot papers and results sheets had been destroyed shortly after vote-counting, thereby preventing meaningful verification of the election results.74
38. While noting that internet penetration had reached 85 percent, RVT stated that rights to free expression and privacy had been subjected to State control. It highlighted the nationwide internet shutdown from 29 October to 3 November 2025, allegedly to prevent youth organizers from live streaming the violence perpetrated by the security forces.75
39. RVT underscored the restrictive regulatory environment for digital content and noted that individuals had been prosecuted for anti-government satire under the Cybercrimes Act of 2015. JS20 added that the Act enabled excessive surveillance without effective judicial oversight.76

Right to privacy
40. JS20 stated that the enactment of the Personal Data Protection Act No. 11 of 2022 established statutory rights and core principles for the handling of personal data. However, this positive trajectory was reversed in the lead up to the October 2025 elections, with the deployment of advanced surveillance apparatuses against political dissidents.77
Prohibition of all forms of slavery, including trafficking in persons
41. JS10 noted efforts to address child labour, including through the adoption of the National Strategy on the Elimination of Child Labour (2024/25-2028/29), but observed that child labour had remained widespread, particularly in agriculture, fishing, mining, and domestic work.78
Right to work and to just and favourable conditions of work
42. Noting the protection of labour rights through the enactment of Labour Laws (Amendments) Act of 2025, RVT stated that workers in the “gig economy” did not benefit from any statutory protections, leaving them without social security, health insurance, or accident safety nets.79
43. JS10 noted that although women had constituted about 80 percent of the labour force, they had been disproportionately represented in informal, low-income, and unpaid family work. There was also a gender wage gap.80
44. JS14 stated that unemployment remained the primary challenge for persons between the ages of 18 and 30 years. Youth had often experienced poor working conditions, unfair wages, and abuse in informal employment sectors.81
Right to social security
45. JS4 recalled that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania supported a recommendation to implement social protection policies and stated that LGBTI persons had been discriminated against in accessing social protection programmes.82
Right to an adequate standard of living
46. JS9 state that there was a chronic shortage of affordable housing, which was fuelled by rapid population growth.83
47. Noting that the United Republic of Tanzania was ranked 91st out of 123 countries in the 2025 Global Hunger Index, ERF stated that access to food was hindered by poverty, rising food prices, and declining crop productivity due to climate change and droughts.84
Right to health
48. JS2 recalled that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported recommendations on the right to health, including women’s health, and highlighted conditions that compromised both quality and dignity of care, including the prevalence of obstetric violence, shortages of medicines and medical supplies, and staffing shortages of health care workers, and overcrowded maternity wards.85 JS8 noted that maternal mortality remained well above the Sustainable Development Goal target of fewer than 70 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births.86
49. JS8 stated that the criminalization of abortion in most circumstances had limited access to safe and legal services and had compelled many women and girls to resort to unsafe methods. 87 Family planning services had been limited by constraints in the health system, including contraceptive commodities. Also, social and cultural factors played a significant role in limiting contraceptive uptake.88 JS6 noted that adolescents and young people faced judgemental attitudes from health care providers when seeking sexual and reproductive health services.89
50. JS6 stated that legal, policy, and programmatic measures aimed at improving access to sexual and reproductive health services had been introduced. However, their weak implementation continued to hinder access to these services. JS6 called for a focus on
6

effective implementation the measures, accountability, sustained investment in health systems, and a rights-based approach that promoted inclusion, dignity, and equality.90
51. JS6 noted that HIV transmission remained widespread and that stigma had remained a critical barrier to achieving universal access to HIV prevention, treatment, care, and support services; adolescent girls and young women had been disproportionately affected due to gender inequality and their limited ability to negotiate safe sexual practices; and individuals had been discouraged from accessing HIV prevention and treatment services due to criminalization of certain behaviours, including aspects of sex work and drug use.91
52. Stakeholders highlighted that: (a) healthcare providers’ discriminatory attitudes towards LGBTI persons had contributed to mistrust and reduced utilization of health-care services; 92 (b) shortages of medicines, equipment and personnel in health facilities in rural areas had particularly affected Indigenous Peoples; 93 (c) persons with albinism faced a heightened risk of sun-induced skin damage and skin cancer, yet many of them had not received sunscreen lotions;94 and (d) the Zanzibar Drug Control and Prevention Authority Act of 2021 adopted a punitive rather than health-based approach to drug use and consequently, drug use was not treated as a health condition.95
Right to education
53. JS12 stated that the national education system should transition towards a technologydriven learning environment. It noted that the strategic roadmap presented in the Education Sector Development Plan (ESDP) 2025/26-2029/30 had been designed to comprehensively reform the education system and to address infrastructure deficits and revise the curriculum to meet the demands of a technology-driven economy, including by providing students with access to computers, projectors and modern laboratory equipment.96
54. While noting efforts to implement the fee-free policy at both primary and secondary levels, JS14 expressed concern that families were still required to make contributions to support school operations and the acquisition of learning materials.97
55. Stakeholders underscored the shortage of classrooms which resulted in overcrowded learning environments, the lack of basic furniture, and a shortage of teaching staff.98
56. While highlighting that progress had been made in increasing access to education, JS10 noted: (a) continuing barriers to education for vulnerable groups, including children with disabilities and children with albinism; (b) weak implementation of inclusive education policies; (c) chronic under-resourcing of the education sector; and (d) low transition rates from primary to secondary education and low completion rates at the secondary level.99
57. AcAs stated that weak institutional and policy coordination, underfunding, and inadequate infrastructure and teacher support, had restricted access to quality education for children with disabilities. 100 Noting the adoption of the National Strategy for Inclusive Education 2021/22-2025/26, GERG underscored the need to strengthen its effectiveness and considered a relevant recommendation from the previous review to have been only partially implemented.101
58. JS1 stated that children with albinism often required reasonable accommodation for low vision to ensure equal access to education and recommended ensuring access to spectacles and low-vision aids, large-print and high-contrast learning materials, seating and lighting adjustments, teacher training on low vision, and school-level accommodation plans for learners with albinism.102
59. JS2 recalled that, during the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported recommendations aimed at improving access to education for pregnant schoolgirls and adolescent mothers. However, existing laws and policies neither adequately addressed, nor sufficiently facilitated, their re-entry into the education system. Although the re-entry guidelines allowed girls to return to school within two years, several provisions remained restrictive, and implementation depended heavily on the discretion, capacity and willingness of individual schools and educators.103

60. JS29 stated that access to comprehensive sexuality education remained limited and, consequently, many young people lacked accurate knowledge about contraception, reproductive health and HIV prevention.104
Cultural rights
61. JS13 expressed concern over the restrictions on the right to artistic expression.105 It noted that artists and content creators were required to submit video content for preapproval;106 and that the Film Board and the Minister responsible for culture were granted sweeping and discretionary powers to censor or ban films and stage plays.107
Development, the environment, and business and human rights
62. JS12 referred to recommendations supported during the previous review and highlighted that, although progress in national development had been made, the full realization of robust infrastructure and broad-based economic empowerment remained an ongoing endeavour, noting that the Tanzania Development Vision 2050 recognized the need for sustained efforts to ensure inclusive growth and access to essential services throughout the country.108
63. JS9 noted the steps that had been taken to address climate change, including through the adoption of the National Climate Change Response Strategy, 2021-2026, but underscored that the Strategy did incorporate traditional knowledge and human rights principles, particularly with regard to ensuring the free, prior, and informed consent of communities affected by climate-related projects. In addition, women, youth, persons with disabilities, and local communities, were under-represented in climate-related decision-making processes.109
2. Rights of specific persons or groups
Women
64. Noting inter alia the National Plan of Action to End Violence Against Women and Children for 2024-2029, GERG stated that critical gaps remained in legal protections, enforcement mechanisms, and in access to justice. 110 GERG considered a relevant supported recommendation from the previous review to have been partially implemented.111
65. JS30 underscored that there was no comprehensive legislation on gender-based violence and that the existing legal framework remained fragmented and incomplete.112 JS14 noted the absence of definitions of “marital rape” and “family violence” in national policy, as well as limited education and economic independence, increased women’s vulnerability to abuse and limited their ability to seek justice or access necessary support services.113
66. JS2 recalled that during the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported recommendations to address FGM and noted the criminalization of FGM under the Sexual Offenses Special Provisions Act. However, the Act restricted criminal liability to parents, guardians, or other custodial figures, which discouraged reporting, as victims were unwilling to testify against relatives.114
67. JS2 stated that legislation addressing technology-facilitated gender-based violence remained inadequate and did not specify key forms of technology-facilitated gender-based violence, such AI generated sexualized content.115
Children
68. JS25 noted the establishment of children’s councils at village-level and called for adequate funding to ensure their effective functioning.116
69. JS2 noted that despite supporting relevant recommendations at the previous review which conveyed the commitment to address child marriage, the Law of Marriage Act, which allowed girls as young as 14 years of age to marry, had not been amended. 117 GERG recalled that in 2016, the High Court had declared unconstitutional relevant provisions of the Act and had ordered its amendment, a decision that had been upheld on appeal.118 JS14 noted that child, early and forced marriage had persisted due to poverty and deep-rooted cultural traditions.119

70. JS10 expressed concern about violence against children, noting that corporal punishment remained lawful and widely practices in homes and schools;120 It also highlighted the increasing risks in digital environments, including children’s exposure to online sexual abuse, without their awareness.121
Older persons
71. JS19 stated that while Zanzibar had established a specific legal framework for older persons, mainland Tanzania still lacked a comprehensive standalone law on the rights of older persons, resulting in fragmented protection and limited policy coherence.122
Persons with disabilities
72. JS9 highlighted the adoption of the National Assistive Technology Strategy (20242027) to provide wheelchairs, hearing aids and other devices; implementation of a five-year strategic plan to train health workers on disability-sensitive care; the establishment of a national business and disability network to champion workplace inclusion; and the availability of interest-free loans to persons with disabilities for livelihood activities. It noted that a draft national disability policy had yet to be finalized.123
Indigenous Peoples and minorities
73. KOO stated that the Maasai and other pastoralist and hunter gatherer communities, including the Barbaig, Akie, and Hadzabe, satisfied internationally accepted criteria for recognition as Indigenous Peoples, as reflected in, inter alia, UNDRIP. However, the United Republic of Tanzania did not recognize Indigenous Peoples as distinct rights holders in the Constitution or legislation.124
74. JS14 stated that indigenous communities remained socially excluded and politically underrepresented. Their nomadic lifestyles prevented them from accessing basic health and education services.125
75. RVT highlighted a significant deterioration in land and pastoral rights due to the systematic alienation of ancestral lands for conservation and carbon-credit investments.126 JS23 stated that since the previous review, Indigenous Maasai pastoralists had faced increasing land alienation through reclassification, conservation expansion, militarization, and carbon market interventions;127 and emphasized the lack of alignment of conservation, climate, and development policies with human rights obligations.128
76. JS18 stated that the planned eviction of pastoralists from at least 25 villages in Ngorongoro Division had undermined statutory and customary land tenure, lacked meaningful consultation, and violated the requirement of free, prior and informed consent. The expansion of the Ruaha National Park under the REGROW project led to the displacement of thousands of people, often without adequate consultation, compensation, or resettlement planning.129
77. JS7 stated that in 2022, and without consultation with indigenous groups, 1,500 sq. kms of land in Liliondo, Ngorongoro District had been designated as a game reserve, the Pololeti Game Reserve, and that the primarily pastoral Maasai residents had been prevented from living on, grazing cattle on, or seeking water in this demarcated area.130
78. JS22 stated that the cumulative effect of the recommendations of the two Presidential Commissions launched in 2025 to investigate land issues (land commission) and to assess the relocation process (relocation commission) in the Ngorongoro Conservation Area could result in targeted displacement of a single ethnic community, and their findings had failed to acknowledge documented contributions of Maasai rotational grazing, and seasonal land management, among others. It called for a pause in the implementation of the recommendations until an independent review is conducted in line with international human rights standards.131

Migrants, refugees and asylum-seekers
81. SU highlighted concerns about respect for the principle of non-refoulment, delays in processing asylum applications, and strict camp policies that limited freedom of movement of refugees. 136 ERF noted that the Tanzanian National Commission for Asylum Decisions had not convened since 2022, thereby hindering the asylum status determination process.137
Stateless persons
82. JS14 welcomed the provision of free birth registration and the progress that had been made in decentralizing the system but noted that children not born in health facilities faced barriers to birth registration.138
