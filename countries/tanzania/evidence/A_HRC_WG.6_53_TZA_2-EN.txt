I. Background
1. The present report was prepared pursuant to Human Rights Council resolutions 5/1 and 16/21, taking into consideration the outcome of the previous review.1 It is a compilation of information contained in relevant United Nations documents, presented in a summarized manner owing to word-limit constraints.
II. Scope of international obligations and cooperation with human rights mechanisms
2. The United Nations country team noted that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported recommendations to cooperate with the special procedures of the Human Rights Council and to engage with the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR) for technical assistance. The United Nations country team considered that the United Republic of Tanzania had not implemented those recommendations and expressed concern that the State had not issued a standing invitation to the special procedures or granted the requests for visits from the Special Rapporteur on the right of everyone to the enjoyment of the highest attainable standard of physical and mental health, the Special Rapporteur on the promotion and protection of the right to freedom of opinion and expression or the Special Rapporteur in the field of cultural rights.2
III. National human rights framework
1. Constitutional and legislative framework
3. The United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) noted that, while the Constitution of the United Republic of Tanzania of 1977, as amended in 2005, recognized the right to education and training, the Constitution of Zanzibar of 1984 did not enshrine the right to education. UNESCO recommended enshrining the right to education in the Constitution of Zanzibar.3 4. The United Nations country team noted that there had been demands for constitutional reform within the framework of the agenda for reform, reconciliation, rebuilding and reconstruction. It also noted that the Commission of Inquiry into the violence and breaches

2. Institutional infrastructure and policy measures
5. The United Nations country team recalled that, while the United Republic of Tanzania had supported a recommendation made during its first universal periodic review, in 2011, to strengthen the capacity of the Commission for Human Rights and Good Governance, that recommendation had not been implemented. The country team recommended that the United Republic of Tanzania amend the Constitution and the Commission for Human Rights and Good Governance Act to strengthen the Commission’s compliance with the principles relating to the status of national institutions for the promotion and protection of human rights (the Paris Principles).5
6. The United Nations country team noted that the United Republic of Tanzania had established a ministry of youth and recommended that it strengthen youth agencies with technical assistance from the United Nations.6
7. The United Nations country team noted that at the previous review, in 2021, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported a recommendation to tackle corruption. The country team considered that recommendation to have been partially implemented, highlighting the establishment of the Prevention and Combating of Corruption Bureau. It recommended that the State increase the Bureau’s budget and strengthen anti-corruption measures, transparency and whistle-blower protection.7

IV. Promotion and protection of human rights

A. Implementation of international human rights obligations, taking into account applicable international humanitarian law
1. Equality and non-discrimination
8. The United Nations country team noted that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported broad recommendations relating to non-discrimination and gender equality, 8 but had not supported specific recommendations relating to the prevention of discrimination against all vulnerable groups, including all persons living with HIV and LGBTQI persons.9 It recommended that the State: (a) adopt legal frameworks that provided for gender parity and that enhanced equal representation and participation of women in leadership and decision-making at all levels;10 and (b) amend relevant provisions of the Penal Code and the Zanzibar Penal Act (No. 6 of 2018) with a view to decriminalizing consensual same-sex relations and ensuring non-discriminatory protection of sexual minorities.11
2. Right to life, liberty and security of person, and freedom from torture
9. The United Nations country team recalled that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported one part of a recommendation – to strengthen awareness-raising campaigns on the death penalty and to hold public debates on the death penalty, including in the parliament – and expressed concern about the lack of implementation of those commitments. The State had also committed to strengthen the fight against torture and the country team considered those recommendations to have not been implemented.12 Also, the State had committed to strengthening the fight against torture and the country team considered that recommendation to have not been implemented.13
10. In their communication of 1 December 2025 to the United Republic of Tanzania, several special procedure mechanisms expressed deep concern about reported widespread violence against citizens and human rights violations, which conveyed a disturbing picture of the state of democracy and enjoyment of human rights and fundamental freedoms. They

drew the State’s attention to information that they had received concerning allegations of the use of excessive and lethal force by security forces leading to widespread extrajudicial killings and injuries of protesters, as well as the arbitrary arrests and detentions and enforced disappearances of protesters and civil society activists in cities and towns across the country in the aftermath of the general elections held on 29 October 2025. They also expressed alarm at reports of reprisals against citizens who had spoken up or documented the violations, including human rights defenders, which seemed to be part of a very concerning effort to cover up potential crimes and serious human rights violations.14
11. In its reply of 8 December 2025, the United Republic of Tanzania stated that it did not condone extrajudicial killings, enforced disappearances or arbitrary detentions under any circumstances and that allegations of that nature were treated with utmost seriousness. It rejected the allegations of widespread arbitrary arrests and detentions. It stated that on 29 October 2025, the country had experienced unprecedented organized violence, particularly in Dar es Salaam. Coordinated acts of violence and destruction had been deliberately designed to destabilize the State and had posed a serious threat to public order and the safety of citizens. When law enforcement officers had intervened to restore public order, they had been confronted with organized, violent and dangerous resistance, which had necessitated the use of force to contain the situation and prevent further harm to lives and property. An independent commission of inquiry had been established, which demonstrated the State’s commitment to transparency, accountability and the rule of law in the electoral process.15 Noting the establishment of the commission of inquiry, the special procedure mechanisms recommended that the commission also advance on proposals to ensure access to accountability, justice and reparations, including guarantees of non-recurrence, and offered their technical support in that regard.16
12. The United Nations country team recalled that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported recommendations to combat violence and other abuses of the human rights of persons with albinism. It noted the adoption of the national strategy on the rights of persons with albinism 2024/25–2028/29 and the National Assistive Technology Strategy 2024–2027. It recommended that the State: (a) introduce measures to strengthen the effective implementation of the national strategy to ensure stronger protection for persons with albinism from violence, attacks and abuse; and (b) fully implement the judgment of the African Court on Human and Peoples’ Rights in the case of the Centre for Human Rights and Others v. United Republic of Tanzania (Application No. 019/2018).17
3. Human rights and counter-terrorism
13. In their communication of 1 December 2025, the special procedure mechanisms observed that, according to information received, some opposition leaders had been detained and were facing terrorism-related charges. They recalled that States must ensure that their counter-terrorism legislation complied with international law and the principles of legality, necessity, proportionality and non-discrimination. They underscored that counter-terrorism charges should never be abused to suppress legitimate exercise of the rights to freedom of expression, association, peaceful assembly or political participation.18
14. In its reply of 8 December 2025, the United Republic of Tanzania stated that the cases of those who remained in lawful custody would proceed strictly in accordance with due process and the protections guaranteed by law.19
4. Administration of justice, including impunity, and the rule of law
15. The United Nations country team expressed concern about the lack of judicial independence and recommended that the State undertake comprehensive constitutional and legislative reforms to strengthen judicial independence for the effective administration of justice.20
16. The United Nations country team also expressed concern about the arrests of an opposition leader during a public rally and of several young people in the aftermath of the October 2025 elections. It recommended that the authorities ensure that all such arrests complied with human rights standards, include due process and fair trial guarantees.21

5. Fundamental freedoms and the right to participate in public and political life
17. The United Nations country team recalled that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported recommendations relating to the freedoms of expression, association and assembly. The country team considered those recommendations not to have been implemented and recommended strengthening legal safeguards for the protection of fundamental freedoms and ensuring accountability where such freedoms had been violated.22
18. UNESCO noted concerns about the potential of the Cybercrimes Act of 2015 to restrict legitimate journalistic work and public interest reporting. It also noted challenges in the implementation of the Access to Information Act, 2016, which provided for public access to information held by public authorities. UNESCO recommended establishing an independent oversight institution with the capacity to implement that Act.23
19. In their communication of 1 December 2025, special procedure mechanisms expressed serious concern at the restrictions imposed on media freedom, freedom of expression and access to information, including the reported Internet shutdown across the country in the aftermath of the elections in 2025. Such shutdowns were incompatible with international law and in most cases, shutdown orders lacked a legal basis. Where a legal basis had existed, shutdown orders had been coupled with broad and vague provisions and lacked adequate independent oversight. While those measures were typically justified on the grounds of national security and public order, as an indiscriminate blanket measure, they were a disproportionate – and generally ineffective – means of achieving any potential legitimate security aims.24
20. In its reply of 8 December 2025, the United Republic of Tanzania stated that the Internet disruption following the events of 29 October 2025 had been a temporary measure, taken in accordance with article 19 (3) (a) and (b) of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, solely to safeguard public safety and protect the rights of others in the face of credible threats of violence and coordinated harmful actions. That intervention had sought to prevent the dissemination of coordinated dangerous hate speech and misinformation that could have exacerbated tensions or jeopardized lives.25
21. The United Nations country team noted that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported a recommendation to embark on reforms to ensure free and fair elections, including implementation of the recommendations issued in the final report of the European Union election observation mission to the United Republic of Tanzania of 2015. While noting the progress made in that regard, the country team expressed concern about the independence and impartiality of the Independent National Electoral Commission and limitations on judicial oversight of the presidential election results.26
22. The United Nations country team recommended that the United Republic of Tanzania undertake comprehensive electoral and constitutional reforms to ensure electoral independence, transparency, judicial oversight and strengthened protection of civic space, as well as inclusive participation of all groups in public affairs without discrimination. It also recommended amending the Constitution to provide safeguards for judicial review of presidential elections.27
23. In their communication of 1 December 2025, the special procedure mechanisms noted that in 2024, amendments had been made to the Independent National Commission Act, the Presidential and Councillors Elections Act and the Political Parties Affairs (Amendment) Act, which had raised serious questions regarding the independence of key institutions, notably regarding the appointment and removal of members of the electoral commission and about the commission’s funding. Those changes, together with newly introduced restrictions, had reportedly resulted in the disqualification of opposition candidates during the nomination process and had undermined the fairness and inclusivity of the electoral framework prior to the elections.28
24. In its reply of 8 December 2025, the United Republic of Tanzania stated that the amendments to those Acts had enhanced electoral fairness and transparency and had been the result of a consultative process involving political parties, the media, religious groups, civil society and a special taskforce formed to assess challenges to multiparty democracy.29

6. Prohibition of all forms of slavery, including trafficking in persons
25. The United Nations country team noted that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported recommendations on combating trafficking in persons. While welcoming the enactment of the Anti-Trafficking in Persons Act, 2008, and the adoption of the National Anti-Trafficking in Persons Action Plan 2021–2024, the country team considered that those recommendations had only been partially implemented. It remained concerned about limited prosecutions, weak data systems and the absence of provisions explicitly prohibiting trafficking in body parts.30
7. Right to work and to just and favourable conditions of work
26. UNESCO noted that in the mainland of the United Republic of Tanzania, the minimum age of employment was set at 14 years old, pursuant to the Law of the Child Act of 2009 and the Employment and Labour Relations Act of 2004. In Zanzibar, the Children’s Act, 2011, prescribed 15 years as the minimum age of employment, while section 3 of the Employment Act, 2005, provided that a child could not work and defined a child as a person under 17 years of age. There were therefore inconsistencies within the legal framework. UNESCO recommended raising the minimum age of employment in accordance with international human rights law.31
8. Right to social security
27. The United Nations country team noted that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported recommendations on ensuring social protection. While highlighting the progress made in supporting beneficiaries through the Productive Social Safety Net programme, the country team expressed concern that the existing social protection programmes did not adequately cover all vulnerable groups, including domestic workers, persons with leprosy and persons with albinism. It recommended that the State establish an expanded, inclusive social protection programme.32
9. Right to an adequate standard of living
28. The United Nations country team noted that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported recommendations to reduce poverty. It considered those recommendations to have been partially implemented. Noting that the country had continued to face significant poverty and inequality-related challenges, it recommended scaling up inclusive social protection, strengthening farmers’ livelihoods and improving gender-disaggregated poverty data to ensure equitable, evidence-based affirmative action programmes.33
29. The United Nations country team considered that the recommendations made and supported at the previous review relating to access to adequate housing, safe drinking water and sanitation had been partially implemented. While noting the progress made in expanding water infrastructure, the country team also highlighted disparities in access to water in rural and informal settlements. It recommended that the United Republic of Tanzania accelerate equitable investment in water, sanitation and housing, prioritizing underserved communities.34
10. Right to health
30. The United Nations country team noted that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported recommendations to expand equitable access to quality health services, strengthen maternal health services, advance universal health coverage and enhance HIV prevention. The country team considered that those recommendations had been partially implemented and noted the progress made in relation to expanded primary healthcare, improved maternal and HIV services and strengthened epidemic preparedness. It recommended that the State accelerate equitable access to healthcare, particularly in underserved areas, strengthen the workforce in the health sector and increase funding to address the needs of persons with disabilities, persons with albinism and persons with leprosy.35

31. The United Nations country team considered that the United Republic of Tanzania had fully implemented the recommendation to strengthen measures to safeguard the health rights of women, particularly on issues relating to prenatal and postnatal care, and noted the strengthening of health policies, the implementation of the relevant action plan and the reduction in maternal mortality. However, it noted gaps in the provision of access to sexual and reproductive health services in rural areas and in workforce capacity, and limited access to information on sexual and reproductive health rights for persons with disabilities and persons with albinism. It recommended that the United Republic of Tanzania increase financing for the health sector, strengthen primary healthcare services and the capacity of the healthcare workforce, expand equitable youth-friendly sexual and reproductive health services and enhance accountability mechanisms.36
32. The United Nations country team recommended that the United Republic of Tanzania expand youth-focused HIV prevention, noting that at the previous review, the State had supported a recommendation to strengthen HIV-prevention programmes aimed at young people.37
33. The United Nations country team considered that the United Republic of Tanzania had partially implemented a recommendation from the previous review that it had supported relating to the rights of persons with leprosy, including timely and adequate access to health services, while expressing concern about continued late diagnosis of leprosy and the continued stigma associated with the disease.38
11. Right to education
34. The United Nations country team noted that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported recommendations to strengthen inclusive education, expand information and communications technology (ICT) infrastructure, improve school infrastructure, increase school enrolment, ensure re-entry into the education system for pregnant girls and young mothers, and enhance access to education for children with disabilities and children in rural areas. The country team considered that the State had partially implemented those recommendations, noting that although progress had been made through policy reforms providing for the re-entry into the education system of teenage mothers, challenges persisted, including owing to infrastructure deficits, quality gaps, limited access to assistive devices and disparities in ICT infrastructure.39
35. UNESCO noted that the right to education without discrimination was guaranteed in the mainland of the United Republic of Tanzania by the Law of the Child Act of 2009 and in Zanzibar by the Children’s Act of 2011. While every child aged 5 and over was eligible for enrolment in pre-primary education for a period of two years, neither the Education Act of 1978, which was enforceable in the mainland, nor the Education Act of 1982, which was enforceable in Zanzibar, provided for free and compulsory pre-primary education. UNESCO recommended that the United Republic of Tanzania enact legislation to make pre-primary education free and compulsory for at least one year and to guarantee at least 12 years of free primary and secondary education, of which at least 9 were compulsory.40
36. UNESCO stated that, since the previous review, the enrolment rates at all levels of education, except at tertiary level, had decreased. At the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported recommendations to increase enrolment in schools.41
37. UNESCO noted that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported recommendations to enhance access to quality education, including in rural areas. In that regard, it highlighted the fact that the Education Sector Development Plan 2025/26–2029/30 focused on expanding access to education, including by addressing inequities in education and training opportunities, enhancing the quality of education and ensuring that education and training were responsive to students’ social and economic needs, as well as national development priorities.42
38. Referring to recommendations on ensuring inclusive education, especially for persons with disabilities and vulnerable groups, that the United Republic of Tanzania had supported at the previous review, UNESCO noted that: (a) inclusive education was among the core policy priorities in the Education Sector Development Plan, which included specific strategies for accessible infrastructure, teacher capacity-building and data disaggregation to

12. Development, the environment, and business and human rights
41. The United Nations country team noted that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported recommendations to implement the Tanzania Development Vision 2025, Agenda 2063 of the African Union and the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development. The country team considered that it had partially implemented those recommendations.46
42. The United Nations country team noted that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported a recommendation to promote the participation of women, children, persons with disabilities and local communities in climate and disaster risk frameworks. The country team considered that it had fully implemented that recommendation. Nevertheless, as sea-level rise and coastal erosion continued to threaten housing, infrastructure and livelihoods, the country team recommended integrating human rights safeguards in the climate frameworks, scaling up gender- and disability-responsive financing to improve adaptation and resilience and strengthening data systems and evaluation of climate-related disasters on human rights through human rights impact assessments.47
43. The United Nations country team noted the ongoing development of a national action plan on business and human rights. It recommended that the United Republic of Tanzania finalize and operationalize that plan and put in place an accountability mechanism to ensure access to remedies for third parties.48
B. Rights of specific persons or groups
1. Women
44. The United Nations country team noted that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported recommendations to combat discrimination and violence against women and girls and to strengthen safeguards for the protection of women’s rights. The country team considered that it had partially implemented those recommendations.49
45. The United Nations country team commended the United Republic of Tanzania for having implemented gender policies and expanded gender-based violence services. However, the country team considered that it had partially implemented the recommendation it had supported at the previous review to strengthen planning, budgeting and institutional mechanisms to ensure effective implementation of the National Plan of Action to End Violence against Women and Children in Tanzania 2017/18–2021/22. The country team recommended that the State: (a) strengthen gender-responsive data systems to improve

The United Nations country team noted the steps that the United Republic of Tanzania had taken to ensure that its National Land Policy (2023) included gender-equitable land rights and supported women’s access to finance and tenure documents, such as certificates of customary right of occupancy. 2. Children
47. The United Nations country team considered that the United Republic of Tanzania had not implemented the recommendations it had supported at its previous review on amending the Law of Marriage Act (1971) to raise the minimum age of marriage for girls to 18 years and to eliminate child marriage. The country team recommended raising the age of marriage to 18 years.52
48. The United Nations country team was concerned that the implementation of the Convention on the Rights of the Child in the United Republic of Tanzania was constrained by limited human and financial resources. The country team recommended allocating increased resources to strengthen child protection systems and ensure effective implementation of children’s rights.53
49. UNESCO highlighted the fact that, in the mainland of the United Republic of Tanzania, the Education Act of 1978 authorized the Minister of Education to regulate the administration of corporal punishment in schools and the National Education (Corporal Punishment) Regulations of 1979 permitted corporal punishment for serious breaches of discipline, subject to conditions and limits. UNESCO noted that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had received a recommendation to abolish corporal punishment in schools. UNESCO recommended legally proscribing corporal punishment in education settings.54
3. Older persons
50. The United Nations country team noted progress in the promotion and protection of the rights of older persons, including through the implementation of the Older Persons Affairs Act in Zanzibar and the National Ageing Policy, as well as the inclusion of older persons in the Tanzania Social Action Fund. The country team considered that the United Republic of Tanzania had implemented the relevant recommendation it had supported at the previous review and recommended updating the National Ageing Policy.55
4. Persons with disabilities
51. The United Nations country team noted that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported a recommendation to implement national policies and programmes aimed at empowering persons with disabilities. The country team considered that the United Republic of Tanzania had partially implemented that recommendation. It recommended that the State increase financial investments to strengthen disability data systems, the management information system for persons with disabilities and the system for the registration of persons with disabilities.56
5. Indigenous Peoples and minorities
52. On 25 June 2025, several special procedure mechanisms brought to the attention of the United Republic of Tanzania information on alleged violations of the rights of Indigenous Peoples and of other affected communities near or inside Ruaha National Park in the Great Ruaha River Basin. These included threats of forced eviction, involuntary and inadequate resettlement, prohibition of access to farm and grazing lands, cattle seizure, excessive use of force, enforced disappearances and extrajudicial killings by the national parks authorities. While the mechanisms did not wish to prejudice the accuracy of those alleged violations, they expressed concern that: (a) the extension of the boundaries of Ruaha National Park had

been adopted without the free, prior and informed consent of Indigenous Peoples and without good faith consultation with other affected communities in the Great Ruaha River Basin, putting them at risk of forced evictions; and (b) despite assurances given to the World Bank that announced plans to enforce the boundaries would not be implemented in the foreseeable future, affected communities had not been given any guarantee in that regard, leaving them at risk of forced eviction and inadequate resettlement. The mechanisms stated that, if relocation were to take place without the free, prior and informed consent of the Indigenous Peoples affected, it would amount to forced eviction.57
53. In its reply of 9 September 2025, the United Republic of Tanzania stated that the interventions under the Tanzania: Resilient Natural Resource Management for Tourism and Growth Project were development-orientated, focusing on tourism infrastructure, improved visitor facilities and livelihood support for surrounding communities; they did not involve altering or expanding the boundaries of Ruaha National Park. The authorities categorically refuted the allegations of forced evictions, torture, extrajudicial killings, enforced disappearances and excessive use of force and disputed the allegations that communities had been denied access to land, grazing areas or fishing rights within the vicinity of Ruaha National Park, indicating that villagers residing within the Park’s gazetted boundaries continued to pursue their livelihoods without interference. The authorities firmly refuted the allegations that communities surrounding Ruaha National Park, including the Maasai, Sukuma and Datoda, had been excluded from consultation or that their consent had been disregarded, and indicated that extensive engagement with local communities had been a consistent feature of the Project.58
54. In the same reply, the United Republic of Tanzania stated that no special group or community in the country was recognized as indigenous and that all Tanzanians were equal and enjoyed equal rights as prescribed in the Constitution of the United Republic of Tanzania. Consequently, any suggestion that consultation obligations specific to Indigenous Peoples had been ignored was therefore legally and factually unfounded.59
6. Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender and intersex persons
55. The United Nations country team expressed concern about the criminalization of consensual same-sex relations and the continued subjection of LGBTIQ+ persons to multiple forms of violations, including sexual violence, police harassment and forced HIV testing.60
7. Migrants, refugees and asylum-seekers
56. The United Nations country team, referring to recommendations concerning the rights of migrants, refugees and asylum-seekers that the United Republic of Tanzania had supported at the previous review, noted that the State had an open-door policy for refugees, and continued to receive new arrivals and to repatriate Burundian refugees. The country team recommended that the State comply with the principle of non-refoulement in the implementation of the tripartite agreement on the repatriation of Burundian refugees and ensure that refugees and asylum-seekers had access to protection services, legal status, and return in safety and dignity.61
8. Stateless persons
57. The United Nations country team noted that at the previous review, the United Republic of Tanzania had supported a recommendation to increase access to birth registration for all children born in the country, including children of migrants and refugees, thus avoiding statelessness. The country team considered that the United Republic of Tanzania had fully implemented the recommendation, noting relevant legal reforms and the nationwide roll-out of a simplified registration process for children under 5.62
