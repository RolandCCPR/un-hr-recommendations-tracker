 |  Greece, as an EU front line state, has continued to face considerable challenges in managing irregular migratory flows. Despite the gravity of these challenges, significant progress has been achieved during the last years, allowing the authorities to ensure a higher level of protection of the rights of migrants and refugees.  More specifically, in the field of reception and asylum, Greece has established an up-to-date Reception System and one of Europe's most efficient Asylum Services.  In 2022, Law 4939/2022 entered into force, consolidating provisions on reception, international protection, and temporary protection. Its goal is to systematize all legislation related to migration, reception conditions, and asylum procedures into a single legal framework. This codification enhances the protection of individual/applicants - third-country citizens’ rights, streamlines administrative processes, and upholds the principles of legality and the rule of law  Any Third Country National (TCΝ) or stateless person who arrives or is within the Greek territory has the right to apply for international protection. The Greek Asylum Service is responsible for the examination of the application and makes a decision in accordance with European and Greek legislation. Each asylum application undergoes an individual and fair assessment to accurately identify the applicant’s need for international protection   The Greek Asylum Service has expanded its capacity, with 26 offices nationwide. This expansion includes offices on migration front-line islands and two specialized offices in Athens and Thessaloniki, equipped with highly skilled staff and competent to manage and examine international protection applications of vulnerable people, e.g. unaccompanied children, disabled persons, elderly, pregnant women, single parents with minor children, victims of human trafficking, etc.  Staffing levels have been increased, and the backlog of asylum applications has been substantially reduced. Furthermore, stricter application deadlines, and digitization have significantly cut processing times.  The Reception and Identification Service (RIS) supervises and coordinates the operation of (a) registration facilities, to swiftly implement the reception and identification process for third country nationals and stateless persons who enter Greece without the necessary legal formalities, as well as register applications for asylum, and (b) accommodation facilities, for the temporary accommodation of asylum seekers.  Dignified living conditions and safety are provided in both types of RIS Facilities, while safeguards have also been put in place for the timely identification and care of persons with special reception needs, including children (both unaccompanied and accompanied), single parent families, elderly persons, persons with disabilities and serious diseases, victims of torture, trafficking, and various types of violence.   Systematic training on various issues (including identification and management of vulnerable persons, registration, etc.) is offered to staff, alongside awareness-raising activities for RIS Facilities residents.    Furthermore, Greece has built an entire system of protection for unaccompanied minors with the National Emergency Response Mechanism being its prominent part and supporting thousands of children in precarious conditions since 2021. In addition, the new National Guardianship System took effect in 2024, while appropriate accommodation is offered to all children.   The social integration measures have been strengthened and the main integration project “HELIOS” facilitates, inter alia, access of refugees to the labour market, while various targeted interventions are in place, including cultural mediation.   The Hellenic Police and the Hellenic Coast Guard conduct (land and sea) border surveillance duties with full respect for the fundamental rights of every third country national crossing illegally the borders.  The competent border surveillance teams implementing patrolling activities within the Hellenic territory conduct national and EU Joint Border Operations, in cooperation with the European Border and Coast Guard Agency (Frontex). Both national authorities and Frontex follow a strict Code of Conduct, and every accusation of ill-treatment, including alleged pushbacks, on behalf of the deployed personnel, taking place during these activities, is investigated at internal and/or EU level.   The protection of human life at sea and the provision of assistance to any person in distress, without any discrimination, constitutes the highest professional and moral obligation of the Hellenic Coast Guard. The action taken by the Hellenic authorities at the sea borders, are carried out in full compliance with the country’s international obligations according to the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, the International Convention for Safety of Life at Sea and the International Convention on Maritime Search and Rescue. Hundreds of thousands of migrants have been rescued by the Hellenic Coast Guard (HCG) throughout the migration/refugee crisis and its efforts to assist individuals and ships in distress have been repeatedly recognized internationally.  In any case where HCG staff members identify vulnerable groups such as children, pregnant women, elderly or sick individuals or people who have suffered psychological and physical violence, they ensure that they are immediately provided with the appropriate medical care. Especially in the case of identified minors, the juvenile prosecutor is immediately informed by the competent port authority, who gives specific orders for their management.  Furthermore, Hellenic Police personnel receive numerous initial and refresher training sessions on fundamental rights when conducting detection, interception, and first reception activities in support of the respective authorities of the Ministry of Migration and Asylum. These training sessions are organized either exclusively by the Hellenic Police or in cooperation with relevant stakeholders, such as Frontex, UNHCR, and others. These training activities focus on the protection of fundamental rights at the borders, with particular emphasis on vulnerable persons, especially women and children newly arriving at the borders.   The training of personnel is a major pillar for the Hellenic Coast Guard in strengthening its capacity to fulfill its mandate in accordance with the highest ethical standards. The integration of fundamental rights into the curriculum of cadet academies, along with continuous follow-up and advanced education programs, ensures that all staff are well-informed and sensitized to this paramount issue. Within this framework, a Memorandum of Understanding is in place with UNHCR Greece, outlining various synergies between the two parties, including cooperation in training programs. Allegations on so-called “push-backs” are not compatible with the well-established standard operating procedures of the Hellenic Authorities. Greece has put in place a three-tier system to address complaints about allegations of violations of fundamental rights at the borders. More specifically, at administrative level, as soon as any information regarding such allegations or complaint reaches the competent Authorities, a sworn administrative inquiry is ordered without delay. At the level of independent authorities, the National Transparency Authority is the competent entity to investigate allegations of incidents involving human rights violations at the borders. In parallel, the Greek Ombudsperson has also reviewed cases related to border protection and fundamental rights, while simultaneously serving as the responsible independent authority for monitoring return operations and allegations of ill-treatment committed by law enforcement personnel. Finally, such claims may be submitted to the competent judicial authorities.  Recently, monitoring of compliance with fundamental rights has further been strengthened. Articles 49 and 50 of law 4960/2022 provide for the establishment within the Ministry of Migration and Asylum of a) the post of Fundamental Rights Officer (FRO), responsible for the collection and preliminary evaluation of complaints regarding alleged fundamental rights violations during the reception or asylum procedures and b) a Special Fundamental Rights Compliance Committee, with the mandate to monitor the relevant procedures and the implementation of the national, EU and international legislation in the fields of border protection and granting of international protection. The establishment of the FRO demonstrates Greece's commitment to respecting and promoting fundamental rights; it should be noted that Greece is the only state in the European Union that has established such institution at the  national level.  In this framework, a Complaints Mechanism has been operating at the national level since the end of September 2023. It is intended for third-country nationals who believe that one or more of their fundamental rights have been violated during entry into the country, reception, or international protection procedures. Third-country nationals can submit a written complaint using the standardized complaint forms available on websites of the Ministry of Migration and Asylum, the Ministry of Citizen Protection, and the Ministry of Maritime Affairs and Insular Policy.  Additionally, the FRO is conducting several field visits to islands and mainland regions in Greece, to liaise with relevant stakeholders, monitor compliance with fundamental rights and provide updates, advice, and assistance. During these meetings, the FRO meets with police and port authorities and is granted access to all areas of the Facilities, including UAM (unaccompanied minors) safe zones, medical units, and hygiene facilities. These visits provide the FRO with a comprehensive understanding of the situation, challenges, and needs, enabling him to assess the compliance of policies with fundamental rights. They also serve to present the FRO's mandate, explain the Complaints Mechanism, and collect feedback to improve effectiveness.  Finally, another essential aspect of the FRO's work involves conducting regular training sessions on fundamental rights protection for Hellenic Police and Hellenic Coast Guard officers, as well as other frontline personnel in border areas. To date, the FRO has conducted numerous training sessions for police officers and other border management staff in various border regions  Finally, the Hellenic Police and the Hellenic Coast-Guard, in cooperation with Frontex, implement important follow-up and monitoring mechanisms, foreseen at the EBCG (EU) 2019/1896 Regulation, in order to safeguard the protection of fundamental rights during the implementation of Joint Operations, at the borders.   It is important to clarify that there are two types of RIS Facilities: a) registration facilities, where TCNs who enter Greece without the necessary legal formalities undergo reception and identification processes (including registration of personal data, fingerprinting, and medical and psychosocial screening), as well as have their application for asylum registered. Those include Reception and Identification Centers (RICs) that operate in the mainland and Closed Controlled Access Centers (CCACs) that operate in five east Aegean islands. CCACs may also include predeparture facilities, which are, nevertheless, under the authority of the police; and b) accommodation Facilities (called Controlled Access Facilities for the Temporary Accommodation of Asylum Seekers) that operate in various location in the mainland and provide temporary accommodation and other services to asylum-seekers.   The Reception and Identification Service ensures that third-country nationals, throughout the reception procedure, are provided with dignified living conditions, maintain family unity, have access to healthcare, medical treatment and psychosocial support, and receive appropriate treatment if they belong to vulnerable groups, particularly unaccompanied minors, victims of trafficking and gender-based violence and persons with disabilities. They are also entitled to legal guidance/advice, to maintain contact with civil society organizations working in the field of migration and human rights, and to communicate with their relatives and close family members.  In all RICs and CCACs the resident population is organized into distinct areas with separate accommodation areas for vulnerable groups (such as unaccompanied minors and single-parent families), while access is provided to shared laundry facilities, kitchens, sport facilities, recreation areas etc.  Designated spaces for unaccompanied minors within CCACs and RICs, known as Safe Areas, are supported by child protection personnel from NGOs and the International Organization for Migration (IOM), funded by partner country donations and national resources. Their purpose is to prevent unaccompanied minors from mingling with the general population and to ensure they receive the enhanced protection and 24/7 care they need, delivered in three shifts. .  Care is provided by a multidisciplinary team and includes psychosocial and legal support, interpretation services, individual needs and best interests assessments, and informative sessions to help children understand their legal status, rights, and responsibilities. Recreational activities are also offered. Children receive essential supplies, including non-food items, and support in accessing basic services. Currently, there are eight operational Safe Areas across Greece: Lesvos, Samos, Chios, Kos, Leros, Evros – Fylakio, Malakasa, and Diavata.  Primary health care in RIS Facilities is provided by Medical and Psychosocial Support Units. Since July 2024, the “Hippocrates” program—implemented by IOM and partners—has carried out medical and psychosocial screening as part of the reception and identification process. It addresses immediate medical and psychological needs, identifies persons belonging to vulnerable groups requiring specialized care, and provides first-level medical and psychosocial support to residents.  Facility managers, based on recommendations from support staff, refer vulnerable individuals to public healthcare units or social protection services. Rapid assessments are also conducted to detect serious vulnerabilities or protection needs, allowing for appropriate follow-up and ensuring continuity of care. Identified vulnerabilities lead to the immediate application of special reception conditions.  To improve living conditions, RIS has contracted private companies for cleaning, security, and maintenance services across its Facilities.  The Reception and Identification Service has issued Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs), concerning Identification and Response to cases of Human Trafficking within facilities accommodating asylum applicants. These SOPs outline procedures for the timely identification of potential victims and the provision of appropriate support and information.  Since October 2022, case managers, deployed by the European Union Agency for Asylum (EUAA), conduct an initial rapid assessment upon the arrival of individuals at the facilities, in order to identify any vulnerabilities or circumstances requiring immediate intervention, appointing focal persons for a) child protection, b) gender-based violence, victims of trafficking and victims of torture, and c) other vulnerabilities. In accordance with the established guidelines, individuals identified as potential victims of trafficking are referred to the National Referral Mechanism (NRM) for the Protection of Victims of Human Trafficking, operated by the National Center for Social Solidarity (EKKA). They are duly informed of their rights, including the right to cooperate with prosecuting authorities and to be formally recognized as victims of trafficking under applicable legislation. Where required, referrals are also made to specialized actors to ensure that individual reception needs are appropriately addressed. All cases are subject to regular follow-up by the case managers to ensure continued support and protection.  Finally, there are seven pre-removal centers for third-country nationals, which are under the management and supervision of the Hellenic Police. A series of actions related to the renovation, reconstruction and maintenance of these Centers are taken to ensure decent living conditions.   During the COVID-19 pandemic, protective equipment, including masks, gloves and antiseptic products, were distributed to the residents of facilities for migrants, and common areas were thoroughly cleaned and disinfected daily. A temporary social security number was assigned to migrants in an irregular situation to ensure that vaccinations were administered free of charge to all, regardless of their legal status.    NGOs are recognized as invaluable partners, collaborating with competent authorities on a wide range of migration-related issues and contributing their expertise and significant added value.   Cooperation with NGOs covers areas such as the provision of appropriate interpretation in the asylum procedure, the protection of children and, in particular, unaccompanied minors, housing of applicants and beneficiaries of international protection, integration of migrants in collaboration with local communities and communities of migrants. Focal points have been appointed across various services to enhance coordination with CSOs, while a series of meetings between representatives of the Ministry of Migration and Asylum and Civil Society Organizations (CSOs) have been organized, at the initiative of the Ministry, in the framework of a structured and regular dialogue with CSOs.  The registration of NGOs, and their members, active in the field of migration is important to ensure transparency in their activities within Greece and safeguard beneficiaries, many of whom belonging to vulnerable groups. The Registry of NGOs fully respects freedom of association without imposing excessive or burdensome requirements. The submission of the documents required and the registration itself are free. The authorities evaluate the existing legal framework, and the competent NGO Registry Unit of the General Secretariat for the Reception of Asylum Seekers has already elaborated proposals that aim to further reduce bureaucratic burden, improve efficiency, and revise the legal framework based on experience, input from the European Commission, and recommendations by the National Transparency Authority. As a result of a recent amendment, CSOs no longer need to renew their registration every three years; instead, they remain registered until a revocation decision is issued.  Human rights defenders, including those working on migration issues, are fully protected; they are not sanctioned for their lawful activities and are subject only to the general legislation in force and to the country’s independent courts. With regard to access by visitors to the RICs and CCACs, it should be noted that specific provisions are included in the General Regulations governing the operation of these facilities. In general, for the entry of visitors, professionals, and volunteers into official accommodation sites — with the exception of staff from organizations that cooperate with the Ministry and are registered in the Registry of Greek and Foreign NGOs authorized to provide services at these facilities — an application must be submitted, which is then subject to approval by the Reception and Identification Service following consultation with the facility’s Director. Lawyers and legal advisors are granted access to all centers without the above formalities. They are only required to notify the Director of the Facility and show their lawyer’s Identification Card and the relevant authorization; no further approval is necessary. The access of lawyers to the sites cannot be hindered and may only be limited for reasons related to public order. In all cases, third country nationals are not deprived of their right to legal representation and support  Furthermore, article 40 of Law 4825/2021 establishes a clear regulatory framework for the participation of NGOs and civil society in maritime search and rescue (SAR) operations under the responsibility of the Hellenic Coast Guard. It ensures that private actors operate within a coordinated system to enhance rescue efforts while avoiding interference with official operations. The law mandates registration, compliance with international standards, and imposes sanctions for non-compliance. Its primary goal is to protect the safety of individuals in distress and ensure SAR operations are conducted efficiently and professionally, while respecting human rights and maritime safety standards.  This law is not intended to discourage private support; on the contrary, it aims to maximize the effectiveness of such participation within a framework of legal clarity, ensuring that migrants in distress receive the highest standard of assistance       The Voluntary Relocation Program for Unaccompanied Minors from Greece began in May 2020, overseen by the Special Secretariat for the Protection of Unaccompanied Minors (now the General Secretariat for Vulnerable Persons and Institutional Protection), with support from the European Commission, EU Member States and other countries, as well as the Asylum Service, NGOs, the EUAA, IOM, and UNHCR.  The program aimed to voluntarily relocate unaccompanied minors from Greece to other countries, based on terms agreed on May 11, 2020. It concluded on March 31, 2023, having relocated 1,368 minors of various nationalities, including from Afghanistan and Syria, to 12 Member States.  Its contribution was vital: it enabled systematic registration and identification of minors in precarious conditions, ensured timely transfers to accommodations offering decent conditions, initiated best interest assessments, and successfully relocated most eligible children. Any future relocations will now fall under the new solidarity mechanism of the Pact on Migration and Asylum, set for full implementation by June 2026.  In December 2020, Greece abolished by law the practice of placing unaccompanied minors from third countries in protective custody, solely because they lacked a safe or known place of residence. According to this law, once an individual is identified as an unaccompanied minor, the Secretary General for Vulnerable Persons and Institutional Protection (formerly the Special Secretariat for the Protection of Unaccompanied Minors) of the Ministry of Migration and Asylum becomes responsible for ensuring their safe accommodation.  The national accommodation system for unaccompanied minors is governed by national law, and since 2023, a comprehensive legal framework has been established for the operation of accommodation centers and supervised apartments for semi-independent living. A monitoring and evaluation mechanism is in place, along with a complaint system for children under care of each accommodation and facility.  Professionals working with unaccompanied minors are required to have appropriate training, follow a code of conduct, and engage in continuous professional development. Currently, there are 1,746 long-term accommodation places—1,508 in 59 Accommodation Centres for Unaccompanied Minors and 238 in 60 Supervised Apartments for Semi-Independent Living.   In 2022, the, operational since 2021, National Emergency Response Mechanism (NERM) was established by law. The NERM is responsible for addressing the immediate protection needs of unaccompanied minors living in precarious conditions by providing a comprehensive framework of emergency assistance that includes tracing, emergency accommodation, support in identification procedures and in primary healthcare, and holistic case management; it also addresses issues such as prevention of human trafficking, recruitment of minors into criminal networks, as well as protection of presumed victims of exploitation, including forced criminality, begging, prostitution, and drug trafficking. NERM continues its operation, and since April 5th, 2021, has successfully provided accommodation to 6,325 unaccompanied children. Currently, four (4) Emergency Accommodation Facilities for Unaccompanied Children are operating across the country, by the International Organization for Migration (IOM), with a total capacity of 155 places and in accordance with the provisions of the decision of the Deputy Minister of Migration and Asylum on “Standard Rules for the Operation of Emergency Accommodation Facilities for Unaccompanied Minors”.  On January 1, 2024, the National Guardianship System for Unaccompanied Minors was operationally launched, to ensure that every unaccompanied child has a designated guardian, providing care that is tailored to their age and specific needs. The General Secretariat for Vulnerable Persons and Institutional Protection is tasked with overseeing the planning, monitoring, and coordination of the guardianship system.  The competent public prosecutor of the region appoints a legal entity as the guardian, which in turn assigns the tasks to a natural person, its employee, called “guardianship mandated person”. For 2024, following a rigorous selection process, two local civil society organizations were chosen to provide these services (a responsibility extended for 2025).  The guardian is responsible for overseeing all matters related to child custody and care, working closely with the organization that provides daily care for the child. This includes representing the child in legal or administrative proceedings, ensuring that their rights are respected, providing access to education and healthcare, developing a tailored action plan for durable solutions, and assisting with other practical matters.  Currently, 139 guardians are active, 26 of whom are stationed at key entry points. Since the beginning of the nationwide project, 1,966 unaccompanied and separated minors have been under care, with 5,573 having received guardianship services to date.  In collaboration with UNHCR and the European Union Asylum Agency, the General Secretariat for Vulnerable Persons and Institutional Protection (GSVPIP) launched the “Harmonization, Establishment and Certification of Best Interests Procedures” project, introducing a Toolkit to standardize assessments and consistently prioritize the child’s best interests. Aimed at enhancing the protection of unaccompanied children—particularly against risks like trafficking and exploitation—the Toolkit supports child protection professionals, including guardians, in delivering high-quality care.  The project aligns with the National Strategy for the Protection of Unaccompanied Minors, promoting uniform procedures and the engagement of trained experts to safeguard all unaccompanied, separated, or temporarily removed children in Greece, regardless of nationality or statelessness.  As far as education is concerned, refugee children may be enrolled in schools even with insufficient documentation; they can also enroll in school units with Reception Classes without having to provide any documentation. In the latter, they receive intensive Greek language support, as part of the mainstream program in «Educational Priority Zones».  There are also other important educational schemes for refugee children such as the Reception School Annexes for Refugee Education and the position of Refugee Education Coordinators who mediate among the Ministry, the Refugee Accommodation Centers and Greek schools.  The operation of afternoon classes in Reception School Annexes for Refugee Education allows children to leave the centers and provides them with the necessary time to adjust to educational facilities without experiencing excessive pressure.  Reception Units for Refugee Education, operate after school hours but are also part of formal education – they focus on language acquisition, familiarization with school, as well as psychological and social support. Refugee Education Coordinators assist parents with the registration process and ensure student enrolment.   Teacher trainings are also organized. The Ministry of Education has utilized funding and strategic frameworks such as the National Strategic Reference Framework (NSRF) for the periods 2014-2020 and 2021-2027, as well as the Asylum, Migration, and Integration Fund, to design and implement co-funded programs/actions specifically targeted at addressing the educational needs of marginalized and vulnerable groups, including refugees and migrants  The Special Department of Coordination and Monitoring of Refugee Education (ATSPEP) of the Ministry of Education oversees the planning, coordination, and evaluation of refugee children's education in Greece, in collaboration with the Ministry of Migration and other relevant bodies. Its core goals are the full inclusion of refugee children in the Greek educational system, ensuring their uninterrupted education, and promoting awareness within schools and society.  During 2023-2024, a number of supporting programs were implemented, such as teacher training, interpretation services, research study on Reception Classes, infrastructure support (school units, accommodation centers, network access, etc.), inclusive education programs, digital learning initiatives, non-formal education programs, workshops, school visits, and discussions on inclusive education, aiming to establish a common axis for the management of issues related to the attendance and smooth integration of students with a refugee profile.

According to legislative provisions adopted in 2022, candidate lists for parliamentary elections cannot be established if senior party officials or individuals who exert actual—not formal—leadership have been convicted of imprisonment for offenses such as violations of the democratic regime, offenses against the international status of Greece, offenses against foreign states, offenses against state and political organs or authorities, and crimes against public order. This requirement is not met if there has been a conviction, at any level, of parliamentary candidates, founding members, or former presidents for offenses such as high treason (against the democratic regime), membership in a criminal organization, terrorism, or participation in a terrorist organization. The Greek Ombudsperson, an independent authority guaranteed by the Constitution, is tasked with investigating allegations about arbitrary acts by law enforcement officers.  The Ombudsperson may act on complaints, initiate investigations ex officio, or address cases referred by the competent authorities or following European Court of Human Rights judgments. During investigations, disciplinary decisions are suspended until the Ombudsperson issues a report, which the authorities must consider before proceeding. The Hellenic Police transmits (in electronic form) to the Greek Ombudsperson relevant cases for informative purposes or possible further actions on a weekly basis. A number of disciplinary procedures based on these investigations have resulted in disciplinary sanctions. Since 2017, more than 1,400 cases involving such allegations have been referred to the Ombudsperson. Finally, in 2024, the provisions of the disciplinary legislative framework were amended. The key objectives of the reform include removing uniformed officers involved in serious criminal or disciplinary offenses from active service, enhancing the effectiveness of measures to address corruption, streamlining bureaucratic procedures, and expediting the administration of disciplinary justice. As far as demonstrations are concerned, the relevant Law protects freedom of assembly within a balanced framework, emphasizing the principles of necessity and proportionality. It clearly defines police powers, provides judicial protection to those affected by police actions, and establishes procedural safeguards. Public assemblies can only be restricted or dispersed only if there are convincing reasons, prior approval from a judge and prosecutor, and the opportunity for organizers to appeal such decisions. It is significant that in 2023, more than 4,500 rallies were organized and only three were banned. The police prioritizes non-violent crowd control methods, using force, such as tear gas or the operation of riot squads, only when necessary. Guidelines have been issued to ensure law enforcement authorities fully comply with the prohibition of violence against individuals in pretrial detention and prioritize the timely conduct of administrative and criminal proceedings. Detainees are informed of the reasons for their detention and their rights immediately upon custody and have access to legal assistance at all stages. Law enforcement prioritize detainees' health and safety, and make every effort to use detention only as a last resort, and apply alternative measures, such as regular reporting or confinement to a specific location. For foreign detainees, guidelines emphasize assistance in contacting relatives, lawyers, consular authorities, and international bodies. Interpreters are provided for those lacking proficiency in Greek, and translated information leaflets are made available. Such individuals could appeal detention decisions through multiple channels.

With this change, defamation now applies only when false statements harmful to someone's honor and reputation are knowingly disseminated to third parties. Previously, journalists accused of press libel were often convicted of simple defamation due to the difficulty of proving malice, leading to criminal convictions being used in civil courts for compensation claims. Following the repeal, journalists and human rights defenders can now either be convicted of libel or acquitted, making civil claims against them more difficult to pursue. Additionally, individuals who become aware of defamatory claims in an official capacity without personal ties to the parties are excluded as 'third recipients,' further limiting the scope of defamation.  On May 23, 2022, a Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) on the Protection, Safety, and Empowerment of Journalists and Media Professionals was signed, involving key governmental bodies, and establishing a framework for legal and non-legislative measures to promote media pluralism and freedom of expression. Aligned with international standards, including the UN resolutions on journalist safety, it reflects Greece’s commitment to protecting journalists and serves as a foundation for further initiatives enhancing media protections. In July 2022, following this MoU, Greece established a Task Force to enhance the protection and empowerment of media professionals. Comprising 22 members from government, academia, media associations, and journalist unions, the Task Force fosters collaboration between the public sector and media stakeholders. The Task Force meets monthly to address issues such as secure working conditions, digital safety, gender-based challenges, and journalist security in conflict zones, combatting SLAPPs, while incorporating feedback from media professionals. In this direction, a new Collective Labour Agreement (CLA) was signed on 23 May 2024 between the Greek State and journalistic unions concerning the journalists employed in the public sector. The new CLA includes provisions to improve the working conditions of journalists including measures for the personal protection and safety of journalists in war zones or areas affected by natural disasters, humanitarian crises, or epidemics. This CLA will be in force for two years (from 01.01.2024 to 31.12.2025) and is essentially a continuation, with several beneficial additions, of the previous CLA. In 2024, the ICSJ conducted several training initiatives, including a Joint Training session for police officers and journalists held under the auspices of UNESCO. This training aimed to enhance police officers’ knowledge in the area of respecting and protecting press freedom, by preventing attacks on journalists by violent groups. Additionally, during the 4th School for the Safety of Journalists, the program addressed the protection of journalists operating in war zones and crisis areas. Article 8 of Law 4779/2021 prohibits discrimination and hate speech in audiovisual media. The National Council for Radio and Television, Greece's independent supervisory authority, is the supervisory and sanctioning independent administrative authority in Greece; its jurisdiction extends beyond traditional media, allowing it to impose sanctions regardless of the technology or medium used to broadcast content. The National Action Plan for the Management of Public Open-Air Assemblies has been in full force since 2021 to better manage demonstrations on behalf of journalists and media professionals. It includes the appointment of a liaison officer who acts as a channel of communication with media professionals throughout the assembly in order to facilitate, if requested, the unhindered exercise of their mission and to assist them in the event of an injury.  Moreover, article 15 of Law 5085/2025 imposes stricter penalties on those who incite violence against journalists who cover sports events. An independent Committee of Deontology, composed of experts in the media sector, ensures adherence to journalistic ethics. The Committee reviews complaints against print or electronic media for violating the ethical standards outlined by the Journalists’ Union of Athens Daily and the Panhellenic Federation of Journalists’ Unions Codes of Ethics.

130.76 Revise its national legislation with a view to recognizing the right to conscientious objection to military service, envisaging an alternative service to military service to which all conscientious objectors have access to and that is not punitive or discriminatory in its nature, cost or duration (Panama);66 130.78 Continue efforts to guarantee the right to freedom of religion or belief of every member of the society (Poland);  | Military service, mandated by Article 4(6) of the Constitution, is obligatory. Conscientious objectors may fulfill this duty through alternative service, based on consistently upheld religious, philosophical, or moral beliefs reflected in their behavior. Under Law 4609/2019, the committee which is responsible for examining requests for the status of conscientious objector, comprises three university professors, a State Legal Council member, and one Armed Forces Officer. If a conscientious objector's application is denied and they appeal, service suspension is automatically granted. Most applications are ultimately approved. Notably, the Minister of National Defense can no longer suspend alternative service provisions, even during wartime. Standard military service is 12 months across all branches, with possible reductions in specific cases. Alternative service for conscientious objectors lasts 15 months or, if reduced, 12, 9, or 5 months. The longer duration reflects proportional equality, as alternative service is less demanding and often involves public utility work under better conditions. The status of conscientious objectors is not differentiated in other provisions of the law from those fulfilling military service.
