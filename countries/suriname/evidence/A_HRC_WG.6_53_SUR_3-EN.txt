Summary of stakeholders’ submissions on Suriname*
Report of the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights
I. Background
1. The present report was prepared pursuant to Human Rights Council resolutions 5/1 and 16/21, taking into consideration the periodicity of the universal periodic review and the outcome of the previous review.1 It is a summary of 11 stakeholders’ submissions2 for the universal periodic review, presented in a summarized manner owing to word-limit constraints.
II. Information provided by stakeholders
A. Scope of international obligations and cooperation with human rights mechanisms
2. JS5 recommended that Suriname take all necessary steps to accede to the Second Optional Protocol to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, without reservations.3 3. JS7 recommended that Suriname, as a matter of urgency, ratify the Indigenous and Tribal Peoples Convention, 1989 (No. 169) of the International Labour Organization and sign and ratify the Regional Agreement on Access to Information, Public Participation and Justice in Environmental Matters in Latin America and the Caribbean (Escazú Agreement) and withdraw any reservations to the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples.4 4. JS7 recommended that Suriname act without delay on the judgments of the InterAmerican Court of Human Rights (Moiwana Community v. Suriname (2005), Saramaka People v. Suriname (2007) and Kaliña and Lokono Peoples v. Suriname (2015)) by recognizing communal property rights and reparations and establish a national commission with direct Indigenous and Tribal representation to oversee the recognition, protection, and titling of demarcated ancestral territories.5

5. The International Campaign to Abolish Nuclear Weapons (ICAN) recommended that Suriname sign and ratify the UN Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons as a matter of international urgency.6
6. The Center for Global Nonkilling (CGNK) recommended that Suriname ratify the Convention on the Prevention and the Punishment of the crime of Genocide.7
B. National human rights framework
1. Constitutional and legislative framework
7. JS6 recommended that Suriname adopt and implement comprehensive disability rights legislation in line with the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, including explicit prohibition of discrimination, enforceable accessibility standards, and a clear framework for inclusive education at all levels, supported by adequate funding, data collection systems, and monitoring mechanisms.8
2. Institutional infrastructure and policy measures
8. JS6 stated that since 2025, there had been a pattern indicative of gradual institutional paralysis. Several draft decisions remained pending and could not be rendered, such as those concerning land allocation and mining legislation, which had direct implications for Indigenous and Tribal Peoples’ collective land rights, access to natural resources, and the protection of their traditional livelihoods and cultural integrity.9
9. JS6 stated that despite repeated commitments, Suriname had persistently failed to establish and operationalize an independent national human rights institution in line with the Paris Principles, a gap that had been consistently highlighted across multiple United Nations treaty body reviews.10
10. JS6 recommended that Suriname expedite the formal and legal establishment of an independent national human rights institution in full compliance with the Paris Principles, and guarantee adequate and sustainable funding, staffing, and institutional capacity for the institution, including adequate representation and an accessible complaint mechanism for individuals and vulnerable groups.11
C. Promotion and protection of human rights
1. Implementation of international human rights obligations, taking into account applicable international humanitarian law
Equality and non-discrimination
11. JS3 stated that Indigenous peoples faced systemic discrimination, including in education, healthcare, public administration and access to finance.12
12. JS3 recommended that Suriname ensure availability and accessibility to safe drinking water, education, and quality health services for all citizens, without discrimination.13
Right to life, liberty and security of person, and freedom from torture
13. JS6 recommended that Suriname establish an independent oversight mechanism to investigate allegations of police misconduct, excessive use of force and ill-treatment, ensuring full independence from the police chain of command and compliance with international standards.14
14. JS8 recommended that Suriname adopt an independent police oversight body that impartially, promptly, and thoroughly investigated all claims of violations of human rights by police officers.15
15. JS6 recommended that Suriname adopt and enforce clear use-of-force regulations aligned with international norms and ensure mandatory human rights training for law enforcement personnel.16

16. JS7 recommended that Suriname investigate excessive use of force against Indigenous protestors and hold security forces accountable and halt criminal prosecutions of leaders advocating rights.17
Administration of justice, including impunity, and the rule of law
17. JS6 stated that the continued challenges affecting the functioning of the Constitutional Court of Suriname significantly undermined the State’s capacity to operationalize its international human rights commitments. It noted that since 7 May 2025 the term of six of its members, including the Chair and the Registrar, had expired and that the Government and the National Assembly had omitted to appoint a new Chair, Registrar and members.18
18. JS6 recommended that Suriname urgently restore the functionality of the Constitutional Court by appointing a Chair, Registrar, and full number of members, ensuring the Court was adequately resourced, including permanent institutional housing and administrative capacity.19
19. JS6 stated that members of the Constitutional Court were appointed through processes in which political actors played a decisive role, as the National Assembly presented candidates to the President. There was a risk that independence, neutrality, impartiality, and credibility could be undermined if incoming governments sought to appoint politically affiliated individuals to the Court when members’ five-year terms ended. The five-year term was especially significant as it coincided with general election cycles.20
20. JS6 recommended that Suriname adopt legislative and constitutional safeguards to ensure independence of the Constitutional Court, including clear and transparent procedures for appointment, tenure, reappointment, and dismissal which are insulated from political influence and in line with international standards.21
Fundamental freedoms
21. JS3 stated that environmental human rights defenders had reported intimidation and harassment following peaceful demonstrations in 2023, including surveillance, obstruction, and threats. Other cases had resulted in the deaths of two activists.22
22. JS7 stated that no protection mechanism existed for threatened Indigenous and Tribal Peoples’ activists. There had been no prosecutions of security forces for excessive force against Indigenous protestors. Defenders had also reported defamation and official smearing (calling protestors “terrorists”) in Parliament. The overall criminalization and threatening trend signified an escalating effort to silence Indigenous and Tribal Peoples' human rights defenders through intimidation. The lack of an independent national human rights institution with Indigenous representation further impeded remedies.23
23. JS7 recommended that Suriname pass a national law or administrative order to protect environmental and land rights defenders, with training of police and judiciary on Indigenous rights issues.24
Right to marriage and family life
24. JS8 recommended that Suriname amend article 33, Book 1 of the Civil Code to permit same-sex marriages, compatible with the IACHR Advisory Opinion on Gender Identity, Equality, and Non-Discrimination of Same-Sex Couples.25
Prohibition of all forms of slavery, including trafficking in persons
25. The European Centre for Law and Justice (ECLJ) stated that in Suriname, nearly all identified victims of human trafficking had been trafficked for sexual exploitation. Once victims arrived in Suriname, the traffickers often sold them to club owners who sexually exploited them.26
26. ECLJ stated that while Suriname had taken steps to combat human trafficking, which had led to an increase in the number of cases and victims being identified, the number of convictions remained low.27

27. ECLJ recommended that Suriname take prosecuting human traffickers seriously and prosecute them to the fullest extent of the law.28
28. JS6 stated that children aged 12–16 years were particularly vulnerable to the worst forms of child labour because they were not legally required to attend school nor were they legally permitted to work. The compulsory education age (12 years) did not extend to the minimum age for employment (16 years).29
29. JS6 recommended that Suriname align the compulsory education age with the minimum age for employment to eliminate the legal gap that increases children’s vulnerability to the worst forms of child labour.30
Right to work and to just and favourable conditions of work
30. JS2 stated that persons with disabilities faced significant barriers in accessing employment, including the absence of inclusive labour policies, reasonable accommodations, and targeted measures to support labour market participation.31
31. JS6 recommended that Suriname amend existing legislation on sexual harassment in the workplace to ensure equal protection for public servants.32
Right to social security
32. JS6 stated that persons with disabilities, older persons, and informal workers encountered systemic obstacles in accessing social assistance, while pension levels have not kept pace with inflation.33
33. JS6 stated that Indigenous and Maroon communities in the interior faced compounded barriers, including geographic isolation, limited access to social protection, and exclusion from compensation schemes due to weak civil registration systems and digital access gaps.34
34. JS6 recommended that Suriname remove administrative and structural barriers to accessing social services, including simplifying registration processes, strengthening civil registries, expanding digital and physical access points, and ensuring that mechanisms were inclusive and non-discriminatory.35
35. JS2 recommended that Suriname legally guarantee disability benefits and align the amount and allocation of disability benefits with the national cost of living, while also ensuring that those with extra needs received extra benefits.36
36. JS6 recommended that Suriname establish an inclusive national social security framework that ensured adequate and differentiated coverage for informal workers, persons with disabilities, older persons, and populations in the interior.37
Right to an adequate standard of living
37. JS6 stated that multidimensional poverty in Suriname affected up to 73% of large households, with disproportionately high rates among children and working-age adults raising families. Those deprivations extended beyond income poverty and included limited access to education, health care, food, and basic services, constituting a failure to progressively realize economic and social rights in line with international obligations.38
38. JS6 stated that food insecurity was acute and widespread, affecting approximately 61% of the population and nearly 80% in the interior, highlighting severe geographic disparities.39
39. JS2 stated that persons with disabilities were disproportionately affected by poverty. The monthly disability allowance of SRD 4,550 (approximately USD 114) was significantly below the national poverty line of SRD 7,810 ((approximately USD 200) for a single-adult household. The allowance was not legally guaranteed and could be adjusted administratively, resulting in financial insecurity, and affecting access to basic needs, including food, healthcare, and independent living.40
40. JS6 recommended that Suriname adopt and implement a comprehensive, rights-based national poverty reduction strategy, with measurable targets, disaggregated data, and explicit alignment with human rights obligations.41

Right to health
41. JS6 stated that progress in ensuring equitable access to quality health care remained limited and structurally uneven. Suriname had not ensured sustained investments in rightsbased health legislation, policies, and programmes that addressed underlying determinants of health disparities.42
42. JS6 stated that the health system was further strained by the over-centralization of specialized care in the Academic Hospital Paramaribo, which provided around 60% of hospital care, 90% of intensive care, and all top clinical services. The hospital faced severe financial constraints, staff shortages, brain drain, and inconsistent availability of medicines and supplies, undermining service quality and access, especially for vulnerable and rural populations.43
43. JS6 stated that healthcare accessibility outside the capital city was limited, with low numbers of clinics and primary care doctors. In the interior, where Indigenous and Tribal Communities lived, most clinics were staffed by healthcare assistants instead of doctors. Those in need of more specialized care had to travel to the city, which was prohibitively expensive.44
44. JS2 stated that access to healthcare remained limited for persons with disabilities due to physical inaccessibility, financial constraints, and limited availability of specialized services, particularly in rural and interior regions.45
45. JS6 recommended that Suriname increase public expenditure on health, with maintenance of critical infrastructure, and transparent procurement and supply chain management to address recurrent shortages.46
46. JS6 stated that Suriname had reduced maternal mortality over time, but the level remained high and pointed to persistent weaknesses in the availability and quality of maternal health services.47
47. JS1 recommended that Suriname strengthen maternal healthcare by ensuring timely and equitable access to maternal healthcare, especially in rural areas.48
48. JS6 recommended that Suriname ensure targeted maternal and reproductive health services in rural communities.49
49. JS1 stated that an eminent concern was the high suicide trend in Suriname, with an estimated 22.2 suicides per 100.000 persons in 2021.50
50. JS6 stated that implementation of a national mental health plan remained insufficient to address a worsening crisis. Suriname continued to record among the highest suicide rates in the region.51
51. JS1 recommended that Suriname strengthen access to mental health services and further media training in relation to protecting adolescents and children against suicide.52
52. JS6 recommended that Suriname integrate mental health services into schools, including routine screening, early identification, and referral systems for children and adolescents, making use of, and expanding the program of care-coordinators in schools.53
53. JS6 recommended that Suriname adopt and implement age-appropriate comprehensive sexuality education in and outside schools, focused on prevention of adolescent pregnancy, sexually transmitted infections, consent, and access to services.54
Right to education
54. JS6 stated that structural deficiencies continued to undermine both access and quality of education in the hinterland. These included persistent shortages of qualified teachers, limited availability of secondary-level facilities, inadequate infrastructure, lack of water and electricity, insufficient maintenance of school buildings, inadequate housing for teachers, and transportation challenges. Indigenous and Tribal children were disproportionately affected. These challenges were compounded by systemic disruptions, including delayed school openings compared to coastal areas, and recurrent flooding leading to prolonged school closures. In addition, teacher absenteeism and delayed deployment, sometimes linked to

inadequate government support for transportation, further compromised the continuity and quality of education in those regions.55
55. JS3 stated that there were no secondary schools available in, or near, indigenous territories, hindering indigenous peoples’ opportunities in education.56
56. JS3 stated that due to low wages, neglected infrastructure in the interior and the lack of potable water, availability of teachers for primary schools in indigenous territories was not guaranteed, resulting in regular school closures, shortened semesters or large, combined classes.57
57. JS2 recommended that Suriname allocate targeted funding to ensure equal access to education in rural and interior areas and develop accessible and affordable transportation systems for students with disabilities in rural areas.58
58. JS6 recommended that Suriname invest in equitable access to quality education at all levels, including measures to reduce school dropout, and expand secondary and tertiary education opportunities.59
59. JS1 recommended that Suriname address the high rate of school dropouts by combating sexual violence and domestic violence and through elevating the positive role of the family in public policymaking.60
60. JS2 stated that evidence from documented cases indicated that no fully implemented inclusive education system existed. Key barriers included a lack of adapted learning materials, a shortage of trained and specialized teachers, insufficient reasonable accommodations, and physical inaccessibility of school buildings. Transportation barriers frequently resulted in irregular school attendance. Those factors contributed to higher dropout rates and limited progression to further education.61
61. JS2 recommended that Suriname remove structural barriers to education for children with disabilities, ensure availability of inclusive materials, trained teachers, and reasonable accommodations, and strengthen policies to guarantee equal access and participation.62
Cultural rights
62. JS3 stated that traditional knowledge systems, practices, dress, arts and languages were eroding due to cultural suppression and lack of documentation.63
63. JS3 stated that Suriname should support and enable the systematic documentation and preservation of Indigenous cultural heritage, including traditional ecological knowledge, medicinal practices, rituals, and languages, to safeguard them for future generations.64
64. JS3 recommended that Suriname document and safeguard Indigenous languages, cultures, and knowledge, and embed these into national education, public awareness campaigns, and policymaking.65
Development, the environment, and business and human rights
65. JS7 stated that mercury‐intensive gold mining, logging, and new concessions continued in unceded territories, contaminating rivers and undermining health and food security.66
66. JS3 stated that small-scale gold mining dumped around 40.000 to 60.000 kilograms of mercury in Suriname’s environment, impacting the biodiversity and the health of mainly indigenous communities.67
67. JS7 recommended that Suriname enforce a national ban on mercury and cyanide in gold mining, strengthen inspection and penalties for illegal mining, provide healthcare screening and environmental remediation (water purification, soil restoration) in contaminated Indigenous and Tribal Peoples areas.68
68. JS3 recommended that Suriname strictly regulate small-scale and industrial mining and logging, including no-mining buffer zones around rivers (e.g., 50 meters), mandatory restoration, and a real enforcement mechanism.69

69. JS2 recommended that Suriname ensure the inclusion of persons with disabilities in climate change and disaster risk reduction frameworks.70
2. Rights of specific persons or groups
Women
70. JS6 stated that data provided by shelters such as STICRIS and Hadassah indicated that existing capacity was insufficient, with victims regularly being turned away due to lack of space. Services were heavily concentrated in Paramaribo, leaving women in rural, interior and remote areas with significant access barriers. In addition, prolonged shelter stays due to the absence of reintegration and housing solutions pointed to systemic shortcomings in longterm support. Both shelters reported chronic underfunding, shortages of qualified personnel, and the absence of predictable and structural government financing, resulting in a continued reliance on donor funding.71
71. JS6 recommended that Suriname swiftly table, adopt and operationalize the revised Domestic Violence Law, and increase the availability, capacity and geographic accessibility of shelters, particularly in interior and underserved regions.72
Children
72. JS6 stated that children were subjected to commercial sexual exploitation, sometimes as a result of human trafficking, including in informal mining camps in Suriname's remote interior. In addition, children who worked in small-scale gold mines risk exposure to mercury and cyanide.73
73. JS6 recommended that Suriname amend legislation to explicitly prohibit all forms of commercial sexual exploitation of children, including the use, procurement and offering of children for commercial sex.74
74. JS6 recommended that Suriname strengthen prevention, identification and protection mechanisms for children at risk of trafficking and commercial sexual exploitation, particularly in remote and high-risk areas.75
75. JS6 stated that Suriname launched the Child Ombuds Institute in April 2025 as an independent body mandated to receive and investigate complaints, advocate for children’s best interests, and ensure accountability to national and international child rights standards. However, as it was not yet operational, cases could not be investigated, and relevant institutions could not be held accountable.76
76. JS6 recommended that Suriname operationalize the Child Ombuds Institute, ensuring its independence, adequate resources, and accessibility to all children, including those in rural and interior regions.77
77. JS6 stated that there was a near universal acceptance of forms of violent “discipline” in Suriname, such as slapping or caning. Those were accepted forms to discipline children and were not prosecuted. The Ministry of Education had given instructions to schools against the use of corporal punishment, but it still occurred because of its general acceptance by society.78
78. JS6 recommended that Suriname adopt comprehensive legislation prohibiting all forms of violence against children, including corporal punishment in all settings (home, schools, alternative care, and institutions), in line with the Convention on the Rights of the Child.79
79. JS6 recommended that Suriname develop and implement a national child protection strategy, addressing prevention, reporting, investigation, and response to all forms of violence, including corporal punishment, psychological violence, bullying, and neglect.80
80. JS6 recommended that Suriname conduct nationwide awareness and behaviour change campaigns to eliminate social acceptance of corporal punishment and promote positive, non-violent forms of child-rearing.81

Persons with disabilities
81. JS2 stated that since having ratified the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities in 2017, Suriname had made limited progress in implementing the rights of persons with disabilities. Structural barriers persisted across key sectors. Persons with disabilities continued to face exclusion in education, public services, economic participation, and decision-making processes. Accessibility, particularly mobility, remained a central barrier affecting the realization of multiple rights. Disparities between urban and interior regions further exacerbated inequalities in access to essential services.82
82. JS2 stated that no policy framework or formal recognition of sign language existed in Suriname. This limited access to public information, services, and communication for persons with hearing impairments.83
83. JS2 recommended that Suriname establish and enforce accessibility standards for public infrastructure, transport, and digital services.84
84. JS2 stated that no structured or institutionalized mechanism existed to ensure meaningful participation of persons with disabilities in policy processes.85
85. JS2 recommended that Suriname establish institutional mechanisms ensuring the structured and meaningful participation of persons with disabilities in all policy processes.86
Indigenous Peoples
86. JS7 stated that Suriname had accepted multiple universal periodic review recommendations on Indigenous rights; to recognize collective land rights, implement InterAmerican Court of Human Rights judgments, guarantee free, prior and informed consent, and to address discrimination in services. In practice, little progress was evident. The long‐ promised collective rights law had not been adopted. An Act passed in 2017 to protect Indigenous residential areas still lacked force as it had not been promulgated by the President. The Government had not ratified the Indigenous and Tribal Peoples Convention, 1989 (No. 169) of the International Labour Organization by 2021 as pledged. Concession‐issuance and land titling continued apace, contrary to recommendations; by 2024 roughly half the national territory, including most Indigenous villages, were covered by mining/logging concessions. Ongoing gold mining remained unregulated, with no national ban on mercury.87
87. JS3 stated that it was crucial to underscore that the relationship between Indigenous peoples in Suriname and their ancestral lands constituted the foundation of their cultural identity, spiritual life, social integrity, and economic survival.88
88. JS3 stated that despite binding judgments of the Inter-American Court of Human Rights with clear rulings requiring Suriname to recognize the collective juridical personality of Indigenous peoples, to delimit, demarcate, and grant collective title to their territories, and to establish effective mechanisms for territorial recognition and protection, Suriname continued to fall short on action and implementation.89
89. JS7 stated that national legislation granted broad discretionary powers over forests and minerals to the State or concessionaires, with only a narrow exclusion zone (5 km from villages) in the 2017 Domain Land amendment. That retained sweeping powers to issue leases or concessions inside Indigenous and tribal peoples’ territories, without requiring consent.90
90. JS7 stated that Suriname had delayed and diluted a draft “Collective land Rights” law (first tabled in 2021) and had bypassed the legislative process for a controversial land‐ conversion decree, sparking Indigenous protests.91
91. JS3 stated that Suriname must move beyond partial or administrative measures and adopt comprehensive constitutional and legislative reforms that guaranteed rights for Indigenous peoples (inclusive of collective land rights) in accordance with international standards and binding regional jurisprudence.92
92. JS3 stated that uncontrolled small-scale gold mining, sand mining, logging, wildlife exploitation, and drug trafficking severely disrupted ecosystems and undermined Indigenous livelihoods.93

93. JS7 stated that between 2021 and 2026, the Palmera logging road had been extended from 4 km to 125 km, which cut right through the Saamaka ancestral forest.94
94. JS3 stated that the continuation and expansion of extractive and large-scale agricultural activities in Suriname posed serious risks to the rights of Indigenous and Tribal peoples, particularly regarding land tenure, environmental integrity, and participation in decision-making.95
95. JS3 stated that free, prior and informed consent was not implemented in practice. Consultations were often superficial or misleading, without uniform regulation poor governance.96
96. JS7 stated that decisions on mining concessions, land leases, or infrastructure were routinely taken without effectively including Indigenous or tribal peoples’ input. Indigenous authorities had learned of large-scale oil, logging or plantation deals only through press reports.97
97. JS6 stated that there had been a growing dissatisfaction of how traditional Indigenous lands were given away for mining and logging without the internationally recognized right to free, prior and informed consent. Concessions had been issued in traditional lands to apparently influential individuals, including leading politicians, and there was an absence of legal recourse for Indigenous peoples.98
98. JS3 stated that Suriname should adopt and enforce a single, nationally endorsed free, prior and informed consent protocol, developed in genuine partnership with Indigenous peoples, and ensure that it was conducted through culturally appropriate, community-wide decision-making processes before any project approval.99
99. JS3 stated that the constitutional establishment of an independent (and long-term budgeted) Indigenous Peoples’ Institute, mandated to monitor rights implementation, safeguard cultural heritage, and oversee free, prior and informed consent compliance, would strengthen accountability and ensure sustained protection of Indigenous identity, lands, and environmental stewardship.100
100. JS3 recommended that Suriname take the necessary measures to establish an effective mechanism for delimiting, demarcating, and titling the territories of indigenous and tribal peoples in Suriname.101
101. JS7 recommended that Suriname expedite the adoption of robust legislation recognizing the legal personality of communities, collective identity, traditional authority and communal land and resource rights of Indigenous and Tribal peoples, as required by InterAmerican Court of Human Rights judgments. It stated that that should include constitutional amendments for communal collective title, and safeguards preventing expropriation of already granted concessions.102
102. JS7 recommended that Suriname formalize the national free, prior and informed consent protocol of Indigenous people in Suriname to prevent further and more serious violations of the rights of Indigenous or Tribal Peoples in government actions.103
Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender and intersex persons
103. JS8 stated that many LGBTIQ+ people continued to experience discrimination, harassment, and violence by both State and non-State actors.104
104. JS8 stated that LGBTIQ+ youth faced considerable stigma, discrimination, and violence from family members, including but not limited to, expulsion from the home. Those individuals were at increased risk of homelessness and housing insecurity; exposure to violence and exploitation; barriers to education, healthcare, and employment; and increased risk of criminalization and mental health harm. Those cumulative human rights violations were further complicated by the lack of specialized shelters or safe housing, family mediation mechanisms sensitive to SOGIESC issues, and coordinated social services for displaced LGBTIQ+ youth.105
105. JS8 recommended that Suriname enact comprehensive legislation to prohibit discrimination by State and non-State actors based on sexual orientation, gender identity,
