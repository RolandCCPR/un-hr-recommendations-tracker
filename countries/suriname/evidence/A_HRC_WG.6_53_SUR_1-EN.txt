I. Introduction
1. The Republic of Suriname hereby presents its fourth national report within the framework of the UPR of the Human Rights Council of the United Nations. This report covers the period 2021–2026 and provides an overview of measures, legislative developments, policy initiatives, institutional strengthening and challenges relating to the implementation of human rights obligations and previous UPR recommendations.
2. The Constitution of the Republic of Suriname (S.B. 1987 no. 116, as amended) forms the basis of the national legal order and safeguards the principle of non-discrimination (art. 8(2)), gender equality (art. 35), the right to health (art. 36), the protection of youth (art. 37) and the right to education (art. 39). Pursuant to articles 105 and 106, approved treaty provisions have direct effect and take precedence over national legislation, provided that by their content they can be binding on everyone.
3. Suriname proactively operationalizes its international human rights obligations under the core treaties to which the State is a party (ICCPR, ICESCR, CRC, CEDAW, CRPD, ICERD and CAT). This synergy directly implements the UN 2030 Agenda, with specific focus on poverty reduction (SDG 1), food security (SDG 2), universal health care (SDG 3), quality education (SDG 4), gender equality (SDG 5), decent work (SDG 8), reducing inequality (SDG 10), sustainable communities (SDG 11), climate action (SDG 13) and strong rule-of-law institutions (SDG 16).
4. In order to give material effect to the principle of ‘leaving no one behind’, Suriname applies a rights-based approach within its national planning. Legislative and policy priorities are de facto geared towards the legal protection and structural inclusion of historically and socially vulnerable groups, in particular women, children, older persons, persons with disabilities, and Indigenous and tribal peoples. This approach guides the specific measures and programs presented in this report.
II. Methodology and consultation process
5. This report was prepared under the coordination of the State of Suriname through a participatory and inter-institutional approach. Data collection and substantive validation took place through broad consultations with the National Assembly, line ministries, academic institutions and independent experts. Civil society, including NGOs, women’s, youth and older-persons’ organizations, as well as representatives of persons with disabilities and Indigenous and tribal peoples, was actively consulted (see Annex 2 for the list of stakeholders).
6. Quantitative substantiation and policy information were systematically drawn from the SPS and the ABS. In addition, the State cooperated strategically with international partners, including OHCHR, UNDP, UNICEF, UNFPA, IOM and PAHO, in support of capacity-strengthening and data validation. This results in a balanced document that reflects both de jure progress and de facto structural challenges.

IV. International treaty system and cooperation with human rights mechanisms
9. The Republic of Suriname upholds its obligations as a State party to the core UN human rights treaties, including the ICCPR, ICESCR, ICERD, CEDAW, CRC, CRPD and CAT/UNCAT, including the optional protocols OP-ICCPR, OP2-ICCPR, OP-CRC-AC and OP-CRC-SC. Ratification of OPCAT, OP-CEDAW and OP-CRPD is currently in the preparliamentary preparatory phase.
10. Interdepartmental implementation, monitoring and reporting take place under the central direction of the Human Rights Bureau of the Ministry of Justice and Police (JusPol). This inter-ministerial coordination is structurally anchored through permanent institutional focal points within the respective line ministries and NGOs.
11. To strengthen evidence-based policymaking and the monitoring of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), the State works closely with the ABS and the Suriname Planning Bureau Foundation (SPS) through the National SDG Committee and the National Recommendations Tracking Database (NRTD). This process is de facto flanked by structural stakeholder engagement with civil society, including the WRC, VIDS, NVB, VSG, ASA, ISOS and the LGBTQ+ Platform Suriname, as well as through strategic international assistance from OHCHR, UNDP, UNICEF, UNFPA, IOM, PAHO/WHO, UN Women and the ILO.

VII. Extrajudicial executions, the rule of law and public safety
32. This part implements SDG 16, in conjunction with UPR recommendations 97.31– 97.33, 97.42, 97.61, 97.102 and 97.106 concerning effective legal protection, the right to life and the independence of the judiciary.

Suriname, individual and structural remedial measures have been implemented, including a State apology (2006) and a commemorative monument (2007). 35. Access to justice is safeguarded through a decentralized chain of police and the Judiciary. In the interior, this is supplemented by mobile interventions and field missions, giving even remote communities access to legal protection and legal referral.
36. The capacity of the KPS was strengthened in 2025–2026 with digital and technological means, including drones and camera technology, to support investigation, evidence-gathering and public-order maintenance in urban and rural areas.

This transition to digitized proceedings minimizes administrative delays in the transfer of criminal files, optimizes procedural transparency and significantly accelerates the throughput time of criminal proceedings. This de facto contributes to preventing excessive and unlawful pretrial detention. Furthermore, to combat impunity and strengthen the legal position of victims, the Judiciary structurally expanded the competences of the Service for the Execution of Criminal Sentences (DES) as of 19 February 2026. Whereas the DES previously focused primarily on traffic offences, the service is now tasked with the de facto execution of judicial rulings in a wide range of ordinary criminal cases. This includes actively tracing convicted persons and ensuring the actual payment of civil damages awarded to injured parties, which substantially intensifies national legal certainty and victim care.

These strengthen road safety, fire prevention and disaster management. They contribute to the protection of the right to life and physical integrity (arts. 6 and 9 ICCPR) and improve public safety. VIII. Conditions of detention and juvenile justice
41. This section reflects the implementation of SDG 16 and UPR recommendations 97.42, 97.61, 97.102 and 97.106, in accordance with Suriname’s obligations under the ICCPR, in particular articles 6, 9 and 10, as well as the CRC, in particular articles 37 and 40.

The legal framework for the execution of custodial sentences and measures has been strengthened by the Penitentiary Institutions and Remand Centres Act (S.B. 2023 no. 147) and the Penitentiary Decree (S.B. 2023 no. 148). In line with this, in 2026 the State is working towards the operationalization of a forensic observation facility for the specialized reception and guidance of detainees with a psychological or intellectual disability. (a) Legislation and restorative justice: The introduction of the State Decree on the Implementation of Educational and Work Projects and Community Service for Juveniles (S.B. 2025 no. 2) has strengthened the legal framework for alternative sanctions and promotes the application of restorative-justice approaches.
(b) Policy programs and prevention: The Judiciary carries out preventive and alternative interventions, including programs aimed at early guidance of at-risk youth, reduction of recidivism and out-of-court settlement of less serious offences.
(c) Resocialization and education: Within the JCC, education, vocational-training and skills programs are offered, including technical and agricultural training, aimed at sustainable reintegration and social participation.
50. To give effect to the earlier recommendations on access to efficient justice, the State has substantially strengthened the capacity of the sitting judiciary. Whereas the Court of Justice of Suriname previously operated under heavy pressure with a stable roster of 31 judges, a historic capacity expansion has been achieved through the successful implementation of the three-year RAIO training programs (Judicial Officers in Training). The Judiciary will be expanded to 47 judges by the end of the 2026 court year. By the end of 2027, approximately 12 more new judges are expected. The intention is to continue the training programs for the Judiciary.

56. In the period 2024–2025, the National Anti-Trafficking Task Force carried out prevention and awareness-raising activities in the border areas of Marowijne, Nickerie and Sipaliwini, aimed at strengthening societal resilience against human trafficking and other forms of exploitation.
X. Children’s rights and child protection
57. This section reflects the integrated implementation of SDGs 3, 4, 5, 8, 10 and 16, in conjunction with the specific UPR recommendations 97.79, 97.80, 97.81, 97.82, 97.84, 97.83, 97.86, 97.87, 97.88, 98.35, 98.36, 98.48, 98.50 and 98.51.
58. The protection of the child is anchored in the CRC, the Youth Protection Organization Act (G.B. 1965 no. 110), the Civil Code (Book 1), and the criminal and criminal-procedural provisions of the Criminal Code and the Code of Criminal Procedure. The Children’s Ombudsperson Act (S.B. 2025 no. 1) strengthens institutional oversight of compliance with children’s rights, while further operationalization is underway.
59. To de facto strengthen operational child protection and crisis intervention, the Bureau of Family Law Affairs (BuFaZ) de jure directs the protection of minors in at-risk situations in accordance with the State Decree of 28 December 1983 (S.B. 1983 no. 127). Pursuant to this legal mandate, BuFaZ is competent to initiate preventive child-protection measures and to file petitions with the cantonal court for formal custody arrangements to safeguard the best interests of the child. This institutional mandate, which also includes a structural civil-law advisory function towards the judiciary, intensifies national capacity to de facto protect minors who need special or alternative care within the family structure. In 2024, BuFaZ handled a considerable number of cases of abuse, neglect and sexual abuse.
60. The institutional protection of children’s rights is strengthened by the Children’s Ombudsperson Act (S.B. 2025 no. 1), which provides for the establishment of the Children’s Ombudsman Institute Suriname (KOI). Following the formal promulgation of the Act, the establishment of the KOI entered a concrete operational phase in 2026. In the current 2026 budget year, the Government allocated the financial means for the physical establishment of the head office. The establishment process is currently in the phase of public recruitment and selection of specialized staff, including the Children’s Ombudsperson and the complaints investigators. This guarantees the de facto operationalization and the accessibility of the complaint’s mechanisms for minors in the third quarter of 2026.
61. Pursuant to the Transitional Act on the New Civil Code, the statutory age of majority has been lowered de jure from 21 to 18 years, so that young adults acquire full legal capacity and independent civil-law powers de facto from the age of 18. Furthermore, through this codification the State has absolutely eliminated the historical and discriminatory distinction between legitimate and natural children (born out of wedlock). Every child now enjoys a de jure equal status and identical protection in inheritance and family law, which institutionalizes legal certainty for Surinamese youth in accordance with international human rights standards.

Through the Education Innovation Program (OVP), education professionals are systematically trained, in strategic partnership with UNICEF Suriname, in methods of positive discipline as a de facto alternative to corporal punishment. In parallel, institutional child protection is broadened through the National Decentralized Child Protection Program (2024–2026). XI. Persons with disabilities
65. This section reflects the integrated implementation of SDGs 4, 8, 10 and 16, in conjunction with the specific UPR recommendations 97.87, 97.88 and 97.102.
66. Suriname maintains full de jure compliance with the international CRPD, which the State ratified through the formal approval act in S.B. 2015 no. 48. To harmonized the national legal order with these treaty obligations and to operationalize the principle of nondiscrimination in article 8(2) of the Constitution, on 4 June 2024, pursuant to article 78 of the Constitution, the formal member’s bill for the General Act for Persons with Disabilities (AWMB) was submitted to the National Assembly. This body of legislation anchors mandatory de jure provisions on universal physical accessibility, the prohibition of employment discrimination, the provision of reasonable accommodation within the education system, and guaranteed social participation within the public and private sectors.
67. The strategic policy is coordinated through the National Policy Plan for Persons with Disabilities Suriname 2025–2030, which was delivered by the Presidential Commission in April 2025 and formally approved through a binding decision of the Council of Government. This central policy document, which is substantively based on the scientific data of the Situation Analysis on Children and Adolescents with Disabilities in Suriname, operationalizes concrete objectives in the areas of inclusive education, targeted labourmarket participation and the structural strengthening of inter-ministerial coordination and monitoring mechanisms in accordance with articles 5, 9 and 24 of the CRPD.
68. The material expansion of the national social-protection network has been consolidated through the overarching Financial Assistance for Persons with Disabilities program of the Ministry of SoZaVo. According to the verified statistical data of the Suriname Planning Bureau Foundation (SPS), the reach of these direct financial benefits has been systematically scaled up from 10,940 registered clients in 2021 to 21,982 clients in 2024, and grown further to a current number of 23,633 active beneficiaries in June 2025.
69. The State recognizes that, although social budgets have been substantially increased, this vulnerable target group still faces infrastructural, geographical and cultural barriers in daily practice in accessing regular health care and public transport.

71. To give de jure and de facto effect to the right to universal quality education (SDG 4), the Ministry of Education, Science and Culture is implementing far-reaching structural reforms. This policy framework is strategically anchored in the National Education Policy Plan Suriname 2024–2031 (NOBS), aimed at quality improvement, digitalization and eliminating regional asymmetry for the benefit of vulnerable groups in the interior.
72. Within this transformative framework, the State has proceeded to the de facto introduction of Development-Oriented Education (OGO) and the integration of Comprehensive Sexuality Education (CSE) into the national curriculum. This is flanked by the National Multisectoral Early Childhood Development Policy 2025–2035 to prevent early learning delays. Although programmatically consolidated, operationalization is in the implementation phase, pending de jure anchoring in an updated Primary Education Act, while work is also underway on an integrated system for the annual public monitoring of learning outcomes and school dropout.
73. The de facto implementation of the CSE curriculum is cross-sectoral synchronized with the National Sexual and Reproductive Health and Rights (SRHR) Policy 2020–2030 to reduce teenage pregnancies and promote gender equality. This preventive strategy is operationally supported by the structural deployment of the Youth Information Unit of the Suriname Police Force (KPS) through targeted school and media interventions (2024–2026), as well as by the University Institute for Children’s Rights of the AdeKUS. The State recognizes that the systematic impact measurement and coverage of these programs per school location require further data-technical strengthening.
74. To protect the cultural and linguistic treaty rights of Indigenous and tribal peoples, the Multilingual Education Program 2025–2030 was formally ratified in October 2024. This framework operationalizes education in local languages, deployed de facto through successful pilot projects such as the bilingual education project in Galibi. However, nationwide scaling-up to other parts of the interior encounters infrastructural, logistical and staffing barriers, which the Government aims to mitigate through targeted capacity expansion.
75. To structurally reduce the de facto school dropout in the interior, which averages 21 per cent, the State is implementing a coherent package of social and infrastructural measures in the period 2024–2028. This includes the National School Feeding and Health Program, the School Packages Project, subsidized pupil transport by bus and boat, and the National Protocol on Inclusive Education 2025.
76. De jure, geographical inequality is broken down through the decentralization of education services via strategic core center’s in Atjoni, Apoera and Stoelmanseiland, flanked by digital access through satellite connections (VSAT) under the National E-Education Strategy Plan. This is strengthened by measures against the teacher shortage in rural areas through station allowances, the renovation of service housing and the expansion of physical school facilities in, among other places, Sipaliwini, Amatopo and Coeroeni.

85. The right to social security and an adequate standard of living is anchored in articles 36 and 37 of the Constitution and is implemented through the National Social Protection Strategy (NSPS) 2024–2030. This framework, in line with ILO Convention 102, strengthens social protection across the life cycle, with a focus on expanding social assistance, improved targeting and better access for vulnerable groups in rural and interior areas.

The General Old-Age Provision covers approximately 77,246 older persons (more than 98%), while the General Child Allowance supports approximately 96,500 children and contributes to the implementation of the CRC on the subsistence security of families.

93. Suriname reaffirms its international and regional obligations toward Indigenous and tribal peoples under the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP, 2007) and the jurisprudence of the Inter-American Court of Human Rights, specifically the cases Moiwana Community v. 97. While Article 1a of the New Civil Code recognizes de jure customary law, its validity is constitutionally bounded by the national legal order and the Criminal Code. 105. This chapter reflects the integrated de jure and de facto implementation of SDGs 3, 6, 7, 10, 13, 15 and 17, in conjunction with the specific UPR recommendations 97.21, 97.22, 97.24, 97.42, 97.86, 97.88, 97.90, 97.102, 97.106 and 98.28–98.29.
106. Suriname implements its obligations under the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement to maintain its universal carbon-negative status (forest cover of 93%). The environmental-law foundation is anchored in the Environmental Framework Act (S.B. 2020 no. 97, amended by S.B. 2024 no. 56), which provides the formal framework for environmental management and the national permit system. In line with the ambitions of this Act, the State is studying the preconditions for possible future accession to the Regional Agreement on Access to Information, Participation and Justice in Environmental Matters (Escazú Agreement).

XVII. Challenges and remaining points of attention
111. Institutional capacity, legislative reforms and press freedom: Fragmentation of government and a lack of fine-grained data delay de facto human rights monitoring. Although the State is willing to undertake direct legislative harmonization, the final adoption of the draft acts for the National Human Rights Institute and the Public Health Framework Act is hindered by a shortage of specialized legislative-drafting capacity in the pre-parliamentary phase, while the Act on the Collective Rights of Indigenous and Tribal Peoples requires intensive deliberation to safeguard broad national consensus following the formal amendment. Although press freedom is constitutionally anchored, the decline on the international index (from 28 to 32) requires de jure modernization of the Criminal Code with respect to defamation provisions. The State prioritizes the accelerated realization of the Freedom of Information Act to protect journalists against self-censorship and to harmonized with the ICCPR.
112. Access to basic services, migration and protection of vulnerable groups: Ensuring equal and barrier-free de facto access to tertiary health care, quality education and social security remains under heavy infrastructural and geographical pressure, in particular for Indigenous and tribal communities in rural areas. This rule-of-law risk is substantially increased by the persistent brain drain and exodus of specialized medical and teaching

In parallel, the effective enforcement against gender-based violence, human trafficking and discrimination of marginalized groups, including the LGBTQ+ community, requires centralized reception structures and renewed judicial priority. Climate vulnerability, environmental protection and economic transition: as a low-lying coastal State with a unique High Forest, Low Deforestation (HFLD) status, Suriname is extremely vulnerable to the de facto destructive effects of climate change, including sea-level rise and acute flooding risks. This ecological vulnerability intersects directly with public health and the integrity of traditional living areas through the persistent environmental impact of illegal, small-scale gold mining and the associated mercury pollution. The enforcement capacity of the NMA encounters logistical barriers in the interior. 113. Financial constraints and international cooperation: Suriname’s status as a Small Island Developing State (SIDS) entails inherent budgetary constraints and shortages of specialized technical expertise. The de facto realization of the SDGs and the full implementation of the UPR recommendations therefore remain directly dependent on strategic international partnerships, de facto technical assistance and targeted multilateral financing mechanisms to maximize national implementation capacity.
XVIII. Closing statement
114. The Republic of Suriname reaffirms its unconditional sovereign commitment to the promotion and de facto protection of all human rights and fundamental freedoms, in strict accordance with the Constitution, the international treaty system and the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development. The State maintains its constructive participation within the UPR mechanism as an essential platform for dialogue, capacity-strengthening and mutual cooperation. To give effect to the remaining recommendations and priorities, the Government is intensifying its legislative harmonization and de jure institutional capacity-building. Suriname expresses its explicit appreciation for the technical assistance and support of both national and international partners, and commits itself fully to continuing these strategic partnerships towards the realization of an inclusive and just rule of law for society as a whole.
