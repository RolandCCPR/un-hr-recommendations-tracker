Introduction
1. Eswatini presents its 4th Cycle Universal Periodic Review (UPR) National Report, which provides updates on measures taken to address the accepted recommendations. The country received 222 recommendations, of which 156 were accepted and 66 noted.
2. Recommendations reported in Eswatini’s mid-term UPR report will not be repeated in this report. These recommendations include curbing gender-based violence, improving access to clean water, sanitation and hygiene, and ensuring inclusive access to education.

I. Process for the preparation of the report
3. This report has been prepared under the auspices of the National Mechanism for Reporting and Follow-Up (NMRF) and builds upon activities undertaken in preparing the mid-term report. Pursuant to the submission of the mid-term report, the NMRF Secretariat, in collaboration with the UN Country Office (UNCO) and Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR), convened consultative workshops on 8-9 July and 16 December 2025 for a continuous progress update on the implementation of the UPR Recommendations.
4. To initiate the drafting of the National Report, the NMRF Secretariat issued a call for information to duty bearers to provide written submissions on the progress of outstanding recommendations. On 24-26 March 2026, through the support of the Commonwealth Secretariat, a workshop was convened to strengthen the preparations for the National Report and sharing of best practises.
5. Participants in these consultations were drawn from Government Ministries and Departments, Civil Society Organisations, Non- Government Organisations, and the Commission on Human Rights and Public Administration (CHRPA). These consultations resulted in the compilation of the Draft UPR Report, which was subsequently enhanced through the first stakeholder consultation held on 24 June 2026 and validated on 23 July 2026. The report was then submitted to Cabinet for final endorsement and thereafter deposited with the OHCHR Secretariat.

II. Developments since the last review

6. Eswatini established a Legal Aid office under the Ministry of Justice and Constitutional Affairs in 2023 and has also enacted the Legal Aid Act 11 of 2025 and the Legal Practitioners (Amendment) Act of 2025 to provide for access to justice by providing affordable, accessible and accountable legal aid services to indigent persons upon satisfaction of the means and merits tests. The service of legal representation has not yet been provided by the Legal Aid Office, as the Government is in the process of developing the necessary Regulations to operationalise these Acts. Currently, the services offered by the Legal Aid Office include admission of walk-in clients for advice, referrals, drafting court processes for clients, and mediation where parties consent. Furthermore, the office conducts legal education in sectors identified periodically and upon request by institutions.

8. Eswatini enacted the Computer Crime and Cyber Crime Act 6 of 2022 and Data Protection Act 5 of 2022 to address offences committed through computer systems and electronic communications networks. The Act promotes and protects human rights by criminalising cyber-related offences, safeguarding privacy and personal data, protecting children from online exploitation, and strengthening cybersecurity. Further, the Acts have enhanced the protection of individuals and institutions from cybercrime, increased confidence in the digital environment, and facilitated the safe enjoyment of rights in the digital space while ensuring that law enforcement powers are exercised in accordance with the Constitution.
9. The establishment of the National Disability Advisory Council in 2023 plays a crucial role in advancing the rights and wellbeing of persons with disabilities. This institution contributes to the effective implementation of the Persons with Disabilities Act 16 of 2018.

Legal and institutional reforms (118.45, 118.46, 118.48, 118.49, 118.50, 118.51, 118.52, 118.53, 118.54, 118.55, 118.56, 118.57, 118.58, 118.115, 118.116)
15. The Government is expediting efforts towards the enactment of the Commission on Human Rights and Public Administration Bill 2025 and the Leadership Code of Conduct Bill of 2025, which seek to strengthen the mandate, independence, and operational capacity of the Commission on Human Rights and Public Administration. These Bills build on earlier iterations that were subjected to review and modifications over the past years to address emerging human rights standards and ensure consistency with governance frameworks. The Bills were introduced for debate in Parliament in March 2026 and April 2025, respectively.
16. Over the reporting period, the CHRPA continued to strengthen the promotion and protection of human rights through institutional capacity-building, public education, and strategic partnerships. The CHRPA trained more than 2,000 security personnel on human rights-compliant policing, including the lawful use of force, prevention of torture and illtreatment, ethical conduct, and constitutional safeguards, thereby promoting greater accountability and respect for human rights within law enforcement. It also expanded human rights awareness programmes through community dialogues and outreach initiatives, particularly targeting rural and underserved communities, while enhancing the capacity of traditional leaders to promote human rights and refer violations through appropriate mechanisms. In addition, the CHRPA strengthened collaboration with civil society organisations in implementing human rights programmes, community monitoring, documentation of violations, and advocacy, contributing to a more coordinated, inclusive, and effective national human rights protection system.
17. The Law Reform Unit has updated and revised statutes for the period 2023–2025, conducted research on and developed papers on the definition of reasonable provision under Section 29 (7) (b) and 34 (1) of the Constitution, which will inform law reform initiatives on the Administration of Estates and intestate succession.
18. As part of ongoing efforts to strengthen the independence and efficiency of the Judiciary, members of the Judicial Service Commission were appointed through Legal Notice of 2025. The reconstitution of the Commission has enhanced its capacity to discharge its constitutional mandate, including overseeing the appointment, discipline, and administration of judicial officers.
19. During the reporting period, the Judiciary continued to strengthen the administration of justice through the appointment of additional judicial officers. A total of 4 Superior Courts Judges (2 males and 2 females), 2 Principal Magistrates (both Females), 3 Senior Magistrates (2 males and 1 female), and 12 Ordinary Magistrates (7 males and 5 females) were appointed to augment the existing complement of judicial personnel, improve access to justice, reduce case backlogs, and enhance the timely delivery of judicial services.
20. In fighting corruption, the Anti-Corruption Commission (ACC) established in terms of section 3 of the Prevention of Corruption Act 3 of 2006, is an independent body mandated to prevent and eradicate corruption in all sectors of society. The Government continues to strengthen its institutional capacity and operational independence, and efforts to mobilise adequate resources remain ongoing.

Equality and non-discrimination (118.59, 118.76, 118.71, 118.72, 118.165, 118.170, 118.192, 118.171, 118.166, 118.211, 118.212, 118.213, 118.214, 118.168)
21. While CEDAW has not yet been fully incorporated into domestic law, the Government is progressively aligning national laws with the Convention’s provisions.
22. The Government recognises the need to repeal or amend legal provisions that perpetuate gender stereotypes and discrimination against women. To achieve this in a structured and coordinated manner, the Government developed the Programme of Action 2023–2029, which serves as the overarching national development framework. Under objective 5.3 – “Enhance gender and disability mainstreaming and inclusion across all sectors” – all government ministries are directed to review their respective pieces of legislation to promote the objects and purposes of CEDAW.
23. With specific reference to enabling women’s enjoyment of fundamental human rights, including the right to property and inheritance, the Government has prioritised the enactment of a suite of Family Law Bills. These include Marriages Bill 8 of 2022, Matrimonial Property Bill 9 of 2022, Draft Administration of Estates (Amendment) 2024, and Bills amending the Wills Act and Intestate Succession Act. Once enacted, these Bills will harmonise existing customary and statutory legal frameworks, eliminate discriminatory provisions that currently limit women’s capacity to own, inherit, and administer property, and bring domestic law into closer alignment with CEDAW.
24. To eliminate occupational segregation and ensure equal pay for equal work, Eswatini ratified ILO Conventions Nos. 100 and 111. To domesticate these, the Employment Act 5 of 1980 prohibits discrimination and enshrines equal pay, while the Industrial Relations Act (IRA) 1 of 2000 (as amended) requires employers to adopt non-discrimination policies. The Employment Bill further reinforces gender equality. Compliance is monitored through regular unannounced labour inspections, with persistent violations referred for prosecution. Remuneration discrepancies are not among the most common violations found, indicating general compliance. Nevertheless, the Government continues awareness-raising on gender stereotypes and encourages women's participation in non-traditional sectors through skills development and career guidance programmes, consistent with these recommendations.
25. The Government continues to ensure the protection of persons with albinism (PwA) and PWD. The Persons with Disabilities Act of 2018 and the National Disability Plan of Action (NDPA) 2024–2028 prohibit discrimination against PwA and PWD, requiring all ministries to mainstream disability and albinism-related needs across health, education, justice, and social protection systems. In partnership with stakeholders including the Stukie Foundation and UNFPA, public awareness has been advanced through annual commemorations of International Albinism Awareness Day on 13 June, as well as radio programmes that challenge stigma and promote inclusion. These commemorations integrate skin-health clinics and medical assessments, enabling early detection of skin cancer and other conditions. Visibility of the rights and needs of PwA has increased, coordination among Government and civil society has improved, and access to health services has expanded.
26. Regarding PWD, the NDPA 2024–2028 provides a coordinated national framework to advance the rights, inclusion, and dignity of PWD. Key achievements include the establishment of a multi-stakeholder national coordination mechanism, a costing and financing dialogue for disability-inclusive programming, and the integration of disability indicators into the Education Management Information System (EMIS), with over 500 education officers trained to improve identification and reporting of learners with disabilities.
27. Social protection coverage has expanded, with disability grant beneficiaries increasing from 14,802 to 17,500 in the 2024/25 fiscal year. Measures to reduce stigma and discrimination have focused on awareness-raising, disability mainstreaming within public institutions, and community engagement, underpinned by national handbooks and rightsbased advocacy. Public events have been leveraged as strategic platforms for social change, including job and entrepreneurship expos for PWD, music and art festivals, and participation in national cultural events such as Umhlanga Reed Dance, Emaganu, and Incwala, all used

28. Regarding discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity, the constitutional guarantee of equality before the law applies to all persons. The Government continues to engage with stakeholders to ensure that all individuals are protected from violence and discrimination. There have been no reported cases of people being violated or abused on the basis of their sexual orientation.

Human rights and climate change (118.197)
29. On the participation of vulnerable groups in climate change and disaster risk reduction, National Determined Contributions (NDC) 3.0 adopts an inclusivity and Just Transition approach, with dedicated targets for women, youth, and PWDs in climate governance bodies, alongside measures to remove participation barriers such as transport costs. The Ministry of Tourism and Environmental Affairs, in collaboration with DPMO, convened a Gender and Climate Change Integration Indaba to co-design gender-responsive and youth-inclusive actions for NDC 3.0.
30. A child-centred process enabled children and youth to develop recommendations and present them directly to policymakers through a Youth Consultation Forum. The NDC 3.0 process also strengthened protection for refugees and internally displaced persons, with commitments to develop inclusive multi-hazard early warning systems ensuring participation of women, children, and PWDs. The Disaster Risk Management Act 16 of 2025 was enacted through multi-sectoral stakeholder consultations, establishing a proactive, inclusive disaster risk framework.

Prohibition of torture and ill-treatment (118.79, 118.88, 118.89, 118.117, 118.110)

31. Eswatini's Constitution outlaws torture and ill-treatment of persons. Section 18 prohibits inhuman or degrading treatment, while Section 38(e) designates freedom from torture as non-derogable. Section 57(3) explicitly prohibits law enforcement officials from engaging in, encouraging, or tolerating torture under any circumstances.

32. These constitutional provisions are incorporated in the Correctional Services Act 13 of 2017, Police Service Act 22 of 2018, and Umbutfo Eswatini Defence Force Order 10 of 1977 (as amended). Complaints are investigated through Internal Complaints and Discipline Units or Panels of Enquiry. Eswatini does not have a specific torture law, but acts of torture are also prohibited under various pieces of legislation such as the Children Protection and Welfare Act 6 of 2012. The Commissions of Enquiry Act 35 of 1963 empowers investigations, with perpetrators referred for prosecution. The Inquests Act 59 of 1954 ensures transparent investigation of deaths in custody, and no deaths in detention were reported between 2021–2025.

37. In relation to investigations on the use of force during the June 2021 Civil Unrest, the Government assessed the events, including the operational response of law enforcement agencies. The circumstances on the ground resulted in casualties while these agencies were restoring public order, protecting lives, and safeguarding public and private property from widespread acts of violence, looting, arson, and vandalism.
38. In addition, the REPS undertook internal investigations and reviews of incidents involving the use of force during the unrest. These processes examined the circumstances surrounding law enforcement operations, including compliance with applicable laws, standard operational procedures, and rules governing the use of force.
39. The outcome of these reviews did not reveal evidence of unlawful conduct on the part of police officers in the discharge of their duties. The findings indicated that the measures employed were directed at restoring public order and preventing further loss of lives and destruction of property, and were considered necessary and proportionate in the circumstances prevailing at the time.

44. HMCS is finalising a Rehabilitation and Reintegration Policy to standardise rehabilitation programmes, strengthen alternatives to imprisonment, promote successful reintegration of offenders into society, reduce recidivism, and align correctional practices with international human rights standards.
45. On combating arbitrary arrests and ensuring fair trials, Section 16 of the Constitution prohibits arbitrary arrest and detention, permitting deprivation of liberty only in accordance with the law. Section 21 guarantees a fair trial within a reasonable time, preventing unlawful detention. Section 22 protects against arbitrary searches. Section 30 of the Criminal Procedure and Evidence Act 66 of 1938 requires that persons arrested without a warrant be produced before a court within 48 hours, unless impractical (e.g., weekend arrests).

Freedom of opinion and expression and access to information, peaceful assembly (118.98, 101,102, 105, 107, 108, 110, 111, 112, 114, 118, 220, 222)
46. Section 24 of the Constitution guarantees freedom of expression and opinion, while Section 25 guarantees peaceful assembly and association. Restrictions under Sections 24(3) and 25(3) must be prescribed by law and justifiable in a democratic society. Eswatini provides a safe environment for exercising civic space rights, with no individuals targeted. Section 60(2) recognises NGOs in the promotion and protection of human rights.
47. The media landscape is diverse, with outlets publishing critical views without censorship or reprisals, complemented by vibrant online discourse. In Prime Minister of

Human Trafficking and contemporary forms of slavery (118.90, 118.91, 118.92)
50. The Government allocates annual budgets under the Prime Minister's Office to implement the National Strategic Framework and Action Plan to Combat People Trafficking 2019-2023 across all pillars, supplemented by departmental budgets and development partner collaboration.
51. The Task Force against Trafficking in Persons has been strengthened through enhanced multi-agency coordination, comprising the Department of Labour, the National Children’s Services Department (NCSD), the DSW, the Department of Immigration, the Attorney General’s Chambers, the Director of Public Prosecutions (DPP), and REPS. A specialised Protection Committee and Tripartite Mechanism oversee victim identification, risk assessment, referrals, and placement services in line with the People Trafficking and People Smuggling (Prohibition) Act 7 of 2009.
Right to participation in public affairs (118.121, 118.125, 118.196, 118.199, 118.201, 118.198, 118.202)
52. The Elections and Boundaries Commission (EBC) is established by Section 90 of the Constitution and the Elections and Boundaries Commission Act No. 3 of 2013, which stipulates its independence, mandate and composition. The EBC’s functions include overseeing and supervising the registration of voters; ensuring fair and free elections; and reviewing the boundaries of tinkhundla areas (constituencies) for purposes of elections.
53. To strengthen participation in public affairs and decision-making processes, the EBC conducts civic and voter education programmes targeting diverse segments of the population through different platforms. These include community and relevant stakeholder outreach meetings, national events such as Umhlanga, Lusekwane and Buganu ceremonies targeting girls, boys and women respectively. The EBC also reaches the public through the international trade fair, regional career expos, national commemorations such as the International Day of PWDs and other public events. Further, the EBC utilises social media platforms and other digital communication channels to disseminate voter education materials, electoral information and updates, thereby expanding access to information, particularly among young people. All these platforms provide opportunities to engage on the importance of civic participation, voter registration and pursuing leadership positions.
54. In the 2023 General elections, efforts to meet the 30 per cent quota as contemplated under the Constitution have resulted in women holding 29 per cent of seats in the Parliament, with 25% held in the Cabinet. Four women were elected to Parliament under the mechanism provided by the Election of Women Members to the House of Assembly Act 2018, further advancing women’s representation in the national decision-making.

Violence against women (118.61, 118.164, 118.169, 118.176, 118.173, 118.181, 118.183, 118.185, 118.186, 118.187, 118.189, 118.193, 118.194, 118.195, 118.178, 118.182, 118.210, 118.188, 118.179, 118.175, 118.180, 118.190, 118.208, 118.184, 118.191)
57. As reported in the mid-term report, the Government continues to implement the discussed initiatives to reduce the incidence of violence against women.
58. The DPMO has adopted a formalised Gender-Based Violence (GBV) Coordination Mechanism, which provides a guide for improved effectiveness in prevention and response

A/HRC/WG.6/53/SWZ/1
by establishing common processes and ensuring greater accountability and partnership among all stakeholders.
59. At the national level, the coordination structures include the High-Level Task Force on Violence (HLTFV), composed of Principal Secretaries from key government ministries, which operates at the policy level to provide oversight and policy direction on violence response. The Director’s Forum on Violence (DFV) and the Multisectoral Technical Team on Violence (MTTV) consist of technical experts from government ministries, civil society organisations, faith-based organisations, and the traditional sector. At the regional level, four Regional Networks on Violence (RNV), similarly composed, operate across the four regions of the country. These task teams convene quarterly to deliberate on and report on all cases of GBV and violence against children, ensuring systematic information sharing and coordinated responses.
60. With respect to training for law enforcement, educators, and medical personnel, the Government has rolled out continuous capacity-building programmes for frontline responders, including police officers, prosecutors, magistrates, social welfare officers, and healthcare workers. Victim support services have been expanded through the establishment of one-stop centres and referral pathways, while accountability for perpetrators of violence is being pursued through the justice system, including the designation of specialised courts and fast-tracking of GBV cases.
61. Vulnerable women, including survivors of gender-based violence, are supported through the construction of houses, provision of agricultural inputs, and facilitation of business start-ups. This practice aims to reduce dependency on state support while equipping women with sustainable livelihood skills.
62. For survivors of gender-based violence specifically, the Government with the European Union and United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA), implements the Hlonipheka Thriving for Dignity Programme, which provides psychosocial support, counselling, and access to economic, educational, and medical services.
63. The Government has also identified and partnered with civil society organisations to host safe spaces, including FLAS, Kwakha Indvodza, the Moya Centre, and Sight of Hope. Women supported under this programme receive employability training in handicrafts, catering, curriculum vitae and proposal writing, and other technical skills, with graduation ceremonies recognising their achievements.
64. Currently, five safe houses are operational across the country, all of which are privately operated under the oversight and technical guidance of the Government. These shelters work in close collaboration with the DSW to provide emergency accommodation, counselling, legal referrals, and reintegration support. Furthermore, the Government is making efforts to establish a state-owned gender-based violence shelter.
Advancement of women (118.163, 118.200, 118.203, 118.204)
65. The Micro, Small and Medium Enterprise (MSME) Unit under the Ministry of Commerce, Industry and Trade is leading reforms to formalise the informal economy and create sustainable employment pathways. The National MSME Policy 2024–2029 promotes the formalisation of informal enterprises (mostly run by women) through simplified registration, improved compliance systems, and enhanced access to finance, markets, and business development services. This Policy enables unemployed citizens, including women and youth, to transition into entrepreneurship and structured economic participation.
66. Further, the Citizen Economic Empowerment (CEE) Act 2023 promotes MSME development and growth through the economic empowerment of citizen-owned companies. The MSME Unit facilitates MSME participation in public procurement and value chains, integrating informal operators into formal economic systems. A tailored financing model has been developed to improve access to credit for MSMEs by providing structured support for start-ups, working capital, and business expansion. This model has reduced lending risks for financial institutions, thereby increasing the availability of loans and allowing more businesses to expand. Consequently, enterprises have created sustainable employment

Right to social security (118.128, 118.131)

67. The National Social Security Policy (NSSP) of 2021 explicitly endorses ILO Recommendation No. 202 on social security floors and Sustainable Development Goal 1.3. In 2024/2025, the Government introduced a Social Security Inquiry tool used to map existing social protection schemes, assess benefit adequacy, and identify policy gaps and opportunities, particularly among informal economy workers. The tool also provides a mechanism for monitoring implementation of the NSSP.
68. Human rights and poverty (118.130, 118.132, 118.133, 118.43, 118.134, 118.135, 118.133)
74. The government implemented various measures aimed at reducing the poverty rate. The Eswatini Household Income and Expenditure Survey 2023/24 shows the national poverty rate declined from 58.9% (2016/17) to 53.5%. The poverty gap declined from 24.9% to 21.5%, and the poverty severity index from 13.0% to 10.9%, indicating that the living conditions of the poorest individuals improved and that extreme deprivation became less pronounced.
75. The Government developed the Government Programme of Action (PoA) 2024-2028 alongside the National Development Plan (NDP) 2023-2028 to move beyond the 2018–2023 roadmap. The NDP prioritises poverty reduction, food security, and inclusive economic growth. Both frameworks are designed to drive economic recovery and inclusive growth, with a strong emphasis on reducing disparities across society. Further, the Government has expanded social protection measures targeting vulnerable groups by fully operationalising One-Stop Business Facilitation Centres and streamlining business registration processes for local MSMEs, among other measures. To attract foreign direct investment, particularly in manufacturing and renewable energy, Special Economic Zones (SEZs) continue to offer 20year corporate tax exemptions and duty-free imports.
76. During the reporting period, the Regional Development Fund (RDF) supported 107 agricultural projects to enhance productivity and food security. These projects included provision of tractors and farm implements, cattle feedlots, poultry and piggery facilities, goat production, fencing and irrigation systems. A total of 2,161 community members directly benefited, with more than 60 per cent being women. The total value of these projects amounts to E126,694,250.21. These efforts are reinforced by the operationalization of Tinkhundla Service Centres, which bring essential government services, such as civil registration and business permits closer to rural communities, significantly reducing geographic disparities and travel costs.
77. Furthermore, the Eswatini Agricultural Development Fund (EADF), launched in 2023, provides agricultural financing products, input subsidies, infrastructure development and resource management. Since its establishment, the EADF has issued 64 direct loans benefiting 79 emaSwati, with total disbursement of E42.7 million, including fees and guarantees. Seventy-five beneficiaries have been supported under the Hamba Ubuye programme with E5 million, enabling cultivation of 487.24 hectares. The Fund has also absorbed initiation fees and management charges amounting to nearly E4.9 million, easing financial burdens on farmers.
78. The Youth Enterprise Revolving Fund approved loans worth over E4.5 million to beneficiaries in 2025, with E3.4 million already disbursed. A mentorship platform was established pairing experienced business mentors with youth entrepreneurs. The Government established regional arts centres and a recording studio to decentralise services. The Eswatini National Youth Council trained 115 youth on curriculum vitae development to improve their employability. The Eswatini Youth Employment Opportunities Project (EYEOP), supported by the World Bank and launched in 2025, aims to tackle youth unemployment through skills training, particularly in agriculture value chains, and entrepreneurship support, complementing the existing Eswatini Youth Empowerment Programme.
79. The National Financial Inclusion Strategy (2023–2028) is expanding access to formal economic participation, especially for workers in the informal sector. Reforms within the

Data collection and research (118.47)
81. Eswatini, through the Central Statistical Office (Statistics Office), has undertaken legislative, institutional and operational measures aimed at enhancing the national data systems. The Statistics Office is reviewing the Statistics Act 14 of 1967 to ensure that the legislative framework governing official statistics remains responsive to modern statistical requirements and in line with international best practices. A revised Statistics Bill has been formulated to establish a modern legal and institutional framework for statistical operations, enhance collaboration among government institutions, and promote data accessibility and confidentiality.
82. In addition, the Statistics Office adopted the Eswatini Strategy for the Development of Statistics (ESDS) 2026-2028 with the objective of aligning statistical priorities with national development goals and international reporting obligations, particularly the SDGs. The Strategy will guide the implementation of activities aimed at strengthening the effectiveness and responsiveness of the national statistical system.
83. The Statistics Office continues to prioritise the production of reliable, timely and disaggregated data (by gender, age, regions, etc) through the regular implementation of national surveys and censuses that support evidence-based policymaking, monitoring and evaluation of national development programmes.

87. Eswatini is implementing comprehensive HIV prevention measures, including the provision of free oral and long-acting pre-exposure prophylaxis, as well as post-exposure prophylaxis services for individuals at high risk. The country is among the first in Africa to introduce long-acting injectable prevention options. In addition, targeted HIV testing strategies and programmes for adolescent girls, young women, and key populations integrate biomedical, including condom distribution, behavioural, and structural interventions to address heightened vulnerability.

88. Eswatini has committed to reducing the extent of HIV stigma and discrimination biennially through the Stigma Index, which informs the levels of stigma and discrimination in the country.
89. Community and Health System Interventions:
• The Ministry of Health, with partners, mandated "Sensitivity and Stigma Reduction" training for nurses and clinicians. "Self-stigma" counselling is now integrated into Antiretroviral therapy initiation.
• In 2022/2023, guidelines were updated to strictly emphasise client-led referral (the patient approaches their contacts) rather than provider-led tracing without consent.

Improvement of education system (118.153, 118.156, 118.158, 118.159, 118.160, 118.162, 118.206, 118.214, 118.215)
105. The Government, through the Ministry of Education and Training (MoET) is implementing the Education Sector Strategic Plan (ESSP) 2022–2034, which provides a comprehensive framework for strengthening education delivery across all subsectors. The ESSP aligns education reforms with national development priorities and focuses on improving learning quality, learner retention, teacher development, system coordination and sustainable financing. The plan is operationalised through the Multi-Year Action Plan (MYAP) 2022–2025 and supported through a strengthened Sector-Wide Approach (SWAp), which enhances coordination among government institutions, development partners and education stakeholders.
106. To strengthen planning, monitoring and evidence-based decision-making, the MOET has transitioned to a DHIS2-based digital Education Management Information System (EMIS). The system decentralises data collection to schools and regional levels, enabling real-time data aggregation, analysis and monitoring across primary, secondary, Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) and tertiary education. The EMIS supports tracking of national education priorities, SDG 4 indicators and key reforms under the Competency-Based Education (CBE) framework.
107. The MOET is also implementing major curriculum and structural reforms in secondary education through the introduction of a four-year secondary education structure under the CBE framework. The reform promotes learner-centred, flexible and skills-oriented learning, reduces dropouts associated with examination-driven systems, and strengthens progression pathways to tertiary education, TVET, and employment opportunities. Expanded AS/A-Level pathways and investments in ICT infrastructure, digital learning platforms and blended learning models are further supporting equitable access to education, particularly for learners in remote and resource-constrained areas. Teacher training and continuous professional development programmes are also being strengthened to support effective implementation of the new curriculum.

114. IV. New and emerging issues, including advances and challenges
126. Eswatini has advanced the human rights of persons with disabilities through the operationalisation of the National Disability Advisory Council in 2023, enhancing the implementation of the Persons with Disabilities Act. The integration of human rights and climate action is a key emerging priority, reflected in the new Disaster Risk Management Act and the development of the NDC 3.0, which adopts an inclusivity and Just Transition approach to protect vulnerable populations. Technological advances present both opportunities and challenges, with the enactment of the Computer Crime and Data Protection Acts strengthening rights in the digital sphere while requiring ongoing vigilance. A significant challenge is the persistent prevalence of GBV, demanding sustained resources and multi-sectoral coordination. 127. Additionally, while the Government is implementing several measures to improve detention conditions and reduce overcrowding, correctional facilities continue to operate at capacity significantly exceeding approved limits, indicating a need for innovative alternative forms of sentencing and crime prevention campaigns. The education sector is undergoing substantial structural reforms through the introduction of the Competency-Based Education framework and the Education Sector Strategic Plan. However, this is challenged by resource constraints, rising input costs, and persistent barriers to equitable access, retention, and completion for all learners, particularly vulnerable groups such as adolescent mothers and learners with disabilities.
V. Challenges which would require the support of the international community
128. Eswatini continues to face capacity and resource constraints in fully implementing its human rights obligations and the accepted UPR recommendations. The Government requires technical and financial support for the full operationalisation of the Legal Aid Act, the Commission on Human Rights and Public Administration Bill, and the comprehensive Family Law Bills, which are critical for strengthening institutions and aligning domestic legislation with international standards such as CEDAW. 129. Sustained support is necessary to build capacity for treaty reporting, including the development of a robust National Recommendations Tracking Database to monitor implementation. As the Government strengthens its NMRF, the cooperation of development partners remains vital for its full operationalisation. Support from the international community is also crucial for addressing social and economic challenges such as poverty reduction and food security, enhancing social protection systems, and expanding universal access to quality healthcare, education, and clean water, particularly in rural and underserved areas.
