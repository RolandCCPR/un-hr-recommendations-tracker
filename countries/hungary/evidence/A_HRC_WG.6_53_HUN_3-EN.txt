Summary of stakeholders’ submissions on Hungary*
Report of the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights
I. Background
1. The present report was prepared pursuant to Human Rights Council resolutions 5/1 and 16/21, taking into consideration the periodicity of the universal periodic review and the outcome of the previous review.1 It is a summary of 22 stakeholders’ submissions2 for the universal periodic review, presented in a summarized manner owing to word-limit constraints.
II. Information provided by stakeholders
A. Scope of international obligations3 and cooperation with human rights mechanisms
2. The International Campaign to Abolish Nuclear Weapons (ICAN) recommended that Hungary signs and ratifies the UN Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons as a matter of international urgency.4 3. Joint Submission 10 recommended to withdraw the withdrawal instrument, or otherwise, re-ratify the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court. In the interim, it advised to initiate inclusive national consultations, involving parliamentarians, legal experts and civil society organizations on re-ratification of the Rome Statute and re-ratify the Agreement on Privileges and Immunities of the International Criminal Court.5
B. National human rights framework
Constitutional and legislative framework 4. OSCE/ODIHR recommended to revise the legal framework for elections and referendums to ensure better compliance with OSCE commitments and international standards. It also recommended to revise the electoral dispute resolution framework to guarantee that all voters have effective means to appeal against administrative decisions, including adopt measures to provide observation for all stages of the electoral process by

C. Promotion and protection of human rights
1. Implementation of international human rights obligations, taking into account applicable international humanitarian law
Equality and non-discrimination
5. Joint Submission 2 recommended to take effective measures to prevent and combat hate speech, racial discrimination, and violence against Roma, including public awareness campaigns and the vigorous prosecution of hate crimes.7
6. The Hungarian Atheist Association reported that while state schools are not allowed to discriminate, church schools are free to pick their pupils. Besides regular state funding per pupil, established churches receive additional funding but state schools do not. Kindergartens and primary schools of established churches have become a tool for segregating Roma and disadvantaged children and providing a Roma-free zone (with higher state funding) for parents who wish to keep their kids away from disadvantaged children. By consequence, local state schools may become schools at risk which further pushes parents towards enrolling their children in church schools even against their convictions. Therefore it recommended that state funds should be provided on transparent and equal terms for all religious and nonreligious providers and discrimination must be eliminated both at the systemic and the institutional level.8
Right to life, liberty and security of person, and freedom from torture
7. Joint Submission 6 recommended to abolish whole life imprisonment without the possibility of parole.9
International humanitarian law
8. Joint Submission 1 recommended that Hungary recognise the right to conscientious objection to military service for volunteers, professional members of the armed forces, including in state of emergency or war in accordance with international law and standards.10
9. The European Centre for Law and Justice (ECLJ) commended Hungary for its ongoing efforts to support persecuted Christians suffering around the world. The ECLJ highlighted the importance of Hungary's initiatives and encouraged the country to continue its work in providing assistance and advocacy for persecuted Christian communities.11
Administration of justice, including impunity, and the rule of law
10. In 2025, the mission of the European Committee for the Prevention of Torture and Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment (CPT) focused on conditions and treatment of persons held at Tiszalök and Szombathely National Prisons. Several prisoners reported that physical ill-treatment at Tiszalök prison had continued despite the Committee’s (CPT) 2023 findings. The CPT noted that the situation had substantially improved after a change of management at the end of 2024. At Szombathely prison some prisoners alleged physical illtreatment, disrespectful behavior and verbal abuse, including racist and homophobic remarks.12
11. CPT expressed serious concerns regarding the handling of ill-treatment allegations and related investigations. Criminal investigations into alleged prison staff misconduct, some dating back to 2020–2021, were still pending at the pre-trial stage, raising questions about the effectiveness and promptness of these procedures. CPT stressed that investigations should be thorough, comprehensive and expeditious.13

12. Hungary Helsinki Committee requested the effective implementation of the prerogatives of the National Judicial Council introduced by the 2023 judicial reform. Hungary Helsinki Committee required adequate safeguards, resources, and enforcement mechanisms. It called for concrete and effective measures to ensure that judges operate free from pressure or interference from the executive branch or any other external actors, in line with international standards.14
Fundamental freedoms and the right to participate in public and political life
13. Joint Submission 4 asked to repeal or substantially amend all emergency decrees and laws that unduly restrict public assemblies, including blanket bans on demonstrations and ensure that any future emergency measures are strictly necessary, time-limited, proportionate, and subject to effective parliamentary and judicial oversight.15
14. Joint Submission 4 informed that the government has adopted ad hoc legislative measures restricting freedom of assembly. In 2025, Hungary’s Parliament adopted, under an accelerated procedure and without meaningful consultation, Act III of 2025, amending Act LV of 2018 on the right of assembly (later reinforced by a constitutional amendment ), prohibiting Pride marches and any public assemblies deemed to violate Hungary’s antiLGBTQI+ “child protection” or “propaganda” laws.16
15. Sanctions included administrative fines of up to 200,000 HUF (approx. USD 610) for participants, criminal liability for organizers under the Criminal Code with penalties of up to one year of imprisonment, and the imposition of surveillance measures that significantly intrude on privacy. Under this law, the Hungarian regime treated the October 2025 Pécs Pride march as a “prohibited assembly” and relied on the resulting ban to target its principal organizer, Géza Buzás-Hábel, summoning him for police interrogation. The case has been referred to the prosecutor’s office with a recommendation to bring criminal charges for organizing and calling participation in a banned assembly, constituting a clear violation of the right to freedom of peaceful assembly.17
16. Joint Submission 4 recommended to protect, respect, and promote, without reservation, the rights of all individuals to freedom of peaceful assembly, association, and expression, and ensure that activists, journalists, teachers, students, LGBTQI+ persons can operate safely and without fear of retaliation.18
17. Joint Submission 5 requested Hungary to repeal Section 13/A of the Assembly Act and ensure freedom of assembly without discrimination and to immediately drop all criminal charges against the organizers of LGBTQI-related events. The same Joint Submission requested to formally withdraw the Transparency of Public Life bill to ensure that legislation does not restrict the lawful activities of civil society and ensure that LGBTQI organizations receiving international funding are not vilified and guarantee their equal access to domestic funding.19
18. In March 2025, the Council of Europe’s Commissioner for Human Rights addressed a letter to the Speaker of the Hungarian Parliament, requesting Members of Parliament to reconsider amendments to the Law on the right to assembly, which would effectively ban events like Pride marches. The Commissioner urged the Parliamentarians to refrain from adopting pending constitutional and other amendments that raised concerns as to their compatibility with human rights standards.20
19. In June 2025, the Commissioner expressed deep disappointment at the decision to prohibit the Budapest Pride March, due to take place on Saturday, 28 June, and called on the authorities to enable peaceful participants to march safely, without fear of reprisals, prosecution or violence. The Commissioner reiterated that law enforcement officers should guarantee a protective space for participants.21
20. Coordination des Associations et des Particuliers pour la Liberté de Conscience (CAP LC) recommended to amend procedural laws to ensure that in cases involving the data of religious communities, the parishioners are granted automatic standing to intervene and testify. CAP LC stressed that courts must be required to examine the actual content of disputed religious materials before ordering their destruction.22

21. CAP LC urged Hungary to incorporate protections into data protection regulations to shield confidential religious communications, including confessions and pastoral counselling, from State seizure.23
22. It called for objective, transparent, and non-discriminatory criteria for the recognition of religious organizations, removing the requirement for a two-third parliamentary vote which subjects religious freedom to political majority rule. CAP LC recommended to end political stigmatization and publicly retract discriminatory statements made by high-ranking officials regarding specific religious groups. It called for the Data Protection Authority to act independently without any political agenda to target minority beliefs.24
23. Joint Submission 6 requested effective safeguards against the misuse of surveillance technologies, including independent oversight, transparency, and access to effective remedies to protect the rights to freedom of assembly and association.25
Right to marriage and family life
24. Joint Submission 5 stressed that LGBTQI persons should have access individual adoption without discrimination and there should be marriage equality; while removing all discriminatory differences between registered partnership and marriage and providing legal recognition to same-sex couples who married abroad. Also, it urged to legally recognize same-sex parents on an equal basis with others and take measures to prevent discrimination against same-sex parents and their children in all spheres of life and remove discriminatory legislative restrictions for lesbian couples in accessing assisted reproduction technologies.26
Right to work and to just and favourable conditions of work
25. EUROMIL reported that by taking away trade union rights, soldiers were left without any collective voice or redress mechanism in employment-related matters, further compounding their vulnerability under the ongoing emergency regime. Thus, EUROMIL called on the Hungarian government to establish adequate mechanisms for collective bargaining for the Hungarian Defense Forces.27
26. EUROMIL argued that the lack of a defined maximum number of weekly working hours in the army can lead to unreasonably long hours, and the safeguards in such cases did not seem to exist. Moreover, there were no applicable overtime rules. EUROMIL urged the Hungarian government to take adequate measures to clarify the working time of the Hungarian Defence Forces personnel.28
27. ECLJ recommended that Hungary take adequate measures to clarify the working time of the Hungarian Armed Forces personnel, annul Article 45 (8) of the Fundamental Law of Hungary, establish adequate mechanisms for collective bargaining for the Hungarian Defence Forces and end limitation on exit rights.29
Right to social security
28. Joint Submission 2 recommended to collect and publish systematically disaggregated data by ethnicity in education, employment, housing, and health to enable evidence-based policy-making and transparent monitoring of progress toward Roma inclusion and to strengthen efforts to improve Roma access to social services, employment, healthcare, and housing without discrimination, in the framework of the National Social Inclusion Strategy 2030.30
Right to an adequate standard of living
29. The Hungarian Atheist Association stressed that those in need of housing and/or care are provided with state-funded services that come with no religious strings attached. Eligibility criteria for state funding (as well as quality control and transparency requirements) should be the same for any type of non-state non-profit operator.31
30. The Hungarian Atheist Association added that access to housing benefits and statefunded social assistance should be prioritised according to need (e.g. income in relation to poverty line) and not depend on marital or parental status.32

Right to health
31. Joint Submission 2 called for a comprehensive national mental health strategy for children and young people, with dedicated funding, clear targets, and mechanisms for monitoring and evaluation. Joint Submission 2 requested to increase the availability of school psychologists in all educational institutions, prioritizing schools in disadvantaged and rural areas where needs are greatest and expand outpatient child and youth psychiatric services across all counties and reduce waiting times for specialized mental health care.33
32. Joint Submission 2 also recommended to strengthen financial support mechanisms to help families access essential medicines and healthcare, particularly in the most economically disadvantaged communities and invest in the broader public health infrastructure serving children and young people, including better-equipped hospitals, increased recruitment and retention of specialist doctors, and concrete measures to reduce waiting times for medical care across all regions of the country.34
33. The Hungarian Atheist Association stressed that neither life-stance decisions, nor the financial situation of the individual should play a role in the access to any medical services, including contraception, abortion, sterilisation and assisted reproduction.35
34. Joint submission 7 stressed that inadequate access to healthcare was a systemic issue affecting foreign nationals in Hungary, particularly asylum seekers who lacked health insurance. Preventive screenings, routine check-ups, and surgical procedures were largely inaccessible without insurance coverage. In addition, lengthy waiting lists and language barriers impeded access to care for all foreign nationals. There were documented cases showing that immigration detainees had been denied access to prescribed medication, despite having established medical records within Hungary's electronic healthcare system, which contained their full medical history. In several cases, individuals who had received treatment for chronic psychiatric conditions over a period of years in Hungary were denied the same medication upon entry into detention. In other instances, alternative medication was administered without adequate clinical justification.36
35. Joint Submission 7 recommended that asylum seekers and foreign nationals be given access to essential healthcare, including check-ups, screenings, and surgeries, regardless of whether they hold insurance.37
Right to education
36. Joint Submission 2 requested equitable funding for all types of schools (state and church-run) and transparent resource allocation mechanisms to prevent the concentration of disadvantaged students in underfunded institutions.38
37. It encouraged the participation of students, teachers, and parents meaningfully in decisions that affect the education system, providing spaces for young people to express their concerns and contribute to shaping their own learning environments. In the same vein, Joint Submission 2 advised expanding access to vocational training and secondary education pathways, particularly in rural and disadvantaged areas, and making university education accessible to all regardless of socioeconomic background.39
38. Scholars at Risk urged Hungary to commit publicly to restoring academic freedom and related human rights, including by protecting scholars and students from retaliation, ensuring fair employment conditions, due process guarantees, and prohibiting ideological or political interference in academic content, evaluation, or appointments. Scholars at Risk advised to safeguard the independence of research institutions and civil society by halting reorganizations, such as foundation transfers or institutional mergers, that undermine academic integrity and by ensuring a safe environment for public debate and scientific work.40
Cultural rights
39. Joint Submission 3 recommended effective measures to prevent and address harassment, intimidation, and undue pressure against artists and cultural workers, including those critical of government policies; and ensure that no individual is subjected to

surveillance, legal sanctions, or other forms of repression for participating in cultural or artistic expression, including in connection with public events or assemblies.41
40. Joint Submission 3 urged to refrain from stigmatizing or discrediting dissenting artistic expression, including works addressing LGBTI themes, and ensure that artists and cultural actors are protected from discrimination, public vilification, and unequal treatment on the basis of the content of their work or their perceived views.42
41. Joint Submission 3 requested that public funding for the arts and cultural sector is allocated in a transparent, independent, and non-discriminatory manner, and asked to refrain from using financial mechanisms to disadvantage or exclude artists and cultural actors on the basis of their views or the content of their work.43
42. Joint Submission 4 asked to ensure that public funding and governance of cultural institutions are administered through independent, transparent, and non-discriminatory processes, by establishing safeguards to prevent political interference in funding allocations, leadership appointments, and cultural programming, and ensuring that artists and cultural institutions are not disadvantaged on the basis of their work and expressed viewpoints.44
2. Rights of specific persons or groups
Women
43. Joint Submission 9 recommended to ratify the Council of Europe Convention on Preventing and Combating Violence against Women and Domestic Violence (the Istanbul Convention) and to adopt a comprehensive national strategy/action plan for its implementation with concrete measures, responsible actors, deadlines, and budget allocated.45
44. Joint Submission 9 urged to duly address femicide and take legislative and other measures, including effective risk assessment and management to prevent such cases. Joint Submission 9 encouraged Hungary to review the committed cases, in view of the accountability of authorities and institutions concerned, and to identify necessary measures that have to be taken to improve the state and authorities ’response to guarantee due diligence.46
45. JS9 called for meaningfully involving women’s rights NGOs in drafting, reviewing and modifying the laws and policies addressing violence against women and domestic violence, and for the development of services for the victims.47
46. Joint Submission 9 also requested the monitoring of systems for maternal health and rights violations and feedback channels and timely compensation mechanisms for patients whose rights have been violated to ensure fair redress without undue financial or procedural barriers.48
47. Finally Joint Submission 9 recommended to address disparities and discrimination affecting Romani women, migrants, low-income groups, remote and other marginalized, underserved populations.49
Children
48. Joint Submission 1 requested that children not be subjected to military training that involves the use of firearms, and that all forms of advertising and marketing for military service targeted at children, in particular at schools, be prohibited. In this context, Joint Submission 1 urged to implement the recommendations of the Committee on the Rights of the Child to develop mechanisms for the early identification of asylum-seeking, refugee and migrant children who may have been recruited or used in hostilities abroad, to conduct training for personnel responsible for the identification and referral of such children to protection services, and to provide child victims with appropriate assistance for their full physical and psychological recovery and social reintegration.50
49. Joint Submission 2 recommended to improve the targeting of social transfers and family support programs to ensure they reach families with the greatest need, prioritizing children and households in severe material deprivation and strengthen and expand social

housing and housing assistance programs for families in vulnerable conditions, so children have access to safe and dignified living conditions.51
50. Joint Submission 2 urged adequate provision of food, school supplies, and hygiene products for children from families in the most vulnerable conditions, removing financial barriers to school participation.52
51. Joint Submission 2 also advised developing and implementing a multi-stakeholder national strategy to prevent and address all forms of violence against children, including sexual abuse, trafficking, domestic violence, and online exploitation and establishing a specialized, adequately resourced framework for identifying and assisting child victims of trafficking and abuse. Joint Submission 2 recommended strengthening child protection services and ensuring adequate funding for organizations providing support to children at risk, including those experiencing abuse, homelessness, and family breakdown.53
52. Joint Submission 2 suggested that to improve public safety and child protection, the Government of Hungary should renew and adopt a comprehensive National Drug Strategy with adequate funding, prioritizing evidence-based prevention, treatment, and harm reduction programs, particularly for those targeted at young people and marginalized communities. Joint Submission 2 encouraged investment in effective drug prevention education programs in schools that go beyond awareness-raising to develop life skills and resilience, drawing on evidence-based methodologies.54
Persons with disabilities
53. Accessibility Atlas (AA) recommended that Hungary develop and implement a comprehensive national accessibility strategy that mandates the systematic assessment and upgrade public buildings, transportation systems, and public spaces. This should include ensuring step-free access through ramps and elevators, expanding accessible public transport options, and allocating targeted funding to underserved municipalities. AA encouraged the enforcement of accessibility standards for all new construction and renovation projects, and the meaningful inclusion of persons with disabilities and their representative organizations in planning, monitoring, and evaluation processes.55
54. Accessibility Atlas also recommended that Hungary ensure full compliance with digital accessibility standards by conducting comprehensive audits of public and essential private sector platforms, including government websites, banking services, and transport systems. This should include requiring accessible design features such as screen reader compatibility, captioned and sign language-supported media, and simplified content formats. AA encouraged strengthening enforcement mechanisms, capacity-building for institutions and service providers, and the active involvement of people with disabilities in evaluating and improving digital accessibility systems.56
Minorities
55. Joint Submission 2 recommended the development of targeted programs to reduce intergenerational poverty, particularly in the most disadvantaged micro-regions, with special attention to Roma and other communities living in marginalized conditions.57
56. To address discrimination against the Roma community, Joint Submission 2 recommended to strengthen efforts to improve Roma access to social services, employment, healthcare, and housing without discrimination, in the framework of the National Social Inclusion Strategy 2030.58
57. JS2 also requested that the Government of Hungary enforce strict anti-segregation policies in all educational institutions, ensure the effective implementation of existing legal provisions against segregation, and take concrete measures to desegregate schools and classes where Roma children are separated from their peers.59
58. JS2 called for a reform to strengthen the assessment procedures used to identify children with special educational needs, introducing robust safeguards against the culturally biased misdiagnosis of Roma children, and ensuring that placement in special education is based solely on individual educational needs and not on ethnic or socioeconomic factors, in line with the ECtHR ruling in Horváth and Kiss v. Hungary.60

59. JS 2 advised collecting and publishing systematically disaggregated data by ethnicity in education, employment, housing, and health to enable evidence-based policy-making and transparent monitoring of progress toward Roma inclusion.61
Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender and intersex persons
60. Joint Submission 5 recommended to take immediate measures to prevent antiLGBTQI hate crimes and violence and ensure that all reported hate crimes are effectively recorded, investigated and prosecuted, and that victims are encouraged to report incidents.62
61. Joint Submission 5 also recommended to improve the quality of and access to, victim support and legal aid services for hate crime victims by training professionals, decreasing bureaucratic barriers, and providing funding to civil society organisations with services for hate crime victims.63
62. Other recommendations from Joint Submission 5 included mandating all public and private employers to adopt equal opportunity plans and guidelines covering the needs of LGBTQI+ employees and developing programs to improve the employability of trans persons.64
63. Joint Submission 6 recommended ensuring the rights, safety and dignity of LGBTQI+ detainees, including by respecting gender identity in placement and searches and preventing isolation and discrimination.65
64. Joint Submission 5 requested to immediately repeal the amendments introduced by Act LXXIX of 2021, the so-called ‘propaganda’ or anti-LGBTQI law.66
Migrants, refugees and asylum-seekers
65. Joint Submission 8 recommended to adopt and implement an integration strategy for migrants and establish a comprehensive system supporting the integration of migrants. It added that migrant victims of domestic violence should receive adequate protection, including stability of residence and those victims of trafficking in human beings for labour exploitation, ensure access to existing residence rights.67
66. Joint Submission 1 recommended to take measures to guarantee the right to asylum and international protection for conscientious objectors to military service who are at risk of persecution in their country of origin owing to their refusal to perform military service when there is no provision, or no adequate provision, for conscientious objection to military service.68
67. Joint Submission 4 called to end the state of crisis due to mass migration and ensure that emergency powers are not used to restrict the rights of migrants and asylum seekers in a discriminatory manner.69
68. The same Joint Submission reiterated the need/obligation to fully respect for the rights of migrants, asylum seekers, refugees, and dual nationals in accordance with international human rights and refugee law, including the principles of non-discrimination and nonrefoulement. It advised to repeal legislation authorizing the forced return of migrants and asylum seekers to border transit zones or external territories without individualized assessments of protection needs, ending the practice of pushbacks at Hungary’s borders and ensuring that all individuals have access to fair and effective asylum procedures in accordance with international law.70
69. Joint Submission 4 recommended to end the practice of pushbacks at Hungary’s borders and ensure that all individuals have access to fair and effective asylum procedures in accordance with international law.71
Stateless persons
70. Joint Submission 4 called to repeal Act XLIX of 2025 on the Suspension of Hungarian Citizenship and relevant constitutional amendments permitting the suspension of Hungarian citizenship of dual nationals, in order to protect citizenship rights, prevent discrimination based on nationality status, and ensure compliance with international human rights standards.72
