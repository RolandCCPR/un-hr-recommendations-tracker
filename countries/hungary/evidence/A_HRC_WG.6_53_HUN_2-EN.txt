I. Background
1. The present report was prepared pursuant to Human Rights Council resolutions 5/1 and 16/21, taking into consideration the outcome of the previous review.1 It is a compilation of information contained in relevant United Nations documents, presented in a summarized manner owing to word-limit constraints.
II. Scope of international obligations and cooperation with human rights mechanisms
2. The United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) recommended ratifying the Optional Protocol to the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights.2
III. National human rights framework
3. The Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion or belief reported that the Commissioner for Fundamental Rights had received A status accreditation from the Global Alliance of National Human Rights Institutions in 2014 but had been downgraded to B status in 2021, meaning that it could not vote or hold governance positions in the Global Alliance. This had been due to its failure to demonstrate its independence and its inability to address human rights issues adequately or to refer those issues to the Constitutional Court. The Commissioner for Fundamental Rights had objected to the downgrading of its accreditation status.3 4. Accordingly, the same Special Rapporteur advised conducting a thorough review of the current functioning and effectiveness of the Hungarian national human rights institution. That recommendation was made following the integration of various rights protection bodies into the Commissioner for Fundamental Rights.4 5. The same Special Rapporteur stressed that the review of the Hungarian national human rights institution would demonstrate functional independence and autonomy, ensuring compliance, addressing human rights issues by referring them to the Constitutional Court and conducting inquiries into the activities of all other government entities. That measure would

6. The Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women recommended that Hungary strengthen the Office of the Commissioner for Fundamental Rights by providing it with adequate human, technical and financial resources to discharge its mandate effectively, independently and in compliance with the principles relating to the status of national institutions for the promotion and protection of human rights (the Paris Principles). It also recommended supporting the Commissioner in implementing the recommendations of the Subcommittee on Accreditation of the Global Alliance of National Human Rights Institutions.6
1. Constitutional and legislative framework
7. The Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women advised Hungary to adopt, with a clear time frame, comprehensive anti-discrimination legislation, covering direct and indirect discrimination in the public and private spheres, as well as intersecting forms of discrimination, in line with articles 1 and 2 of the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women, the Committee’s general recommendation No. 28 (2010) and target 5.1 of the Sustainable Development Goals, on ending all forms of discrimination against all women and girls everywhere.7
8. The Special Rapporteur on the right to education recommended revising the Public Education Act to eliminate all discriminatory provisions regarding the education of children with disabilities.8
9. The Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) recommended that Hungary amend its national legislation to safeguard the right to seek and enjoy asylum and the prohibition of refoulement.9
2. Institutional infrastructure and policy measures
10. The Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion urged equal protection (including child protection), accountability and financial transparency in church-run services, whether in education, health, child protection or other social institutions.10
IV. Promotion and protection of human rights
A. Implementation of international human rights obligations, taking into account applicable international humanitarian law
1. Equality and non-discrimination
11. The Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion recommended that non-State actors, including religion or belief actors, place non-discrimination, compassion and solidarity at the heart of their orientation and actions and hold on to their neutrality and independence, while seeking to contribute to all human rights for all, in collaboration with other civil society actors.11
12. UNHCR urged Hungary to address discrimination and xenophobia, in particular against refugees and asylum-seekers, and to promote tolerance through campaigns and public messaging.12
13. The Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion encouraged Hungary to strengthen efforts to eradicate stereotyping and discrimination, inter alia, the demonization and stigmatization of LGBTIQ+ persons, persons who were unemployed, persons living in poverty, human rights defenders and journalists, and those advancing the rights of migrants.13
14. The same Special Rapporteur recommended reviewing all laws, legislation and practices in order to identify provisions, processes and procedures that might perpetuate discrimination based on religion or belief, noting that that required an end to all distinction, exclusion, restriction or preference based on religion or belief.14

15. While noting that the Government had joined two recent international statements to combat anti-Muslim hatred, the Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion noted with concern that anti-Muslim hatred, or Islamophobia, and discrimination against Muslims remained widespread in Hungarian society. United Nations human rights mechanisms had continued to raise concerns about reported discrimination, xenophobia and intolerance, including hate crimes and online hate speech, against Muslims and other religious or belief minorities.15
16. The same Special Rapporteur noted that, regrettably, the pattern of anti-Muslim rhetoric had also stemmed from high-level officials, especially in linking strong anti-migrant rhetoric with anti-Muslim hatred, defending “Christian Europe” against Muslim immigration. According to the Special Rapporteur, it had been reported that, while physical assaults were rare, verbal insults were frequent, against women wearing headscarves, persons with darker skin and persons speaking foreign languages.16
17. The same Special Rapporteur reported that, even though Hungary had declared a zerotolerance policy on antisemitism, surveys demonstrated its prevalence across the country, often as coded hate speech, revealing deep-seated prejudice and discriminatory attitudes towards Jews. Incidents of glorification, by State officials and on social media, of historical figures associated with antisemitism also persisted in the country.17
18. The Special Rapporteur on education encouraged the recruitment of Roma teachers and mediators and measures to combat discrimination and segregation in education.18
2. Administration of justice, including impunity, and the rule of law
19. The Special Rapporteur on the promotion and protection of the right to freedom of opinion and expression recommended that Hungary introduce legislation to counter strategic lawsuits against public participation, in line with European Union recommendations to protect journalists from vexatious lawsuits, and that it repeal the provision in the Criminal Code relating to defamation as a criminal offence.19
20. The same Special Rapporteur requested that Hungary enforce the decision of the European Court of Human Rights in the case of Szabó and Vissy v. Hungary and, as a result, ensure that acts of surveillance were subject to independent oversight and judicial review, were conducted in accordance with international human rights principles of legality and necessity and did not result in undue restrictions to freedom of opinion and expression.20
21. The Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion recommended taking effective measures to improve reporting, investigation, prosecution and punishment of religiously motivated hate crimes, including hate speech and physical attacks against Muslims, Jews and Roma, in accordance with its obligations under the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, whether the actors were State officials or actors or not.21
22. The same Special Rapporteur advised developing action plans to effectively combat antisemitism, anti-Muslim hatred and other intolerance and hatred in Hungary, including hate crimes targeted at women and hateful discrimination on social media and in political rhetoric that risked becoming normalized and institutionalized.22
23. The Special Rapporteur on freedom of expression recommended that Hungary publicly denounce and prosecute all instances of hate speech, stigmatization and discrimination against such vulnerable groups as Roma, LGBT persons, asylum-seekers and migrants and those working for them, including by strengthening efforts to eradicate stereotyping and discrimination and by providing training to law enforcement officials to tackle violence, threats and harassment, both online and offline.23
24. The Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women requested women’s and girls’ access to justice, including through procedural accommodations, and the reporting of gender-based violence to law enforcement bodies through affordable and, if necessary, free legal assistance. The Committee encouraged relaxing the burden of proof for complainants, ensuring affordable access to forensic evidence and continuing to build, as a mandatory part of their professional training, the capacity of judges, prosecutors, the police and other law enforcement officers on gender-sensitive investigation and interrogation methods.24

25. The same Committee advised raising awareness among women and girls, including in rural areas and among Roma women, women with disabilities and older women, about the legal remedies available to claim violations of their rights.25
3. Fundamental freedoms and the right to participate in public and political life
26. The Special Rapporteur on freedom of expression recommended that Hungary adopt a legal and policy framework, through an inclusive multi-stakeholder process, to promote media independence and pluralism.26
27. The same Special Rapporteur also recommended reviewing the decision not to renew the broadcasting licence of Klubradio, taking into account the importance of maintaining media diversity.27
28. The same Special Rapporteur further recommended that Hungary revise the appointment procedure for the Chair of the Media Council to make it more transparent and inclusive in order to reflect the full range of the political spectrum and the media community.28
29. The same Special Rapporteur called for the independence, transparency and accountability of the Media Council, including through legislative reforms, to redistribute the concentration of power currently in the hands of the Chair of the Media Council.29
30. The same Special Rapporteur urged Hungary to take measures to reduce bias in news coverage and enhance diversity of views, including by reforming the governance and management of public service media to ensure that all media outlets, including those critical of the Government, were able to access and impart information freely.30
31. The same Special Rapporteur also urged Hungary to protect the safety of journalists, and the privacy and security of their communications and sources, in line with the recommendation of the Committee of Ministers of the Council of Europe, and to launch an independent investigation into the alleged use of Pegasus spyware against journalists and human rights defenders.31
4. Right to privacy
32. The Special Rapporteur on freedom of expression stated that, according to article VI of the Fundamental Law, exercising freedom of expression and freedom of assembly was not to result in any violation of private and family life or of the home of others. While recognizing the importance of upholding the right to privacy, the Special Rapporteur stated that the restriction of freedom of expression in article VI was overly broad and should be interpreted in line with the country’s obligations under article 19 (3) of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, as well as the general provision of article I (3) of the Fundamental Law.32
33. The same Special Rapporteur added that the right to privacy could not be used as a ground to restrict the right of the media to publish information that could be relevant to matters of public interest, for example in relation to public figures.33
34. The same Special Rapporteur noted that, despite the decision of the National Authority for Data Protection and Freedom of Information that the use of drone recordings to report on public spending was an issue of public interest and did not violate legitimate privacy interests, a journalist had been sued in the national courts.34
5. Right to marriage and family life
35. UNESCO recommended that Hungary amend its legislation to ensure that the minimum age of marriage was 18 years, without exceptions.35
36. The Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women encouraged Hungary to establish a clear time frame for adopting the national strategy and action plan on the prevention of child and forced marriage. Recalling its previous recommendations, the Committee recommended that Hungary review the Civil Code to remove all exceptions to the legal minimum age of marriage of 18 years for both women and men and pursue its efforts

to combat child and forced marriage, including by addressing the root causes of this harmful practice, encouraging the reporting of cases and ensuring the prosecution and adequate punishment of those responsible.36
37. The same Committee reiterated that women and men had the same rights and responsibilities in marriage and family relations, including in registered partnerships, and upon their dissolution, including the same parental rights in cases of adoption, irrespective of their marital status, in accordance with article 16 of the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women.37
6. Prohibition of all forms of slavery, including trafficking in persons
38. Recalling its general recommendation No. 38 (2020) on trafficking in women and girls in the context of global migration, and its previous recommendations, the Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women recommended that Hungary ensure sufficient human, technical and financial resources for the effective implementation and monitoring of the National Strategy against Trafficking in Human Beings for 2020–2023. It also recommended that Hungary increase awareness and build the capacity of the judiciary, law enforcement officers, border control officials, social workers and health workers to identify, protect and refer women and girls who were victims of trafficking to appropriate services.38
7. Right to work and to just and favourable conditions of work
39. The Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women recommended that Hungary refocus its employment policies on gender equality and ensure that they were based on results, measurable indicators, partnerships with the private sector and opportunities for professional training in all areas, including innovative sectors such as the information and communications technology sector.39
8. Right to social security
40. The Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women noted with concern that women and households headed by women were disproportionately affected by poverty and that women’s pensions and social benefits were, on average, lower than those received by men.40
9. Right to health
41. The Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion urged access to reproductive health services for all without discrimination.41
10. Right to education
42. UNESCO recommended that Hungary enshrine the right to education in a comprehensive manner, for all and not limited to certain levels of education, in the Constitution and in its legislation, and legally proscribe discrimination in education in its legislation.42
43. UNESCO encouraged efforts to ensure the right to inclusive education for minorities and refugees.43
44. The Special Rapporteur on education praised the commitment of Hungarian educators and civil society, while expressing serious concern about widening inequalities, curriculum rigidity, the marginalization of Roma students and the erosion of academic freedom.44
45. The same Special Rapporteur encouraged the new Government to facilitate an open, pluralistic and competition-based academic system in which professional courage once again replaced self-censorship. Restoring academic freedom was an essential element of the broader reform agenda necessary to rebuild trust in democratic institutions and to re-establish constructive relations with the European Union and international human rights mechanisms.45
46. The same Special Rapporteur welcomed the provision of free primary and secondary education and targeted support for students with special needs. However, she warned of a

“hollowing out” of the public education system, with growing inequalities between underresourced State schools and better-funded church-run institutions.46
47. The same Special Rapporteur warned that, as more schools became non-public, particularly in remote areas, public schools would be left with fewer resources and a higher concentration of disadvantaged students, leading to increased segregation of Roma children. She emphasized that non-public education must always support public education, not replace it.47
48. The same Special Rapporteur highlighted the rigid curriculum, which emphasized memorization over critical thinking and left little room for interactive learning. She also expressed concern about high levels of student anxiety and limited access to school psychologists. She acknowledged that a more student-centred approach was needed, one that promoted well-being and recognized teachers as pillars of the education system.48
49. The same Special Rapporteur recommended adjusting the strict, punitive attendance monitoring approaches to encompass stronger preventive and supportive measures that addressed the underlying causes of absenteeism, including poverty, family hardship, discrimination, mental health challenges and disengagement from learning.49
50. The same Special Rapporteur urged Hungary to build on its strong foundations and renew its commitment to inclusive, quality education for all and stated that the future of the country’s children and of its democracy depended on it.50
51. The same Special Rapporteur advised raising the compulsory school age back to 18, in line with the global trend to progressively universalize and expand access to secondary education.51
52. The same Special Rapporteur requested a thorough analysis of the root causes of school dropout, particularly among disadvantaged groups, and targeted measures to prevent early school leaving among Roma children, children with disabilities and children living in poverty, including scholarships, conditional social support, free school meals, transportation assistance and mentoring programmes.52
53. On equality and non-discrimination in education, the same Special Rapporteur recommended strengthening the public education system as the primary guarantor of the right to inclusive, equitable and quality education for all in a safe environment, regardless of gender or other identities, while ensuring that the expansion of church-run schools did not contribute to social stratification or unequal access to educational opportunities.53
54. In line with the internationally recognized aims of education, the same Special Rapporteur encouraged the Government to ensure equitable public financing across school types, to prevent selective admission practices that disadvantaged marginalized learners and to adopt safeguards against socioeconomic, ethnic or academic segregation. Additional priority investment should be directed towards public schools serving disadvantaged communities, including through improved teacher salaries and incentives.54
55. The same Special Rapporteur recommended early warning and re-engagement systems to identify students at risk of dropout and facilitate their return to education through flexible learning pathways, second-chance education and individualized support, in line with the commitments under the UNESCO Education 2030 Framework for Action for the Implementation of Sustainable Development Goal 4.55
56. The Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion stated that church-run schools should, inter alia, offer ethics as an alternative and not make their faith classes mandatory and that they should ensure that admission was not denied de facto to Roma children, children with disabilities or children who held other religions or beliefs.56
B. Rights of specific persons or groups
1. Women
57. The Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women urged Hungary to uphold the human rights of women and girls in all their diversity, the rule of law and the

independence of the judiciary. It recommended taking measures to address anti-gender public discourse and expanding capacity-building on gender equality for government officials, parliamentarians, leaders of political parties and religious movements, and public and private media professionals.57
58. The same Committee recommended expanding the availability of gender studies in public universities and ensuring broad and participatory consultation and collaboration with civil society, in particular with women’s rights organizations, in the formulation, adoption and implementation of legislation, policies and programmes in relation to the elimination of discrimination against women and the promotion of gender equality.58
59. The same Committee was deeply concerned about retrogressive movements seeking to exclude reference to gender from the political, educational and social discourse, and that the equality policy was based exclusively on a concept of the family in which the role of a woman was primarily viewed as that of wife and mother. It noted with concern the rise in stereotypical attitudes about the roles and responsibilities of women and men in the family and in society, as well as the current unavailability of gender studies in public universities.59
60. The same Committee recommended targeted measures, including temporary special measures, to ensure access to justice, employment and healthcare, including sexual and reproductive health services, social protection and food security for disadvantaged groups of women such as rural women, older women, women with disabilities, women belonging to ethnic minority groups and refugee, asylum-seeking and migrant women, taking into account their specific needs.60
61. Recalling its general recommendation No. 35 (2017), the same Committee recommended that Hungary adopt a comprehensive law on gender-based violence against women, which included a definition of gender-based violence against women, measures and policies aimed at its elimination, and institutions responsible for its implementation and coordination mechanisms, and which took into account the special protection needs of disadvantaged and marginalized groups of women, including Roma women, women with disabilities and lesbian, bisexual, transgender and intersex women.61
62. The same Committee requested that Hungary amend discriminatory provisions to ensure the timely and effective issuance, enforcement and monitoring of protection orders, including, where appropriate, expulsion orders, in cases of domestic violence, and impose adequate deterrent penalties for non-compliance with such orders.62
63. The same Committee asked Hungary to repeal provisions on mandatory mediation in cases of child custody and visitation rights, to introduce a legal requirement to examine the history of violence in such cases, to remove the obligation to pay mediation procedure fees for “non-cooperation”, to give priority to criminal proceedings over mediation and reconciliation in cases involving gender-based violence against women and to encourage reporting of all forms of gender-based violence against women and girls, including domestic and sexual violence, without fear of reprisals, stigmatization or revictimization.63
2. Children
64. The Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion reported that some 74 per cent of child protection care was church-run. Although the law required employers to check whether new employees were listed on the sex offenders’ register, church employees were reportedly exempt from that requirement, meaning that children in church-run care received less protection. The supervision of the networks of foster parents had also changed from being State-run to being church-run. Most were run by the child protection service of the Catholic diocese of Szeged-Csanád and the Saint Agatha Foundation, and some by the Reformed Church.64
65. The Hungarian Catholic Church had reportedly appointed a church official for child protection in at least one diocese and had introduced a course on child protection into the

3. Persons with disabilities
66. The Special Rapporteur on education recommended providing comprehensive and focused training for general education teachers and administrative staff on the principles and methods of inclusive education, the capacities of children with disabilities and the individualized support measures required by such children, and creating incentives for teachers specializing in working with children with special education needs.66
67. The same Special Rapporteur advised increasing targeted investment in trained professionals, including therapists, sign language interpreters and school psychologists, involving equal opportunity experts in cases affecting Roma and marginalized groups, publishing a national list of schools and services for children with special educational needs to help families make informed choices and guaranteeing access to regular education facilities and transportation for children with physical disabilities in their communities across the country.67
4. Minorities
68. The Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion noted with concern the historical discrimination against Roma people in education, with 51 per cent of Roma children dropping out while still in compulsory school. Government officials had blamed Roma children for lowering State exam results, citing “integration difficulties” as the cause; however, it was claimed that Roma children were disproportionately placed in special education and were victims of an education system that failed to support them.68
69. The Special Rapporteur on education advised that Hungary collect and publish disaggregated data on the enrolment, attendance and dropout rates of Roma at all levels of education to monitor the extent and the forms of segregation. She recommended enforcing strict anti-segregation policies to prevent Roma students from being wrongly placed in special education and ensuring equal access to quality education, as had also been stressed by the Deputy Commissioner for Fundamental Rights.69
70. The same Special Rapporteur also recommended that Hungary adopt measures to monitor and to eradicate effectively inter- and intra-school segregation of Roma children and provide effective incentives for integrated education to ensure that segregated pupils were mainstreamed into the regular school system without delay.70
5. Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender and intersex persons
71. Several special procedure mandate holders referred to concerning criminal investigations against the organizer of Pécs Pride, Géza Buzás-Hábel. They reiterated strong concerns about the legislative amendments introduced in March 2025, which effectively banned LGBTIQ-themed assemblies in Hungary.71
72. Several special procedure mandate holders reiterated the legal obligation of the Government to ensure that the development, adoption and implementation of all legislation was without discrimination based on any prohibited ground, including on the basis of sexual orientation or gender identity. They urged the parliament of Hungary to repeal the recently enacted Amendment of Act LV of 2018 on the Right of Assembly related to the protection of children and related acts and to reject the bills currently under consideration, including the Fifteenth Amendment to the Fundamental Law of Hungary.72
73. In March 2025, OHCHR expressed deep concern about legislation passed that had resulted in arbitrary and discriminatory restrictions on the rights of LGBTIQ+ individuals to freedom of expression, peaceful assembly and privacy, including when advocating for their human rights at events such as Pride parades, and about the use of surveillance measures to identify and fine persons peacefully participating in such assemblies. The United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights urged authorities to repeal the law and other legislation that discriminated against LGBTIQ+ individuals and to combat the high levels of

intolerance, discrimination, bullying and harassment related to sexual orientation and gender identity, including faced by children, in line with the international human rights obligations of Hungary.73
74. The Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women noted with concern that registered partnerships were not recognized under the joint and second parent adoption scheme, which created legal, administrative and practical barriers for children living with same-sex parents and disproportionately affected lesbian and bisexual women.74
6. Migrants, refugees and asylum-seekers
75. The Special Rapporteur on freedom of expression acknowledged that, while human rights defenders, especially those working on the rights of LGBT individuals or migrants, were reportedly given limited or no opportunities to appear in the media and express their views and concerns, homophobic and sometimes violent groups were said to be given extensive coverage on online platforms by some pro-government outlets. Various stakeholders claimed that national consultation campaigns organized by the Government deliberately sought to fuel dissension, division and hate. The Special Rapporteur had been provided with visual evidence of such campaigns to observe the toxic and deliberately provocative style of the messages.75
76. The same Special Rapporteur had received reports from stakeholders that the Government made extensive use of billboards to promote highly politicized campaigns portraying refugees, asylum-seekers, migrants and minorities, including LGBT persons, in a negative light, while positive, non-political messages about those groups were not covered by the pro-government media outlets.76
77. The same Special Rapporteur had also received reports that attempts to advertise humanitarian messages about refugees and asylum-seekers on billboards on World Refugee Day had been thwarted, as billboard companies had refused to rent out the space for fear of adverse reaction from the authorities.77
78. The Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion requested that Hungary confront the discriminatory anti-migrant and racist rhetoric that had marred the political and media space, had put asylum-seekers at risk of refoulement and had created a climate conducive to disinformation campaigns that at times had even been government-sponsored.78
79. UNHCR recommended that Hungary establish a formal, structured mechanism to identify at an early stage the specific needs and vulnerabilities of asylum-seekers, refugees and stateless persons, including survivors of gender-based violence and victims of trafficking, and to refer them to relevant service providers.79
7. Stateless persons
80. UNHCR advised allocating adequate and earmarked State funding for special treatment and assistance for asylum-seekers and stateless persons, including children.80
