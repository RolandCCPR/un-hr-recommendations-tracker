Summary of stakeholders' submissions on the United States of America*
Report of the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights
I. Background
1. The present report was prepared pursuant to Human Rights Council resolutions 5/1 and 16/21, taking into consideration the periodicity of the universal periodic review and the outcome of the previous review.1 It is a summary of 155 stakeholders' submissions2 for the universal periodic review, presented in a summarized manner owing to word-limit constraints.
II. Information provided by stakeholders
A. Scope of international obligations3 and cooperation with human rights mechanisms
2. Several stakeholders informed that the United States of America (USA) had not yet ratified several international human rights treaties, notwithstanding repeated recommendations to that effect.4 USA was recommended to ratify international treaties,5 including the CEDAW;6 CRC;7 ICESCR;8 OP-ICESCR;9 OP-CAT;10 ICPPED;11 the two Optional Protocols to the ICCPR; 12 ICRMW; 13 CRPD; 14 the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court (ICC);15 the American Convention on Human Rights;16 and the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties.17 3. Four submissions recommended ratifying several ILO conventions,18 the Treaty of the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons,19 and the Arms Trade Treaty.20 4. IPLSA recommended the USA to remove its reservations to Article 20 of ICCPR.21 5. Several submissions noted that, in February 2025, the USA decided to withdraw from the WHO22 and the Human Rights Council, review its UNESCO membership, cut UNRWA funding, freeze foreign aid, including termination of grants, and sanction the ICC, in addition to withdraw from the Paris Climate Agreement. 23 AI underlined the global impact on development and humanitarian assistance due to the foreign aid freeze.24 HRC stated that the freeze of federal funding, including foreign aid programmes for AIDS relief, was

B. National human rights framework
7. JS61 and Elizka Relief Foundation (ERF) noted that the USA had not established a National Human Rights Institution (NHRI), resulting in a critical gap in the country's human rights protection framework.30 Recommendations were made to establish an independent NHRI in line with the Paris Principles.31 JS61 further urged state and local governments to step up their efforts to implement human rights protections in their respective jurisdictions.32
Constitutional and legislative framework
8. JS15 informed about the 1798 Alien Enemies Act being used to deport natives, citizens and foreigners without a hearing or standard process, invoking it outside a declaration of a state of war and without identifying a so-called enemy nation.33 JS15 recommended to immediately revoke the ongoing invocation of this Act and repeal its validity.34
C. Promotion and protection of human rights
1. Implementation of international human rights obligations, taking into account applicable international humanitarian law
Equality and non-discrimination
9. HRW stressed that the USA had not implemented UPR supported recommendations to combat systemic racial discrimination, further entrenching discriminatory practices, and noted the absence of atonement for slavery and its legacies.35 PC(USA) stated that racial discrimination remained pervasive,36 recommending to review policies at federal, state and local levels to prevent racism, racial discrimination, xenophobia and related intolerance, and to establish reparation commissions.37
10. IFDAA recommended strengthening anti-discrimination laws.38 JS48 recommended addressing racial or ethnic disparities in all spheres of life.39
11. FOR-USA recommended implementing a nation-wide policy clearly defining and outlawing racial profiling and training law enforcement officials on the multidimensional approach to racial profiling.40
12. FOR-USA highlighted the increase of hate crimes and that hate crime was underreported.41 SPLC noted an increasing acceptance of hate and extremism ideologies, including conspiracies such as "the great replacement" and "immigrant invasion", also espoused by elected officials. 42 FOR-USA recommended establishing an interagency initiative on hate crimes; passing the Improving Reporting to Prevent Hate Act (H.R. 7648); addressing white supremacy in Federal Law Enforcement; increasing funding for the Department of Education's Office for Civil Rights (OCR) and support for the Department of Justice's Civil Rights Division and the Community Relations Service (CRS). 43 JS48 recommended promoting understanding, tolerance and diversity.44
13. PHS pointed at the persistent discrimination against Muslims and stressed the need for cultural and religious education within Government agencies.45
14. HRW regretted the growing movement that prevented educating students about structural racism, including banning books or censoring classroom discussions of race, sexuality and gender. HRW recommended codifying the right to teach accurate history about past and present racial discrimination.46

Right to life, liberty and security of person, and freedom from torture
15. DHF expressed concern about Executive Order 14164 reinstating capital punishment for federal crimes, for which a moratorium had been imposed in 2021, and instructing the Attorney-General to preserve it. DHF called for its recission, and retraction of the Department of Justice related policies. 47ERF recommended the USA to take concrete steps toward abolishing the death penalty at federal and state levels.48
16. JS38 stated that women and gender minorities facing the death penalty experienced dismissal of their experiences surviving violence,49 and that Black people were more likely to be wrongfully convicted and sentenced to death than their white counterparts.50
17. Three submissions condemned the use of nitrogen hypoxia in one State as an execution method.51
18. Three submissions indicated that gun violence was the leading cause of death among children and adolescents, whereas the government had failed to enact evidence-based firearms regulations.52 Two submissions noted that gun violence also disproportionately affected marginalized communities, particularly Black and Latino youths, and LGBTQ+.53 JS26 stressed that insufficient regulation of firearms buyers and sellers, the inadequate regulation of gun manufacturers, and the limited or non-enforcement of inadequate gun control laws rendered the USA firearm regulatory scheme deficient.54
19. FOR-USA informed that the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act (2022) tied mental health services to gun control measures, raising concerns regarding privacy and stigmatization.55 In March 2025, the Gun Violence Federal Health Advisory56 and the White House Office of Gun Violence Prevention were shut down.57
20. FOR-USA recommended banning all forms of "assault-style" weapons and highcapacity magazines; funding early identification and early intervention programs; and providing training for the safe handling of weapons,58 in addition to establishing a Racial Diversity Commission to examine connections between gun violence and policing of communities of colour.59
21. AI raised concerns about the disproportionate impact of police force against Black people and that State and federal laws on the use of force did not comply with international law and standards regarding the use of firearms force.60 JS19 stated that the "objectively reasonable" domestic standard applied to justify the use of force by law enforcement violated the principles of legality, necessity, proportionality and non-discrimination. 61 Abshar reported that police violence, particularly targeting Black individuals and other minorities, frequently went unpunished.62 AI regretted the lack of action by Congress to pass the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act.63
22. USA was recommended to adopt federal, state and local legislation to align the use of force with international standards and to limit the use of force to that which is "necessary and proportional" in compliance with treaty obligations; 64 mandate comprehensive police training on de-escalation and anti-racism;65 and develop consistent and effective police accountability mechanisms.66
23. AI raised concerns about the lack of accountability, redress or adequate medical treatment for detainees subjected to torture and other ill treatment and/or enforced disappearance. It deplored the failure to declassify the full report of the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence on the CIA detention and interrogation programme and relevant information on the CIA programmes of rendition, detention and interrogation between 20012009.67
24. SPLC and JS41 stated that solitary confinement was used as punishment and disproportionately imposed on Black and Latino people.68
International humanitarian law
25. AI indicated that USA had continued to use lethal force worldwide and had withheld information regarding the legal and policy standards and criteria applied by armed forces on the use of lethal force.69 JS67 recommended enacting mandatory human rights due diligence law and regulations for the arms sector.70

26. AI referred to the use of USA-made bombs and components in several air strikes carried out by a foreign military force on residential homes and makeshift camps for displaced people71 JS67 and JS29 highlighted the link between USA's role as primary arms supplier to a third country with ongoing allegations of war crimes and human rights violations.72 AI stated that the arms sale and transfer contradicted domestic laws and policies governing arms exports, in terms of preventing civilian harm and violations of international human rights or humanitarian law.73 USA was recommended to withhold military aid from states in violation of international humanitarian and human rights law and to apply the Leahy laws without exceptions;74 and cease the transfer of arms to states where they might be used to violate international human rights and humanitarian law.75
27. AI noted the transfer of non-persistent antipersonnel mines and cluster munitions to a third country in 2024, in violation of the 2022 US Anti-Personnel Landmine policy, the Mine Ban Treaty and the Convention on Cluster Munitions.76
Human rights and counter-terrorism
28. AI reported that 15 men were still arbitrarily and indefinitely detained at Guantanamo Bay, following the transfer of 25 detainees since the previous review.77 JS15 recommended the USA to end military detention at Guantanamo.78
29. Considering the absence of a singular definition of terrorism in USA law, JS15 stressed that the methods deployed in the name of "counter-terrorism" and "national security" evolved from emergency measures to common practices.79 JS15 recommended ceasing the weaponization of the terrorism framework.80
30. IPLSA stressed that the USA had frequently invoked emergency powers under the National Emergencies Act and the International Emergency Economic Powers Act to impose sanctions on foreign nations, but their prolonged and unchecked application raised concerns regarding compliance with international law and human rights standards,81 particularly the overly broad definition of a "threat", 82 extension beyond their original parameters, and frequent annual renewal without reassessing the geopolitical contexts, thereby creating de facto perpetual states of emergency in those countries.83 IPLSA recommended to establish judicial mechanisms to scrutinise sanctions' designations, ensuring accountability and transparency; 84 implement mandatory expiration of emergency declarations unless reauthorised by Congress;85 ensure timely notification to the UN of any derogations from the ICCPR;86 and apply ICCPR protections to individuals outside USA territory.87
31. ADVT recommended the USA to immediately lift all unilateral sanctions that negatively affect civilian populations, establish an independent body to assess the humanitarian impact of its sanctions and provide effective remedies to affected individuals.88
Administration of justice, including impunity, and the rule of law
32. BAC and H.I noted high levels of incarceration, stringent policies and substantial economic and social costs in the USA criminal system.89 Despite supporting previous UPR recommendations to reform the criminal legal system, HRW regretted the increased punitiveness, marked by racial disproportionality among those incarcerated, who were subjected to police violence and criminalised for offences linked to poverty.90C.I.L indicated that African Americans and Latinos were disproportionally subjected to unlawful detention and torture.91 JS2 indicated that women's incarceration had gone up twice the pace of men and that Black and Latina women were disproportionately imprisoned.92
33. BWA highlighted the rise in sentences of life without parole (LWOP, LWP).93 JS44 considered that the life without parole sentences ("death by incarceration") was a consequence of a cruel and racially discriminatory criminal system, that disproportionally impacted on Black people and communities.94 Recommendations were made to repeal all laws permitting or mandating LWOP, LWP and other terms of years sentences that exceed life expectancy; 95 and reform the discriminatory criminal justice policies and adopt alternative sentencing.96

34. PHS recommended reforming the judicial system, enhancing an independent oversight of the government and the judicial institutions, and establishing restorative justice for victims.97
35. JS5 informed that Executive Order 14148 reversed policies linked to Executive Order 14074 on advancing effective, accountable policing and criminal justice practices.98
Fundamental freedoms and the right to participate in public and political life
36. Several submissions observed a decline in freedom of opinion and expression, including unjustified restrictions on media content, narrowing digital spaces, and threats against journalists and whistleblowers. They stated that USA tech companies collaborated with federal entities to monitor content opposing the USA administration, and regarding the Palestinian issue.99 They added that the 1917 Espionage Act was used to silence critics.100 Abshar and IFWA recommended reforming restrictive laws to protect whistleblowers and journalists101 and ending violence and arbitrary detention of journalists.102
37. AI reported on the introduction of bills restricting the right to protest, criminalising specific forms of protest, and the use of vague and broad laws to suppress protest movements.103 JS60 stressed that new state laws criminalised protests and demonstrations near pipelines, chemical plants and other infrastructure.104 FOR-USA denounced Executive Order 14188 mandating the deportation of students who violated any law during protests, regardless of the severity of the offense.105
38. Several submissions reported that the government and university administrations had imposed increasingly repressive measures on students and staff advocating for Palestinian human rights.106 JS74 described the clampdown on campus protests and arrests of students, faculty and community supporters, indicating a pattern of censorship.107
39. FOR-USA denounced that, since March 2025, various visas were revoked for participation in pro-Palestinian demonstrations, using immigration law as a tool of political suppression.108 JS74 referred to arrests of visa holding students and threats to deport them on the grounds of supporting terrorism, without evidence or due process.109
40. USA was recommended to cease arresting and detaining individuals for exercising their freedom of expression in line with the First Amendment110 and release individuals arrested and/or detained for political beliefs and expression;111 ensure that restrictions to the rights to opinion, expression, association and assembly comply with international standards; 112 strengthen the right to protest and the right to public and political participation;113cease actions that suppress or criminalise peaceful protest through legislation, surveillance, institutional pressure or other measures;114 end the use of visa revocation and immigration detention and deportation against those engaged in lawful protest or political expression; 115 and reinstate expelled students and fair hearings for students accused of violating school policies.116
41. SAR observed a decline in academic freedom, including legal restrictions on curricula, interference by non-academic actors and suppression of on-campus discourse and protest by both education administrators and the government.117
42. JS15 recommended the USA to repeal executive orders and directives that reduce and/or eliminate funding for civil society organizations deemed not aligned with the administration.118
43. According to OSCE-ODIHR, certain segments of the USA citizenry remained disenfranchised, and voter registration and identification were still politically charged issues.119 SPLC stressed that people of African-descent faced barriers to participation in representative democracy and that States had enacted new laws restricting voting access.120 OSCE-ODIHR recommended adopting a new formula for determining jurisdictions required to undergo federal pre-clearance for changes to elections laws, in line with the Voting Rights Act, to ensure effective safeguards against legal changes that may have discriminatory intent or impact against racial and linguistic minorities.121

44. UNPO recommended that all citizens and nations have the right to vote and be represented at all levels of federal government, regardless of national origin, birth or place of residence.122
45. ECLJ expressed concern at rising hostile incidents against churches and discrimination against Christians.123
46. Connection e.V. recommended to legally recognise the right to conscientious objection according to international human rights law.124
Right to privacy
47. FDA noted that the lack of a comprehensive policy on privacy protection allowed some companies and organizations to exploit data and sell them without user's knowledge or consent and recommended the USA to establish a comprehensive federal data protection framework and an independent oversight body, prohibit data sales without explicit consent and impose severe penalties on violating companies, and enhance transparency in corporate practices.125
48. Concerning the Government's intrusive and disproportionate surveillance with no or insufficient transparency and oversight, JS15 recommended repealing any law that is incompatible with article 17 of ICCPR and article 12 of UDHR and establishing federal-level privacy protection laws that comply with ICCPR and UDHR.126
Prohibition of all forms of slavery, including trafficking in persons
49. IAJW stated that human trafficking was a challenge in the USA, compounded by a lack of appropriate support and fear of reporting to authorities.127 HPG alerted about Artificial Intelligence being weaponized by traffickers to exploit children and vulnerable populations in sophisticated ways.128 JS57 indicated that noncitizen children faced a heightened risk of labour trafficking and exploitation.129 JS22 reported failures in identifying and supporting victims and securing criminal convictions.130 IAJW recommended harmonizing the national legislation with the Palermo Protocol, providing comprehensive support to victims, and establishing specialized courts.131
Right to work and to just and favourable conditions of work
50. ERF observed a worsening of the right to work, including reduction and terminations of federal employees and precarious employment of migrants. 132 Several stakeholders referred to discriminatory practices against racial minorities in the labour market, 133 persistent exploitation through forced labour in prisons, domestic work and agriculture;134 and racial disparities with regards forced labour in prisons.135 Recommendations were made to improve working conditions, ensure fair wages,136 increase the minimum wage,137 halt the dismissal of federal employees,138 and end all forms of forced or coerced labour in prisons, jails and other places of detention.139
51. Considering the high gender pay gap,140 Abshar and IESW recommended enacting federal legislation enforcing equal pay for equal work,141 and creating a federal oversight mechanism to enforce gender pay equity laws.142
Right to social security
52. Considering the inequities in the tax system and the weak enforcement of corporate and individual tax evasion, which deprived governments of revenues needed for social security, 143 JS59 recommended reforming the regressive tax policy and eliminating tax evasion and abuses by wealthy taxpayers and corporate nonprofit entities.144

Several submissions referred to the significant economic inequality in the USA,152 the stark racial wealth gap153 and racialized poverty in the country.154 JS63 stressed the lack of measures to address structural and systemic factors that reproduced poverty and discrimination linked to the legacy of colonisation, slavery of people of African-descent, and the exploitation and dispossession of Indigenous Peoples' territories. 155 USA was recommended to reduce the high levels of poverty and inequality,156 address the root causes of poverty 157 and racial disparities in access to adequate health, nutrition, education, employment and housing.158

Right to health
57. Several submissions stressed that the 2022 overturning of Roe v. Wade had eliminated the federal constitutional right to abortion and returned authority over abortion policy to individual states, leading to the enactment of numerous bans and restrictions,167 and having a harmful impact on pregnant women's and girls' lives.168 JS3 informed that none of the supported recommendations on sexual health and reproductive rights had been implemented.169 AI recommended to fully decriminalise abortion and remove abortion from criminal laws and other punitive laws, policies and practices.170
58. JS39 highlighted that the existing maternal health crisis in the USA was rooted in structural racism.171 Recommendations were made to address the high rates of maternal deaths and morbidities, and ensure access to maternal health care of quality across the territory, without discrimination;172 increase funding for family planning services and access to contraceptive methods and maternal health programmes;173 expand access to prenatal and postnatal healthcare services, reduce disparities in infant mortality rates among racial and ethnic groups;174 and mandate comprehensive sexual and reproductive health education.175
59. ASCP expressed concern at increasing cases of mental illnesses in the USA.176 JS73 stressed the impact on black and brown communities.177 JS9 and HRW informed reported cancellation of grants for substance use and mental health programmes178 and the expansive imposition of coercive and involuntary mental health treatments.179
60. HRW stressed that systemic racism and the cost barriers of the predominantly forprofit health care system played a huge part in health inequities.180 Recommendations were made that the USA adopt a universal healthcare system, subsidise medical costs and expand Medicaid nationwide,181 and ensure equitable access to healthcare for homeless, uninsured and marginalized populations.182
61. Concerned about legislation criminalizing unlicensed midwifery in Hawaii, JS68 recommended enacting laws and policies that protect reproductive choice, including in traditional birthing choice, and enacting comprehensive legislation prohibiting discrimination in healthcare, including traditional cultural midwifery.183

Cultural rights
65. Concerned about "book bans" targeting publications that feature stories about people of colour and LGBT people, destruction of artworks, and defunding of programming commemorating anti-racism struggles and LGBT legacies,191 SRI recommended removing censoring and ensuring the rights to information and to benefit from scientific progress.192

A.F.I. highlighted the decline in protections for artistic freedom, cultural rights and press freedom.193 Executive actions in 2025 had suppressed artistic freedom, barred minority groups from participating in cultural life, and redirected arts funding toward works that aligned with the administration's political platforms.194
Development, the environment, and business and human rights
67. GAF regretted that several 2025 Executive Orders revisited some measures to reduce carbon dioxide emissions, increased the leasing of public lands to oil and gas development, and froze processes to promote alternative sources of energy.195 JS3 noted the elimination of 70 climate change initiatives and the abolition of federal targets to reduce emissions and decarbonise by 2035.196 USA was recommended to implement moratoria on new or expanded fossil fuel operations;197 shift towards renewable energy sources;198 and resume efforts to combat climate change.199
68. HRW underlined the disproportionate exposure of racial and ethnic minorities to environmental hazards.200 JS3 noted that communities from low-income, minority, tribal and indigenous backgrounds faced negative health impacts from environmental hazards, underinvestment and pollution.201 JAI reminded the USA to implement climate adaptation and mitigation strategies aimed at safeguarding access of Indigenous communities to their lands, territories and resources.202
69. JS65 requested the USA to require businesses to conduct human rights due diligence in conflict-affected contexts and develop policies to protect human rights defenders.203
2. Rights of specific persons or groups
Women
70. UWW noted the prevalence of domestic violence and that the existing decentralized legal system to combat it created inconsistencies, leaving many survivors vulnerable.204 GFG reported funding cuts to the Violence Against Women Act and domestic violence programmes, and recommended prioritising long-term and sustainable funding for domestic violence services and programmes and to focus on public awareness campaigns.205
71. Moheb noted that sexual violence remained an issue within the USA military.206
72. F.F.A. recommended the USA to consolidate existing laws into a unified National Sexual Violence Protection Act.207
73. According to AI, American Indian and Alaska Native women disproportionately suffered high levels of sexual violence and faced barriers to accessing post-rape care, including forensic examinations for criminal cases.208 CS linked this violence with extractive industries projects near or in Indigenous Communities.209 JS25 and UWW recommended implementing prevention measures to address gender-based violence associated with workers of extractive projects,210 and the Violence Against Women's Act tribal provisions.211

Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender and intersex persons
89. According to HRW, the USA had enacted policies that reinforced discrimination against LGBT people.237 JS24 and A.F.I. enumerated several recently adopted Executive Orders.238 Some of them contradicted the Supreme Court ruling Bostock v. Clayton County, asserting that the federal employment law's prohibition of sex-based discrimination included prohibitions of sexual orientation and gender identity discrimination.239 Anti-LGBTQ+ bills and executive actions increased harassment, discrimination, marginalization and hate crimes against LGBTQ+.240 AI and JS46 reported violence against LGBTI individuals, especially racialised transgender people,241 and systemic violations, pervasive police harassment of and violence against trans people and sex workers.242USA was urged to immediately repeal the Executive Orders243 and anti-trans laws;244 enact comprehensive state and federal legislation prohibiting discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity;245 and codify same-sex marriage and LGBTIQ+ protections through Congress.246
Migrants, refugees and asylum-seekers
90. PHR expressed concerns about the introduction of Executive actions eroding access to asylum and expanding the infrastructure of expedited removal.247 JS47 noted that at the US-Mexico border, asylum had been all but eliminated; migration enforcement escalated focusing on mass deportations and authorizing expulsions248 and the Refugee Resettlement program was suspended.249 SBW observed systemic barriers to legal representation.250 JS49 reported that through negotiated agreements with third countries, hundreds of asylum seekers and other migrants were transferred, facing human rights abuses.251
91. Several stakeholders reported the use of military force at the border and the application of the 1798 Alien Enemies Act for summary removals;252 abuses and killings by U.S. borders agents and lack of accountability;253 widespread arrests and raids;254 prolonged detention;255 expedited removal;256 mass and unjust deportations;257 separation of children from their families;258 denial of legal protections, including legal counsel;259 pushbacks;260 asylum bans;261 and third-country expulsions.262
92. JS12 deplored the suspension of asylum processing and other legal protections at the southern borders and recommended toreinstate timely access to territory and rescind unlawful policies.263 JS49 recommended to ensure due process in immigration adjudications.264 JS12 urged to end border externalization policies that forced migrants to seek asylum and/or remain in Mexico.265
93. JS7 stated that political rhetoric portraying migrants, refugees and asylum seekers as threats fueled xenophobia.266
94. Raha reported migrants' barriers to accessing and affording health care 267 IFDAA underscored that recent rescission of policies restricting Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) arrests in sensitive locations had created an environment of fear, affecting access to medical care. 268 JS62 considered crucial to hold ICE to a higher standard of oversight and accountability.269
95. Several submissions reported deplorable detention conditions of migrants, 270 including overcrowding; 271 solitary confinement; 272 poor sanitary conditions; 273 sexual assault274 and torture;275 insufficient or refusal of medical care276 and deaths resulting from immigration detention conditions.277
96. AI reported that Guantanamo detention facility was used as a migratory detention center, raising concerns over conditions and lawfulness of these detentions.278
97. JS49 recommended ending military involvement in deportations and third-country transfers.279
Stateless persons

JS55 reported that a change in the birthright citizenship law threatened to refuse citizenship to people born in the USA unless one or both parents was lawfully present or a citizen. 280 Some stakeholders noted attempts to end birthright citizenship, 281 leading to statelessness for children born to parents with temporary or undocumented status.282
